## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇香港大学、浙江大学、复旦大学等单位的论文，名字叫 HiSTEVE 1.0。

它想做的是，让 AI 像人类玩家一样玩 Minecraft，只看屏幕，只用键盘鼠标，自己规划，完成很长的任务。

### 问题

这需要两种能力。一是长程自主，一直记着目标，随时调整计划。二是可靠的原生界面控制，把第一人称画面变成键盘鼠标操作。

规划这一层有个难点，就是什么时候算一个子任务做完了。停早了，活没干完，停晚了，浪费动作，甚至把做好的东西弄坏。

控制这一层也有麻烦。Minecraft 里，在世界里走动、挖矿、打怪，论文叫非 GUI 操作，打开合成、熔炼界面摆物品，叫 GUI 操作。同样是移动鼠标，前者转视角，后者移光标，直接混在一起训练，容易互相拖后腿。

而且长任务要反复调用底层模型，每次一点点失败的风险，累积起来就很可观。

### 以前的方法

以前的方法大致只顾一头。一类是 VLA，也就是视觉、语言、动作模型，直接把画面映射成键盘鼠标操作，但通常没有机制跟踪任务进度，长任务撑不住。

另一类用大语言模型或者视觉语言模型做规划，擅长拆任务，但底层执行常常依赖环境 API 或者预先写好的技能，大多数游戏根本不提供这些接口。

### 核心想法

HiSTEVE 的思路是分层。上层是视觉语言模型做规划，下层是一个 VLA 负责执行，中间用一层 harness 串起来。Harness 可以理解成 Agent 的调度框架，负责记状态、派任务、验收结果。

这里有两个关键点。第一是能力感知，规划器只能从 VLA 做得到的子任务里挑。第二是交接和验收分开，检测器觉得可能做完了，VLA 就交回控制权，算不算完成，由 harness 看画面再确认。

下层从头到尾只有一个 VLA，所有子任务都靠它，只看画面，只用键盘鼠标。

### 方法

先说规划。没有预设的子任务顺序，规划器每一步根据最终目标和当前情况，选下一个原子子任务。

每种子任务有一个模板，写清楚什么时候适用、要哪些参数、画面上怎么看完成、什么算失败、最多给多少执行预算。规划器给出语义目标，harness 再把它转成一句固定指令交给 VLA。

交接怎么做。VLA 执行时，有一个任务完成状态检测器，不断估计子任务完成的概率。超过阈值，或者预算用完，控制权就交回 harness。

Harness 再看最近的画面，把结果记成成功、失败或者被中断。成功就往下走，还能救就就地恢复，救不了或者反复失败，就重新规划。

比如挖到资源，要在物品栏里看到数量变化才算数，合成出来的东西，也得真的出现在物品栏里。

记忆分四种，工作记忆记目标、进度和最近的失败，情节记忆记每次调用 VLA 前后的状态，程序记忆记成功经验里的子任务依赖，世界记忆记地标和空间关系。整个记忆围绕经过验证的状态变化来组织。

再说下层的 VLA。它从 Qwen2.5-VL-7B 初始化，输入子任务指令和当前画面，输出表示键盘鼠标操作的 token，每秒 20 次。

针对 GUI 和非 GUI 的冲突，作者做了梯度分析，发现两类数据的梯度在两个 RMSNorm 模块上最不一致。RMSNorm 是每层里做归一化的小模块，可学习的只有一组缩放系数。

于是他们提出 RS-Norm，每个 RMSNorm 准备两套缩放系数，非 GUI 一套，GUI 一套，按子任务类型切换，其他参数全部共享，多出来的参数很少。

第二个设计叫 Dual-Mouse，鼠标移动的 token 分成两套，GUI 单独用一套，序列开头再加一个标记说明当前是哪种操作。两套 token 解码成同样的物理动作，区别只在模型内部。

训练分三步。先注入世界知识，用 Minecraft 的问答、截图描述这类数据，让模型认识游戏里的东西。再做模仿学习，数据里既有人类玩家的轨迹，也有脚本生成的轨迹。

第三步是强化学习，因为模仿学习之后，GUI 操作还是不够稳。做法是从同一个起点跑多条轨迹，用环境反馈打分，比如物品栏里是不是真的多了目标物品，再在组内做相对比较。

最后是完成检测器。它的主干是 Qwen2.5-VL-3B，和 VLA 共用图像编码器，看之前 800 到 200 毫秒的四帧画面，同时通过交叉注意力读入 VLA 内部的状态，所以叫和策略耦合。

它的前馈网络剪到大约三分之一宽，表 2 里参数量是 1.4B。部署时，要连续两次判断完成，才发出信号。

### 实验和效果

实验里，上层规划器用 GPT-4o，下层 VLA 是 Qwen2.5-VL-7B。原子任务在 MineStudio Bench 和 OpenHA 上测，长任务在 LongBench 上测。

MineStudio 有 20 个任务，每个跑三次。LongBench 有 14 个只给最终目标的长任务，另有 49 个隐藏的诊断节点，只用来事后分析进度。

结果在表 1。MineStudio 上，HiSTEVE 平均 85.0%，对比里最强的基线 OpenHA Text/CoA 是 65.0%，高了 20 个百分点。挖矿、打怪、合成、熔炼，分别是 93.3、86.7、66.7 和 93.3。

合成提升最多，高了 26.7 个点，但仍是四类里最弱的，作者认为多步 GUI 操作还是更难。

OpenHA 上，HiSTEVE 平均 37.4%，基线 36.8%，只高 0.6 个点。提升集中在具身类任务，从 37.0 到 46.0，GUI 和战斗两类反而略低于基线。

长任务上差距更大。资源和合成类的最终成功率是 42.9%，探索和记忆类 28.6%，恢复和开放式都是 14.3%，比这个基线高 11.4 到 28.6 个百分点。

恢复和开放式任务还是很低，作者也承认这仍是难题。图 3 右边是一条长任务，从砍树开始，做木镐、挖石头、做石剑、挖铁矿、炼铁锭，最后做出铁剑。

完成检测器在 4674 个类别平衡的样本上单独测试，结果在表 2，准确率和 F1 都是 0.863。

对比的通用模型只是用提示词来判断。Qwen3.5 的 9B 和 35B 几乎总说没完成，召回率只有 0.030 和 0.042，GPT-4o 的 F1 是 0.690，GPT-5.5 是 0.776。

### 和以前方法的对比

这篇最值得看的是表 3 的消融，它把底层执行和上层调度分开测了。

先看表示。完全共享参数时，MineStudio 是 41.1%，单加 RS-Norm 到 57.3%，单加 Dual-Mouse 到 61.9%，两个一起到 80.7%。

再看训练。注入世界知识，MineStudio 从 50.4% 涨到 65.1%，强化学习再推到 80.7%，但长任务成功率停在 14.3%，没有变化。作者的结论是，单靠把执行器练强，解决不了长程协调。

最关键的一组，是 VLA 不变，只换上层。对照组是执行固定步数就交回控制权。两者在原子任务上差别很小，MineStudio 是 81.2% 对 85.0%，OpenHA 是 37.8% 对 37.4%。但长任务成功率从 10.7% 提到 25.0%，翻了一倍多。

我的判断是，这是全文最有说服力的结果。长任务失败，很多时候不是手不够巧，而是没人记账、没人验收。

我的判断是，短板也要看清。OpenHA 上 0.6 个点的领先，附录给的标准差是 1.8 和 2.0，在波动范围之内。LongBench 只有 14 个任务，每类标准差大约 5 到 14 个点，单个数字不宜看得太精确。

### 局限

作者写了两条局限。第一，子任务接口是人工定义的，能力受现有 VLA 限制，加新能力就要补数据、重新训练，harness 和 VLA 目前也不会用部署经验更新参数。

第二，高层规划依赖现成的视觉语言模型，这里是 GPT-4o，系统表现会受它的能力影响。

另外，GUI 多步操作仍是瓶颈。作者分析失败案例时发现，GUI 任务常常能打开正确的界面，却在多步摆放物品、修正中间状态或者取出产物时出错。

### 未来方向

作者提的方向是，让 VLA 和 harness 各自或者一起进化，VLA 从失败和恢复的轨迹里学习，harness 根据经验更新规划约束和恢复策略。

另一个方向是把 Minecraft 当成训练 Agent 的环境，看规划、记忆和恢复能力，能不能迁移到别的游戏，甚至游戏以外的场景。

我的判断是，把交接和验收分开、让规划器只在执行器的能力范围内挑任务，这些做法对电脑操作 Agent 也有参考价值。不过这只是推断，论文只在 Minecraft 里做了验证。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the University of Hong Kong, Zhejiang University, Fudan University and others, and it's called HiSTEVE one point zero.

The goal is an AI that plays Minecraft the way a person does: it only sees the screen, only uses a keyboard and mouse, and plans its own way through long tasks.

### Problem

That takes two abilities. One is long-horizon autonomy: keep the goal in mind and adjust the plan as things change. The other is reliable native-interface control: turn a first-person view into keyboard and mouse input.

On the planning side, a hard question is when a subtask is actually done. Stop too early and the job is unfinished. Stop too late and you waste actions, or even undo progress.

On the control side, Minecraft has two kinds of interaction. Moving through the world, mining and fighting is what the paper calls non-GUI. Working inside crafting and smelting screens is GUI. The same mouse movement turns the camera in one and moves a cursor in the other, so naively training on both lets each hurt the other.

And a long task calls the low-level model many times, so even a small failure rate per call adds up.

### Prior work

Earlier approaches mostly handled one side. VLA models, meaning vision-language-action models, map the screen straight to keyboard and mouse actions, but they usually can't track task progress, so long tasks fall apart.

Others use language or vision-language models to plan, which is good at breaking goals down, but they often execute through environment APIs or predefined skills, and most games don't offer those.

### Key idea

HiSTEVE splits the job into layers. A vision-language model plans on top, a single VLA executes at the bottom, and a harness sits in between. Think of the harness as the agent's orchestration layer: it keeps state, hands out tasks, and checks results.

Two ideas matter. First, the planner is capability-aware: it can only pick subtasks the VLA can actually do. Second, handoff and verification are separate: the VLA hands back control when a detector thinks the subtask might be done, and the harness checks the screen to decide whether it really is.

At the bottom, that one VLA handles every subtask, using only the screen and the keyboard and mouse.

### Method

Start with planning. There's no preset sequence of subtasks; at each step the planner picks the next atomic subtask from the final goal and the current situation.

Each subtask type has a schema: when it applies, what parameters it takes, what completion looks like on screen, what counts as failure, and how much execution budget it gets. The planner outputs a semantic goal, and the harness turns it into a fixed instruction for the VLA.

The handoff works like this. While the VLA runs, a task completion state detector keeps estimating the chance the subtask is done. When that crosses a threshold, or the budget runs out, control returns to the harness.

The harness checks recent frames and records the attempt as a success, a failure, or interrupted. On success it moves on. If the subtask is salvageable it recovers locally, and if not, or if recovery keeps failing, it replans.

For example, gathering a resource only counts once the inventory count visibly changes, and a crafted item has to actually show up in the inventory.

Memory comes in four kinds: working memory for the goal, progress and recent failures; episodic memory for the state before and after each VLA call; procedural memory for subtask dependencies learned from successes; and world memory for landmarks and spatial relations. All of it is built around verified state changes.

Now the VLA. It's initialized from Qwen two point five VL seven B, takes the instruction and the current frame, and outputs keyboard and mouse action tokens twenty times a second.

For the GUI versus non-GUI conflict, a gradient analysis showed the two kinds of data disagree most in the two RMSNorm modules. RMSNorm is the small normalization step in each layer, and its only learnable parameters are scale factors.

So they introduce RS-Norm: each RMSNorm gets two sets of scale factors, one for non-GUI and one for GUI, switched by subtask type, with everything else shared and very few extra parameters.

The second design, Dual-Mouse, splits mouse movement tokens into two sets, gives GUI its own, and adds a token at the start saying which mode is active. Both sets decode to the same physical movements, so the split lives only inside the model.

Training has three stages. First, world knowledge injection, using Minecraft questions, screenshot captions and similar data, so the model learns what's in the game. Second, imitation learning on trajectories that include human gameplay and scripted runs.

Third comes reinforcement learning, because after imitation the model was still shaky on GUI tasks. They run several trajectories from the same start, score them with environment feedback, like whether the target item really landed in the inventory, and compare them within the group.

Last, the completion detector. Its backbone is Qwen two point five VL three B. It shares the VLA's image encoder, looks at four frames from eight hundred down to two hundred milliseconds back, and reads the VLA's internal state through cross-attention, which is why it's called policy-coupled.

Its feed-forward layers are pruned to about a third of their width, and Table two lists it at one point four billion parameters. At deployment it must say done twice in a row before it signals.

### Results

In the experiments, GPT-4o plans and Qwen two point five VL seven B is the VLA. Atomic tasks are tested on MineStudio Bench and OpenHA, long tasks on LongBench.

MineStudio has twenty tasks, each run three times. LongBench has fourteen long tasks that give only the final goal, plus forty-nine hidden checkpoints used only to measure progress afterward.

Table one has the results. On MineStudio, HiSTEVE averages eighty-five percent, against sixty-five for the strongest baseline, OpenHA Text slash CoA, a twenty point gap. Mining, killing, crafting and smelting score ninety-three point three, eighty-six point seven, sixty-six point seven and ninety-three point three.

Crafting gains the most, twenty-six point seven points, but it's still the weakest category, which the authors put down to multi-step GUI work being harder.

On OpenHA, HiSTEVE averages thirty-seven point four percent against thirty-six point eight, just zero point six higher. The gain is in embodied tasks, from thirty-seven to forty-six, while GUI and combat land slightly below the baseline.

Long tasks show a bigger gap. Final-goal success is forty-two point nine percent on resource and crafting, twenty-eight point six on exploration and memory, and fourteen point three on both recovery and open-ended tasks, between eleven point four and twenty-eight point six points above that baseline.

Recovery and open-ended tasks are still low, and the authors say these remain hard. Figure three, on the right, shows one long run: chop a tree, craft a wooden pickaxe, mine stone, make a stone sword, mine iron ore, smelt an ingot, and finally craft an iron sword.

The completion detector was tested on its own on four thousand six hundred seventy-four class-balanced samples, in Table two, and scores zero point eight six three on both accuracy and F1.

The general models it's compared with were just prompted. Qwen three point five at nine B and thirty-five B almost always say not done, with recall of only zero point zero three and zero point zero four two, while GPT-4o reaches an F1 of zero point six nine and GPT-5.5 zero point seven seven six.

### Comparison

The most useful part is the ablation in Table three, because it measures low-level execution and high-level orchestration separately.

First, the representation. With fully shared parameters, MineStudio is forty-one point one percent. RS-Norm alone lifts it to fifty-seven point three, Dual-Mouse alone to sixty-one point nine, and both together to eighty point seven.

Next, training. World knowledge takes MineStudio from fifty point four to sixty-five point one, and reinforcement learning pushes it to eighty point seven, but long-task success stays flat at fourteen point three. The authors conclude that a stronger executor alone doesn't solve long-horizon coordination.

The key test keeps the VLA fixed and changes only the layer above. The control hands back control after a fixed number of steps. On atomic tasks the two are close: eighty-one point two versus eighty-five on MineStudio, thirty-seven point eight versus thirty-seven point four on OpenHA. But long-task success goes from ten point seven to twenty-five percent, more than double.

My read is that this is the most convincing result in the paper. When long tasks fail, the problem often isn't clumsy hands; it's that nobody is keeping the books or checking the work.

My read is also that the weak spots deserve a clear look. The zero point six lead on OpenHA sits inside the standard deviations in the appendix, one point eight and two point zero. LongBench has only fourteen tasks, with per-family standard deviations of roughly five to fourteen points, so no single number should be read too precisely.

### Limitations

The authors list two limitations. First, the subtask interfaces are hand-defined and capped by the current VLA, so a new skill needs new data and retraining, and neither the harness nor the VLA currently updates its parameters from deployment experience.

Second, planning relies on an off-the-shelf vision-language model, here GPT-4o, so the system depends on how good that model is.

Multi-step GUI work is also still the bottleneck. In their failure analysis, GUI episodes often reach the right screen, then go wrong placing items over several steps, correcting an intermediate state, or retrieving the result.

### Future work

The authors suggest letting the VLA and the harness evolve, separately or together, with the VLA learning from failure and recovery trajectories and the harness updating its planning constraints and recovery strategies.

Another direction is to use Minecraft as a training ground and test whether planning, memory and recovery skills transfer to other games, and to settings beyond games.

My read is that separating handoff from verification, and keeping the planner within what the executor can do, are ideas worth borrowing for computer-use agents. That's my inference, though; the paper only tests them in Minecraft.
