## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇字节跳动牵头的论文，名字叫 Axion 1.0，副标题是，开放的 Agent 基础模型，简化版。

它问的问题很直接，训练一个强的 Agent 模型，现在那一长串训练流程，到底有多少是必要的。

### 问题

先说背景。现在的大模型越来越像 Agent，会搜索，会跑代码，会调用工具，一个任务要走很多步。

训练这样的模型，流程也越来越长，有 mid-training，有好几轮监督微调，有偏好优化，还有强化学习。作者说，每一步单看都有道理，但堆在一起，就很难说清哪一步真正必要，复现成本也高。

还有一个问题。很多所谓的开放模型，只放了权重，数据怎么造，怎么过滤，怎么验证，都没有公开。

### 以前的方法

论文举了几个前作。MiroThinker 1.7 同时用了 mid-training、监督微调、偏好优化和强化学习，Agents-A1 用了按领域路由的在线蒸馏，Apodex 1.1 研究可执行环境和多个 Agent 的协作。

造数据这块也有前作。比如 Evol-Instruct 通过反复改写把题目变难，TaskCraft 扩展能验证的多工具任务，WebShaper 给信息检索任务的复杂度搭结构。

### 核心想法

Axion 的核心想法是，训练流程可以简化，但学习的问题不简化，把力气花在造又难、又能自动验证的数据上。

整个训练只有三步，两轮轻量的监督微调，再加一轮强化学习，没有专门的 mid-training，没有 DPO，也没有在线蒸馏。第一轮练不靠外部信息的推理，第二轮练用工具查资料再推理，强化学习再把长流程的交互练扎实。

而且它说的开放，不只是权重，造数据、过滤、验证、prompt，还有训练代码，全部公开。

### 方法

先看第一轮的数据，叫自包含推理题，意思是题目本身给了全部信息。作者收集公开的数学、物理、化学、生物和计算机题目，但不直接拿来训练，而是当作种子，去造更难的新题。

第一步叫组合。生成模型找到原题解答里算出的某个量，围绕它出一道新题，新题必须用这个量当中间结果，答案要能核对，也不能泄露原题。

关键在下一步。把新题交给基础模型去做，只开 Python，不让搜索。做错了，直接进题库。做对了，就把它的解法交给生成模型，从四种加难操作里随机选一种，专门针对它会的那条路去加难。

这张图里有个玩具例子。基础模型会算 1 加到 10 等于 55。组合，是后面再加一步。参数化，是把 10 换成 n，要一个通式。范围平移，是换成大到没法逐项相加的数。分情况，是改成正负交替，答案按 n 的奇偶分开。

答案怎么保证对。每道题都让 GPT-5.5 独立做三遍，三遍一致、推导也通过检查，才接受参考答案。然后换一个模型解题，只保留答对、格式完整的轨迹。图里标的是，这一轮有 1.08 万条训练轨迹。

第二轮是需要工具的题。作者收集生物、化学、数学、地理、航空等领域的公开结构化数据，用确定性的程序出题和算答案，分四步，筛选，选定，计算，最后让大模型改写成自然语言。

论文的例子是，先筛出有环的行星，再选质量最小的那颗，也就是天王星，然后问它的平均密度，大约每立方厘米 1.27 克。模型得先认出天王星，再查质量和半径，最后自己算。

为了逼模型真的用工具，解题时屏蔽出题用的数据表，只给通用搜索、打开网页和 Python，不用工具就能答对的题也删掉。最后保留答对的轨迹，这一轮是 7500 条。

训练时，第二轮微调从第一轮的结果接着训。只对模型自己生成的部分算 loss，也就是推理、工具调用和最终答案，工具返回的内容只当上下文。

第三步是强化学习，用的是 GRPO，就是同一道题采样多条轨迹，按组内的相对好坏来更新。奖励是这样的，答案对了，得 1 分。答错了，中间步骤做对可以拿部分分，最多 0.3 分，如果最后没交答案，再扣 0.1 分，最低是 0 分。

中间步骤怎么判。出题的程序本来就记下了每一步的中间结果，比如选中的是哪颗行星。这些结果按依赖顺序检查，前面都对了，后面的步骤才算分。

选题也有讲究。每道题先让当前模型做四遍，至少一遍答对，而且四遍得分不完全一样，才留下来训练。

工程上还有一点。Axion 是混合专家模型，也就是 MoE，推理引擎和训练引擎算同一个 token，可能选到不同的专家。他们用一个叫 R3 的方法，把推理时选的专家记下来，训练时照着用。

### 实验和效果

实验设置。两个主力模型都是混合专家结构，35B 的从 Qwen3.6 初始化，122B 的从 Qwen3.5 初始化。评测时可以用网页搜索、打开网页、查学术文献和 Python。

监督微调的成本不高。122B 模型在 32 张 GPU 上两轮一共 7 个半小时，大约 240 个 GPU 小时，35B 大约 90 个 GPU 小时。强化学习的总算力，论文没有给。

主要结果在表 1。先看 Humanity's Last Exam，简称 HLE，是跨学科的专家级难题。在屏蔽 Hugging Face 等网站的条件下，35B 得 52.2，122B 得 59.0，不加限制是 59.1 和 64.8。

和同样大小的开源模型 Agents-A1 比，35B 在 HLE 上高 4.6 个百分点，在偏信息检索的 SEAL-0 上高 5.8，在 FrontierScience 奥赛题上高 7.0。

FrontierScience 的科研题上，122B 得 66.7，作者自己测的 GPT-5.6 Sol 是 63.3，Agents-A1 是 40.0。

但它不是处处领先。HLE 上，Claude Fable 5 和 Claude Opus 5 的官方数字是 63.8 和 63.6，高于 Axion 受限条件下的 59.0。BioMysteryBench 难题子集上，122B 是 41.7，低于 Claude Opus 5、GPT-5.6 Sol 和 Claude Fable 5。

每一步贡献多少，看表 2。注意这是按训练顺序记录的检查点，不是消融实验。以 35B 为例，在 130 道题的 HLE 子集上，基础模型 47.7，第一轮后 59.2，第二轮后 66.2，强化学习后 70.0。

第二轮的工具数据，对需要查资料、分析数据的题帮助最明显，35B 在 BioMysteryBench 上从 17.6 涨到 35.3。有意思的是，第一轮微调之后，这一项反而从 23.5 掉到了 17.6。

强化学习在两个尺寸上都提高了 HLE 子集和 SEAL-0，比如 35B 的 SEAL-0 从 53.2 到 62.2，但在部分科学推理题上，35B 小幅下降。作者的总结是，很大一部分提升来自两轮微调，强化学习在一部分评测上再往上推。

还有两个补充实验。换一个起点更弱的 Qwen3.5 35B，做完两轮微调，HLE 子集上和 Qwen3.6 的差距从 9.2 个百分点缩到 0.8。只做微调、不做强化学习的 9B 小模型，HLE 子集也到了 63.1。

### 和以前方法的对比

和 MiroThinker、Agents-A1 这些前作比，Axion 首先是流程更短。数据上，Absolute Zero、Agent0 这些工作也按模型水平调难度，Axion 则专门挑基础模型做对的题，沿着它的解法去加难，工具题则是程序先算好答案，再让大模型写题目。

不过论文自己也说了，表 1 里很多对手的成绩来自官方报告或别的论文，工具和执行条件可能不同，这类跨来源比较只能当参考，不是严格对照。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的不是哪一个分数，而是给了一个能复现的基线。三步流程加全套造数据流程和代码，别人可以一步一步地改，看到底哪一步真的有用。

### 局限

论文没有单独写局限这一节，但正文里有几处要留意的保留。

第一，评测方式。Axion 和作者自己测的模型，每个评测跑三次，BioMysteryBench 跑五次，报告的是单次最好成绩，而不是平均。

第二，表 2 是检查点对比，作者明确说不是消融实验，说明不了去掉某一步会怎样。强化学习的效果也随尺寸和评测变化，有的分数还掉了。

第三，R3 带来的分数差异，作者说不能全算在 R3 头上。而且 122B 用了 R3 之后，SEAL-0 从 63.1 掉到了 58.6。

第四，不同尺寸之间不是严格对照。35B 和 122B 的基础模型不同，还多做了强化学习，作者也说这不能当成规模实验来看。

### 未来方向

论文对未来写得不多，主要是希望公开的造数据流程和训练代码，能成为后续 Agent 模型研究的可复现起点。

我的判断是，这篇最值得记住的是，很大一部分提升来自两轮微调，而微调数据加起来不到两万条轨迹。数据的难度和可验证性，可能比训练阶段的数量更重要。

我的判断是，分数也要冷静看。报告的是最好的一次，不少对手是跨来源的数字，在受限条件下的 HLE 和 BioMysteryBench 上，它离最强的闭源模型还有差距。真正的检验，是别人能不能用公开的配方复现出来。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is led by ByteDance, and it's called Axion 1.0. The subtitle is the pitch: open agent foundation models, simplified.

The question behind it is blunt. When you train a strong agent model today, how much of that long training pipeline do you actually need?

### Problem

Some context. Language models are turning into agents. They search, run code, call tools, and work through tasks that take many steps.

The pipelines that build them keep growing: mid-training, several supervised stages, preference optimization, reinforcement learning. The authors' point is that each piece may be well motivated, but stacked together it's hard to tell which ones matter, and the whole thing is expensive to reproduce.

There's a second problem. Many so-called open models release their weights and little else. How the data was built, filtered and verified stays private.

### Prior work

The paper names a few recent systems. MiroThinker one point seven combines mid-training, supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization and RL. Agents-A1 uses domain-routed on-policy distillation. Apodex one point one explores executable environments and coordinating multiple agents.

On the data side, Evol-Instruct makes tasks harder through repeated rewriting, TaskCraft expands verifiable multi-tool tasks, and WebShaper gives structure to the complexity of search tasks.

### Key idea

Axion's key idea: simplify the pipeline, but don't simplify the learning problem. Put the effort into data that is hard and automatically verifiable.

Training has just three steps: two light supervised fine-tuning stages, then reinforcement learning. No dedicated mid-training, no DPO, no on-policy distillation. Stage one teaches reasoning that needs no outside information, stage two teaches reasoning with tools, and RL hardens long, multi-step interaction.

And open here means more than weights. Data construction, filtering, verification, prompts and training code are all released.

### Method

Start with the stage one data, which the paper calls self-contained reasoning: everything you need is in the question. The authors collect public problems in math, physics, chemistry, biology and computer science, but they don't train on them directly. They use them as seeds for harder questions.

The first move is composition. A generator model takes a quantity computed in the seed's solution and writes a new question that needs it as an intermediate result. The answer must be checkable, and the seed must not leak.

The clever part comes next. The base model tries the new question, with Python but no search. If it fails, the question goes straight into the bank. If it succeeds, its solution goes back to the generator, which applies one of four hardening operations at random, aimed squarely at the strategy the model just used.

The figure has a toy example. The base model knows one plus two up to ten is fifty-five. Composition adds one more step. Parameterization swaps ten for n and asks for a formula. Range shift picks a number too big to add term by term. Case analysis alternates the signs, so the answer depends on whether n is even or odd.

How do they know the answers are right? GPT-five point five solves each question three times, independently, and the reference answer is accepted only if all three agree and the derivations check out. Then a separate model solves the bank, and only correct, well-formed trajectories are kept. The figure puts this stage at ten point eight thousand trajectories.

Stage two is about questions that need tools. The authors gather public structured data in areas like biology, chemistry, math, geography and aviation, and use deterministic programs to build each question and compute its answer: filter, select, compute, and finally have a language model phrase it naturally.

Their example: filter for planets with rings, select the least massive one, which is Uranus, and ask for its mean density, about one point two seven grams per cubic centimeter. The solver has to identify Uranus, look up its mass and radius, and do the math.

To force real tool use, the construction databases are blocked during solving; the model only gets search, webpage visits and Python, and questions answerable without tools are removed. Again only correct trajectories survive, seven thousand five hundred of them.

The second fine-tuning stage continues from the first. Loss is computed only on what the model writes itself, its reasoning, tool calls and final answer. Tool outputs are context only.

Step three is reinforcement learning with GRPO, which samples several trajectories per question and compares each with the rest of its group. The reward: a correct answer earns one. A wrong answer can still earn partial credit for verified intermediate steps, up to zero point three, minus zero point one if no answer was submitted, and never below zero.

How are intermediate steps checked? The stage two program already recorded every intermediate result, such as which planet was selected. These are checked in dependency order, and a later step only counts if every step before it was right.

Task selection matters too. The current model attempts each question four times, and a question is kept only if at least one attempt is correct and the four rewards aren't all the same.

One engineering detail. Axion is a mixture-of-experts model, or MoE, and the inference engine and the training engine can route the same token to different experts. A method called R3 records the experts chosen during rollout and replays them in training.

### Results

The setup. Both main models are mixture-of-experts: a 35B model initialized from Qwen three point six, and a 122B model from Qwen three point five. At evaluation time they get web search, webpage visits, scholarly retrieval and Python.

Supervised training is cheap. The 122B model takes seven and a half hours for both stages on thirty-two GPUs, about two hundred forty GPU-hours, and the 35B model about ninety. The paper doesn't give total compute for RL.

The main results are in Table 1. Start with Humanity's Last Exam, or HLE, expert-level questions across many fields. With sites like Hugging Face blocked, the 35B model scores fifty-two point two and the 122B fifty-nine. Without that restriction, it's fifty-nine point one and sixty-four point eight.

Against Agents-A1, an open model of similar size, the 35B model is ahead by four point six points on HLE, five point eight on the search-heavy SEAL-0, and seven on the FrontierScience Olympiad set.

On FrontierScience Research, the 122B model scores sixty-six point seven, against sixty-three point three for GPT-five point six Sol as measured by the authors, and forty for Agents-A1.

But it doesn't lead everywhere. On HLE, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus 5 report sixty-three point eight and sixty-three point six, above Axion's restricted fifty-nine. On the hard subset of BioMysteryBench, the 122B model's forty-one point seven trails Claude Opus 5, GPT-five point six Sol and Claude Fable 5.

Table 2 shows what each step adds. These are checkpoints along the training sequence, not ablations. For the 35B model, on a hundred-thirty-question HLE subset, the base scores forty-seven point seven, then fifty-nine point two after stage one, sixty-six point two after stage two, and seventy after RL.

The tool data helps most where the task needs lookup and data analysis: the 35B model's BioMysteryBench score goes from seventeen point six to thirty-five point three. Oddly, stage one had pushed that same score down, from twenty-three point five to seventeen point six.

RL improves the HLE subset and SEAL-0 at both sizes; the 35B model's SEAL-0 goes from fifty-three point two to sixty-two point two. But on some science reasoning sets, the 35B model slips a little. The authors' summary: much of the gain comes from the two fine-tuning stages, and RL adds more on selected benchmarks.

Two more experiments. Starting from a weaker base, Qwen three point five at 35B, the same fine-tuning shrinks its HLE-subset gap to Qwen three point six from nine point two points to zero point eight. And a 9B model with fine-tuning only, no RL, reaches sixty-three point one on the HLE subset.

### Comparison

Compared with MiroThinker or Agents-A1, the first difference is a shorter pipeline. On data, work like Absolute Zero and Agent0 also adapts difficulty to the model, but Axion specifically hardens the questions the base model already solves, along its own strategy. For tool tasks, a program computes the answer before a language model writes the question.

Still, the paper itself cautions that many baseline numbers in Table 1 come from official reports or other papers, with possibly different tools and settings. Those cross-source comparisons are indicative, not controlled.

My read is that the most valuable thing here isn't any single score. It's a reproducible baseline. A three-step recipe with its data pipelines and code means others can change one piece at a time and see what actually matters.

### Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations section, but the text carries several caveats worth noting.

First, the evaluation protocol. Axion and the models the authors ran themselves get three runs per benchmark, five for BioMysteryBench, and the paper reports the best single run, not the average.

Second, Table 2 is a sequence of checkpoints, and the authors say explicitly it's not an ablation, so it can't tell you what happens if a step is removed. RL's effect also varies by size and benchmark, and some scores went down.

Third, the authors say the score differences with R3 can't be pinned on R3 alone. And with R3, the 122B model's SEAL-0 fell from sixty-three point one to fifty-eight point six.

Fourth, the size comparison isn't controlled. The 35B and 122B models start from different bases and also go through RL, and the authors say it shouldn't be read as a scaling experiment.

### Future work

The paper says little about next steps. Its stated hope is that the released data pipelines and training code become a reproducible starting point for future agent model research.

My read is that the result worth remembering is this: much of the gain comes from two fine-tuning stages, on fewer than twenty thousand trajectories in total. The difficulty and verifiability of the data may matter more than the number of training stages.

My read is also that the scores deserve some caution. They're the best of several runs, many rivals' numbers come from other sources, and on restricted HLE and BioMysteryBench it still trails the strongest closed models. The real test is whether others can reproduce it from the released recipe.
