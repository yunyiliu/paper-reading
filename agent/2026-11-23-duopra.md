**语音助手办不成事卡在前端没开口**

## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇上海交通大学和腾讯等单位的论文，叫 Duopra，副标题是，从全双工对话，走向全双工的办事能力。

它关心的是语音助手，不光要能自然地跟你聊天，还要在聊天的同时，把事情真正办成。

### 问题

先解释全双工。全双工模型边听边说，它得自己决定什么时候接话，什么时候停下，你停顿时不能抢话，你插话它要让，你说话时它还会“嗯”一声，这叫附和。论文把这些叫对话能力。

但要办事，还需要另一种能力，作者叫任务能力。就是判断什么时候该调用工具，用户改口之后按最新的意思来，并且把要求的每一步都做完。

论文开头这张图是个真实例子。用户说，帮我搜一台笔记本，等等，要平板。再加两台到购物车，算了，就一台。产品和数量都改了口，而且加购物车，要用到前一步搜索返回的商品编号。

### 以前的方法

现在常见的做法是前端加后端。前端是语音模型，负责聊天，它把用户的需求写成一段自然语言请求，交给后端，后端是一个会调用工具的大模型，负责真正去执行。

这样分工的问题是，执行可以外包，但请求得前端来发。前端不发请求，后端再强也没用，请求写得不完整，还会把后端带偏。

作者在 Full-Duplex-Bench v3 这个基准上，测了一个这样的系统，Realtime-Venus-Audio 加它自带的 Harness，后端用 Qwen3-8B。在作者的流式测试里，只有 11% 的对话把请求交给了后端，最后的通过率只有 4.7%。

作者把这叫交接天花板。请求都没发出去，后端执行得再完美，通过率也不可能超过交接的比例。所以在这个系统里，瓶颈在发起，不在执行。

要补充一句，这个系统公开报告过 42% 的分数，但论文引用该项目仓库协作者的说法，那是离线推理，工具定义直接写在提示里，和这里的流式设置不一样。

### 核心想法

Duopra 的思路是，让前端自己学会办事。它能看到工具的说明，可以直接调用工具。

关键一步是，把后端也当成一个工具，叫 query。它只有一个参数，就是一段完整、自包含的自然语言任务描述。这样一来，直接调工具和交给后端，用的是同一个动作接口，判断对错的标准也一样。

什么时候可以动手，论文定了一条规则。每个必填参数都得有来源，要么是用户说的话，以最后一次改口为准，要么是前面工具返回的结果，要么是会话里给定的默认值。缺了必填的，就只问缺的那一项。

### 方法

先看模型结构。Duopra 建在 Realtime-Venus-Audio 上，这是一个 9B 的模型。它分两部分，thinker 是语言模型，负责想和决定，talker 是语音生成器，根据 thinker 的隐藏状态，把文字读出来。

模型每一秒处理一个音频单元。每一秒，thinker 决定是听还是说。工具调用写成 JSON，放在一段私有区域里，永远不会被读出来。工具结果返回后，在下一个单元边界插回到流里。

论文给的这个例子很直观。用户问明天航班 CA1701 有没有延误，模型先听两秒，说一句我查一下，然后私下调用航班状态工具，等结果回来，再说晚点二十分钟。

训练分三个阶段。第一阶段是监督微调，用 12800 段对话，七成是中文，教模型这套协议，包括听、问缺的信息、查工具说明、发调用、报结果。

第二阶段是全双工强化学习。每个任务用同一段预先合成的用户语音跑 8 次，工具是模拟的，交给后端的请求由一个冻结的 Qwen3.8-27B 执行。评分结合规则和大模型裁判，裁判认定有依据的话，要引用原文，由程序核对。

第二阶段又分前后两段。前一段练直接调用、改口、追问、打断和附和这些情况。后一段加入多步任务、有条件的调用、交给后端，以及出错后的恢复。

奖励很看重时机。信息够了，三秒内动手加 0.1 分，用了用户已经改掉的旧值，每个扣 0.6 分。后一段的新任务按整个请求打分，全部做完而且回答有依据，三秒内动手得 1.2 分，三到八秒得 1.1 分，更晚得 0.9 分，编造结果扣 0.95 分。

第三阶段，可以叫修嗓子。训练 thinker 会改变它的隐藏状态，而 talker 是根据这些隐藏状态发音的，结果说话可能变得不清楚。

作者的办法是，thinker 和 talker 都不动，只训练中间一个 370 万参数的投影层，让新 thinker 经过它的输出方向，尽量对齐原始模型的方向，同时限制投影层不要离原来的权重太远。

### 实验和效果

评测主要用 Full-Duplex-Bench v3，100 段英语录音，说话带口头禅、停顿和改口，配 12 个模拟 API。其中 66 段需要调用一次工具，18 段两次，16 段三次。每段跑三个随机种子，一共 300 次对话。

判定很严。调用的工具和参数必须和标准答案完全一致，不能少也不能多。改口之前调用过的旧请求，也算错。

主结果在表 1。后端同样用 Qwen3-8B，把前端从 Realtime-Venus-Audio 加 Harness 换成 Duopra，通过率从 4.7% 提到 44.7%。

不用后端、自己直接调工具，Duopra 是 47.7%，只做完第一阶段微调时是 26%。后端换成 DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash，是 49.3%。同样设置下的另一个前后端系统 DuplexOmni，是 17%。

提升从哪来。在基线真的执行了工具的 31 次对话里，基线通过 14 次，Duopra 通过 12 次，并不更好。差距来自剩下 269 次基线根本没动手的对话，Duopra 做成了其中 122 次。

换更强的后端呢。DeepSeek 让发出去的请求成功率更高，但发起调用的比例反而降了，总通过率的变化在统计上不显著。作者的结论是，更强的后端能把请求执行得更好，但替不了前端去发请求。

两种方式都开放时，Duopra 几乎不交给后端，后端是 Qwen3-8B 的 300 次对话里，只交了一次。只能走后端时，给出事实答案的延迟是 14.85 秒，两种都开放时是 10.42 秒。

训练过程也很有意思。第二阶段前一段之后，模型调工具更频繁了，但通过率几乎没变，从 26.0 到 26.7。调用得多，不等于办成得多。

加入后一段之后，通过率又涨了 21 个百分点。前一段的模型，需要两次、三次调用的请求一个都没做成，最终的 Duopra 分别做成了 24.1% 和 31.3%。完全没调用工具的对话，从基线的 89.7% 降到 18.7%。

剩下最大的错误类型是，工具选对了，参数错了。论文举了个例子，用户说订单号 P-O-9-9-9，执行的订单号里，字母 O 变成了数字 0。

对话能力有没有丢。在 FDB v1.0 上，和原始模型比，用户说完时接话的比例从 59.1% 升到 75.6%，用户停顿时抢话的比例从 18.1% 降到 5.4%，用户插话后回应的比例从 79.5% 升到 91.3%。

修嗓子也有效果。同一个 thinker，换上修过的投影层，中文字错率从 6.01% 降到 2.58%，英文词错率从 9.74% 降到 7.66%。

### 和以前方法的对比

和商业系统的公开结果比，作者自己也强调，那些数字来自不同的评测设置和裁判，只能当参考。在这个前提下，作者称 Duopra 和 Gemini Live 2.5 相当，GPT-Realtime 的 60% 和 Gemini Live 3.1 的 54% 仍然领先。

代价也要看。三个训练后的模型，在测试的 55 个附和场景里，一次附和都没有发出。用户插话后回应的比例，也从第二阶段前一段的 96.5% 降到了最终的 91.3%。

还有，修嗓子的效果，是在挑选投影层用的那 221 句话上测的。作者自己说，这只能算挑选集上的证据，不是独立的泛化测试。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的不是 44.7% 这个数，而是它的诊断方法，把通过率拆成有没有发起调用，和发起之后成没成。很多语音 Agent 的问题，出在前端根本没开口办事，换更强的后端解决不了。

我的判断是，弱点也很明显。整套训练和评测只用了一个基础模型，裁判是 Qwen3.8-27B，不是原基准用的 GPT-4o，即使只当参考，离 GPT-Realtime 也还有十个百分点以上。在接话和回应插话两项上，不带工具的 MiniCPM-o 4.5 仍然更高。

### 局限

作者列的局限主要有三条。第一，公开结果的评测设置、裁判和打分方式各不相同，没法直接对比。

第二，Duopra 只在一个全双工基础模型上做了，训练用的是合成语音，声音种类有限。换别的基础模型、更多样的说话条件，还能不能有效，需要进一步验证。

第三，FDB v3 用的是预先录好的用户请求和模拟 API。用户的话是固定的，不会根据 Agent 的回答或者工具结果再追问。

另外，作者也承认，附和能力的丢失说明，同时保住所有对话行为，仍然是一个没解决的问题。

### 未来方向

作者提出的下一步，是在多轮工具任务里评测 Duopra，看它能不能记住任务状态，在用户澄清或者改主意时，调整后面的动作。

还有，把丢掉的附和能力找回来。

我的判断是，语音 Agent 接下来比的，可能不只是后端模型有多强，而是前端能不能在对的时刻，发出一个完整、准确的请求。这篇给了一个能把这件事量化的方法。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Tencent, among others. It's called Duopra, and the subtitle is: from full-duplex conversation to full-duplex agency.

The question is about voice assistants. It's not enough for them to chat naturally. They also have to get things done while the conversation is still going.

### Problem

First, what full-duplex means. A full-duplex model listens while it speaks. It decides on its own when to take the turn, when to stop, not to jump in when you pause, to yield when you cut in, and to drop in a little "mm-hm" while you talk, which is called a backchannel. The paper calls all of this conversational agency.

Getting things done takes something else, which the authors call task agency. Knowing when to call a tool, following the user's latest wording after a correction, and finishing every step that was asked for.

The paper opens with a real example. The user says: search for a laptop, wait, a tablet. Add two to the cart, actually, just one. Both the product and the quantity change mid-sentence, and adding to the cart needs the product ID that the search returns.

### Prior work

The common design today is a frontend plus a backend. The frontend is the speech model that handles the conversation. It writes the user's need as a natural-language request and hands it to the backend, a tool-using language model that actually does the work.

The problem with that split is simple. Execution can be handed off, but the request still has to come from the frontend. If the frontend never sends it, no backend can help, and if the request is incomplete, it can send the backend the wrong way.

The authors tested one such system on Full-Duplex-Bench v3: Realtime-Venus-Audio with its own Harness, using Qwen3-8B as the backend. In their streaming pipeline, only eleven percent of sessions handed a request to the backend, and only four point seven percent passed.

The authors call this the hand-off ceiling. If the request never goes out, even perfect backend execution can't push the pass rate above the hand-off rate. So in this system, the bottleneck is initiation, not execution.

One caveat worth knowing. This system has a published score of forty-two percent, but citing a collaborator on that project's repository, the paper says it came from offline inference with the tool definitions in the prompt, not the streaming setup used here.

### Key idea

Duopra's idea is to let the frontend learn to do the work itself. It sees the tool descriptions and can call tools directly.

The key move is to treat the backend as just one more tool, called query. It takes a single argument: a complete, self-contained task written in natural language. So calling a tool directly and delegating to the backend share one action interface, and they're judged by the same standard.

The paper also sets a rule for when to act. Every required argument needs a source: what the user said, with the last correction winning; the result of an earlier tool call; or a default given by the session. If a required value has no source, ask for that missing item only.

### Method

Start with the architecture. Duopra is built on Realtime-Venus-Audio, a nine-billion-parameter model. It has two parts. The thinker is the language model that reasons and decides. The talker is the speech generator, which voices the text based on the thinker's hidden states.

The model processes audio in one-second units. Every second, the thinker decides whether to listen or speak. Tool calls are written as JSON in a private span that is never spoken. When a result comes back, it's inserted into the stream at the next unit boundary.

The paper's example makes this concrete. The user asks whether flight CA1701 is delayed tomorrow. The model listens for two seconds, says "let me check," quietly calls the flight status tool, and once the result arrives, says it's twenty minutes late.

Training has three stages. Stage one is supervised fine-tuning on twelve thousand eight hundred sessions, seventy percent of them in Chinese, to teach the protocol: listening, asking for missing information, looking up tool schemas, issuing calls, and reporting results.

Stage two is full-duplex reinforcement learning. Each task is rolled out eight times on the same pre-synthesized user audio. The tools are simulated, and delegated requests are run by a frozen Qwen3.8-27B. Scoring mixes rules with a language-model judge, and whenever the judge says a statement is supported, it has to quote it verbatim, which a program then checks.

Stage two itself has two halves. The first covers direct calls, corrections, clarifying questions, interruptions and backchannels. The second adds multi-step tasks, conditional calls, delegation to the backend, and recovery from failures.

The reward cares a lot about timing. With enough information, acting within three seconds earns zero point one, and using a value the user already corrected costs zero point six per value. The new tasks in the second half are scored on the whole request. Finishing everything with a grounded answer earns one point two if the model acts within three seconds, one point one between three and eight seconds, and zero point nine after that. A fabricated result costs zero point nine five.

Stage three you could call fixing the voice. Training the thinker changes its hidden states, and since the talker speaks from those states, the speech can become harder to understand.

The fix leaves both the thinker and the talker alone. It trains only the projector between them, about three point seven million parameters, so that the new thinker's states, once projected, point the same way as the original model's, while keeping the projector's weights close to where they started.

### Results

The main test is Full-Duplex-Bench v3: one hundred English recordings full of fillers, pauses and self-corrections, with twelve simulated APIs. Sixty-six need one tool call, eighteen need two, and sixteen need three. Each runs with three random seeds, so three hundred sessions per setup.

The grading is strict. The executed tools and arguments must match the reference exactly, nothing missing and nothing extra. Even a call made before the user corrected themselves counts as a failure.

The main results are in Table 1. With the same Qwen3-8B backend, swapping the frontend from Realtime-Venus-Audio plus Harness to Duopra raises the pass rate from four point seven to forty-four point seven percent.

Calling tools itself with no backend, Duopra reaches forty-seven point seven, up from twenty-six after stage one alone. With DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash as the backend, it reaches forty-nine point three. Another frontend-backend system, DuplexOmni, gets seventeen in the same setup.

So where does the gain come from? On the thirty-one sessions where the baseline actually executed a tool, the baseline passes fourteen and Duopra twelve, so no better. The gap comes from the other two hundred sixty-nine sessions, where the baseline never acted at all. Duopra completes one hundred twenty-two of them.

What about a stronger backend? DeepSeek raises the success rate of the requests that do go out, but fewer requests go out, and the change in overall pass rate isn't statistically significant. The authors' conclusion: a stronger backend executes requests better, but it can't send them for the frontend.

When both routes are open, Duopra almost never delegates: once in three hundred sessions with the Qwen3-8B backend. When it can only go through the backend, the factual answer arrives after fourteen point eight five seconds, versus ten point four two with both routes open.

The training curve is telling too. After the first half of stage two, the model calls tools more often, but the pass rate barely moves, from twenty-six point zero to twenty-six point seven. Calling more is not the same as completing more.

The second half adds twenty-one percentage points. The earlier checkpoint completed none of the requests needing two or three calls; the final Duopra completes twenty-four point one and thirty-one point three percent of them. Sessions with no tool call at all drop from eighty-nine point seven percent for the baseline to eighteen point seven.

The biggest remaining failure is the right tools with a wrong argument. The paper's example: the user says order P-O-nine-nine-nine, and the executed order ID has a zero where the letter O should be.

Did the conversational skills survive? On FDB v1.0, compared with the original model, taking the turn when the user finishes goes from fifty-nine point one to seventy-five point six percent, jumping in during pauses falls from eighteen point one to five point four, and responding after the user interrupts rises from seventy-nine point five to ninety-one point three.

The voice fix helps as well. With the same thinker, the repaired projector cuts the Chinese character error rate from six point zero one to two point five eight percent, and the English word error rate from nine point seven four to seven point six six.

### Comparison

Against published results from commercial systems, the authors stress that those numbers come from different setups and judges, so they're reference points only. With that caveat, the authors put Duopra on par with Gemini Live 2.5, while GPT-Realtime at sixty and Gemini Live 3.1 at fifty-four stay ahead.

There are costs. On the fifty-five backchannel items tested, none of the three post-trained models produced a single backchannel. And responding after an interruption drops from ninety-six point five after the first half of stage two to ninety-one point three in the final model.

Also, the voice fix was measured on the same two hundred twenty-one utterances used to pick the projector. The authors themselves say that's selection-set evidence, not an independent test of generalization.

My read is that the most useful thing here isn't the forty-four point seven. It's the diagnosis: splitting the pass rate into whether a call was initiated at all, and whether it succeeded once initiated. A lot of voice agents fail because the frontend never acts, and a stronger backend can't fix that.

My read is that the weaknesses are real too. Everything rests on one base model, the judge is Qwen3.8-27B rather than the GPT-4o the benchmark originally used, and even as a rough reference it's more than ten points behind GPT-Realtime. On taking turns and responding to interruptions, MiniCPM-o 4.5 with no tools still scores higher.

### Limitations

The authors list three main limitations. First, published results use different setups, judges and scoring, so they can't be compared directly.

Second, Duopra is built on a single full-duplex model and trained on synthetic speech from a limited set of voices. Whether the gains carry over to other base models and more varied speech is still open.

Third, FDB v3 uses prerecorded requests and simulated APIs. The user's words are fixed, so there are no follow-up requests in response to what the agent says or what the tools return.

And the authors acknowledge that losing backchannels shows keeping every conversational behavior intact is still an open problem.

### Future work

Next, the authors propose evaluating Duopra on multi-turn tool tasks, to see whether it keeps track of the task and adjusts its next actions as users clarify or change their goals.

And getting the lost backchannels back.

My read is that the next race in voice agents may not just be about how strong the backend is, but whether the frontend sends a complete, accurate request at the right moment. This paper gives a way to measure exactly that.
