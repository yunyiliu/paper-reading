# h1: A Taste of the Bitter Lesson in Automated Theorem Proving

**简单循环常能追平复杂流程**

- 单位 Affiliation: FAIR at Meta · EPFL · 纽约大学
- 方向 Direction: Agent
- 讲解日期 Covered: 2026-10-30
- 视频 Videos: [YouTube 中文](https://youtu.be/UDwm6ep556w)

## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇 Meta 旗下 FAIR 的论文，合作方有 EPFL 和纽约大学，名字叫 h1，副标题是，自动定理证明里的一次苦涩教训。

它问的是，让 AI 证明数学定理，需要人精心设计的复杂流程，还是一个简单循环，加上足够的推理算力就够了。

### 问题

先补个背景。Lean 是一种形式化证明语言，命题和证明都写成代码，编译器能机械地检查每一步。Mathlib 是它的公共数学库。

为什么重要。前沿模型已经能写研究级的数学论证，但自然语言的论证很难核对。作者认为，把它们变成机器能检查的证明，已经是自动化发现的必要一环。

可这几年，形式化证明越来越依赖人设计的工作流，比如先规划，再拆成小引理，再调度专门的证明器。这些都写在 harness 里，也就是包在模型外面的工具、上下文和控制循环。

### 以前的方法

以前的系统大致两类。一类把流程写死在代码里，比如 Goedel-Architect，先生成证明蓝图，再派不同的 Agent 去证各个引理，还有 MerLean，和 AlphaProof Nexus 的完整版。

另一类是简单循环，只负责提供 Lean 的反馈，让模型一直干，比如 AxProverBase，和 AlphaProof Nexus 的基础版。

作者指出，新工作流往往和更强的模型、更多的算力一起登场，或者拿扩展性很差的基线来比，提升到底来自哪里，说不清。

而且大家都报一次尝试的成功率，但一次尝试的分量差别很大，Goedel-Architect 一次尝试最多能改 16 次证明计划，AxProverBase 一次能跑 50 轮。所以这篇坚持按美元成本来比。

### 核心想法

核心想法来自 Sutton 的苦涩的教训，能随算力扩展的通用方法，最终会胜过依赖人类领域知识的方法。

用到 harness 上就是，前沿模型已经会推理、会用工具，那就别替它规定怎么证明，只给它一个高效的 Lean 接口，让它一直干，直到验证通过，或者预算用完。

### 方法

看这张结构图，h1 只有三块，证明搜索，验证，和反思。

证明搜索阶段，模型在一个持久的工作文件上，边推理边调用工具，每一轮最多 T 个回合。每改一次都会编译，马上返回错误，和还没证完的目标。

工具都是可选的，可以搜索 Mathlib，可以在 REPL 里试跑一段证明，也可以查看目标和定义。用不用，怎么用，由模型自己决定。

提交之后，外部验证器 SafeVerify 对照原始命题检查，用占位符跳过的步骤，和偷偷加的假设，都会被拒绝。

没通过的话，一个反思模型会把整段记录压缩成摘要，留下已验证的进展、失败的教训和有用的引理名，还会劝模型别放弃。然后模型带着摘要接着干。

和别的简单循环比，h1 的讲究在接口细节。它做局部修改，而不是重写整份证明，反馈即时，同一轮里的上下文还能被缓存。

### 实验和效果

评测从竞赛题到研究问题。PutnamBench 是 672 道 Putnam 大学生竞赛题，FATE-X 是 100 道博士资格考试水平以上的代数题，FC100-solved 是 100 道已知解法的猜想题，还有 2026 年 IMO 的 6 道题。

用 GPT-5.6 Sol，h1 在 FATE-X 上解出 90 道，FC100-solved 上 78 道，2026 年 IMO 全部解出。作者称，按 2026 年 8 月 27 日的对比截止，这些都是最好成绩。

用 GPT-5.5，PutnamBench 的 672 道里解出 670 道。这些都是每题只跑一次，没有重复，也没有并行。

便宜的开源模型也能打。DeepSeek V4 Flash 每题预算 14.35 美元，解出 43 道 FATE-X，超过 Leanstral 1.5 公布的 34 道。不过附录说明，因为配置失误，这组运行关掉了反思，成绩可能偏低。

更有意思的是开放问题。在 236 个标为未解决的目标里，h1 加 GPT-5.6 Sol 解决了 8 个，包括 Erdős 138 和 337 两个问题的变体，和三个 OEIS 数列猜想。

它还揪出了题库里写错的命题。比如庞加莱猜想的形式化少了一个前提，系统直接证明了，写出来的这条命题不成立。作者说，这类错误大部分已经修复。

### 和以前方法的对比

最核心的是同模型对比。在 PutnamBench 上，都用 DeepSeek V4 Flash，h1 一次加长的尝试解出 598 道，Goedel-Architect 跑四次，还有自然语言证明做引导，是 597 道。估算成本，h1 每题 1.08 美元，对方 1.47 美元。

作者还指出，Goedel-Architect 自带的简单基线，在 200 题子集上跑 32 次只有百分之五十四点五，同一个模型放进 h1，在完整基准上一次就是百分之八十九。弱基线，会夸大复杂流程的必要性。

2025 年 Putnam 的 12 道题里，Numina 为最难的 A5 专门换了一套多 Agent 工作流，预算 1000 美元。h1 只用 GPT-5.2，每题跑一次，12 道全解，A5 花了 81.52 美元。

也有例外。在 FormalQualBench 上用 Opus 4.7，MerLean 比 h1 多解一道，重复尝试也追不上。h1 换成 Opus 4.8，解出 13 道，超过 MerLean 的 10 道。

不过这已经不是同模型了，作者也承认，MerLean 换新模型大概也会变强。他们预计，新模型会把工作流里的策略学进去，差距会继续缩小。

再看一次长尝试和多次重复。在五个开放的 Erdős 问题上，都用 Gemini 3.1 Pro，h1 单次尝试的成功率随预算涨到百分之八十二点五，平均每证出一次 121 美元。AlphaProof Nexus 基础版跑三次是百分之七十五点四，191 美元，完整系统是百分之七十六，317 美元。

中间这张图就是这组对比。把失败的花费也算上，h1 单次尝试 138 美元达到百分之八十二点五，跑三次要 202 美元才追上。不过预算再大，重复尝试的成功率更高，加深还是加次数，要看预算和题目。

但简单不等于随便。同一个模型，不同的简单循环，解题数和成本能差好几倍。

比如 AxProverBase，每轮都重新生成整份证明。同样用 Gemini 3 Flash，每题 20 美元，它在 Putnam 的 100 题子集上解出 18 道，h1 是 69 道。它失败的编译里，百分之四十二点二卡在无效的库导入，h1 预先导入了整个 Mathlib，不会出这种错。

再比如 Claude Code。都用 Opus 4.8，在 FATE-X 上，原生 Claude Code 解出 63 道，换上作者的提示词和工具是 73 道，h1 是 83 道。差别在于，模型可能不调验证器就停了，或者被拒就放弃，而 h1 被拒会自动开始下一轮。

上下文管理也关键，看右边这张图。反思太勤，缓存用不上，隔得太久，上下文太长，效果变差。用 Gemini 3 Flash，在 Putnam 的 100 题子集上，每个回合都反思，解出 50 道，每 10 个回合是 72 道，200 个回合掉到 60 道。

我的判断是，这几组对比的价值，在于把工作流的收益，和模型、算力的收益拆开了。同模型、成本可比时，简单循环在大多数测试里追平，甚至超过了专门系统。

我的判断是，对做 Agent 的人，更有用的是它讲清了 harness 该管什么，不管模型怎么证，只管让它高效地查、改、验证，并决定要不要继续。

### 局限

作者讲了几条局限。第一，前沿模型是黑箱，没法衡量训练时见过基准题的影响。他们尽量用了比题目更早发布的模型。

第二，h1 只处理单个文件。像费马大定理这种超出上下文窗口的工程，需要跨文件管理和多 Agent 并行，它还做不到。工具也只覆盖 Lean。

第三，在 707 道对比题里，有两道是专门系统证出、h1 在同模型下没证出的。其中 Erdős 125，h1 的 16 次尝试全部以 Lean 超时告终。

另外，h1 的成本都是按 token 用量和参考价格折算的。在耗时上，专门系统有时更快，比如 Numina 解 12 道 Putnam 题的总用时，就比 h1 少。

### 未来方向

作者也承认，h1 的接口本身就是人设计的，和苦涩的教训有点拧着。他们认为，现阶段算力花在推理上，人力花在接口上，效果最好，再往后，也许模型能自己搭 harness。

下一步，他们想加入并行 Agent、代码库管理和更多工具，同样不规定怎么用。他们的建议是，设计工作流之前，先在现实预算下试试前沿模型加简单循环。

我的判断是，最值得带走的是评测习惯。以后看类似的结果，先问两句，是不是同一个模型，花了多少钱。

我的判断是，短板在成本和时间。有一道群论题，MerLean 用 Opus 4.7 三个多小时就证出来了，h1 试了 11 次，一共花了 6000 美元、255 个小时也没证出，换成 GPT-5.6 Sol 才解决。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from FAIR at Meta, with co-authors at EPFL and New York University. It's called h1: a taste of the bitter lesson in automated theorem proving.

The question: to get AI to prove theorems, do you need carefully engineered workflows, or is a simple loop plus enough inference compute just as good?

### Problem

Some background. Lean is a formal proof language: statements and proofs are written as code, and a compiler checks every step. Mathlib is its shared math library.

Why it matters. Frontier models already write research-level math, but informal arguments are hard to check. The authors argue that turning them into machine-checked proofs is now a necessary part of automated discovery.

Yet formal proving has leaned more and more on human-designed workflows: plan, break the proof into lemmas, orchestrate specialized provers. All of that lives in the harness, meaning the tools, context and control loop around a model.

### Prior work

Existing systems fall into two camps. One hard-codes the workflow, like Goedel-Architect, which writes a proof blueprint and assigns separate agents to its lemmas, or MerLean, or the full AlphaProof Nexus.

The other is simple loops that just supply Lean feedback and keep the model working, like AxProverBase and AlphaProof Nexus's basic agent.

The authors point out that new workflows often arrive with stronger models and more compute, or against baselines that scale poorly, so it's unclear where the gains come from.

And single-attempt success hides a lot. One Goedel-Architect attempt allows up to sixteen revisions of its proof plan, while one AxProverBase attempt allows fifty iterations. So this paper compares systems in dollars.

### Key idea

The key idea is Sutton's bitter lesson: general methods that scale with computation eventually beat approaches built on human domain knowledge.

Applied to harnesses: frontier models can already reason and use tools, so don't tell them how to prove. Give them an efficient interface to Lean, and keep them working until a validator accepts the proof or the budget runs out.

### Method

Here's the diagram. h1 has just three parts: proof search, validation, and reflection.

In proof search, the model edits a persistent working file, reasoning and calling tools for up to T turns per iteration. Every edit is compiled, and errors and open goals come straight back.

The tools are optional: search Mathlib, test proof fragments in the REPL, inspect goals and definitions. Whether and how to use them is the model's call.

On submission, a validator called SafeVerify checks the file against the original statement, rejecting placeholders and added assumptions.

If it fails, a reflection model compacts the transcript into a summary of verified progress, lessons and useful lemma names, and pushes the model not to give up. Then work continues from the current file.

What sets h1 apart from other simple loops is the interface: targeted edits instead of full rewrites, immediate feedback, and context that stays cached within an iteration.

### Results

The benchmarks span competitions to research: six hundred seventy-two Putnam problems in PutnamBench, one hundred PhD-level algebra problems in FATE-X, one hundred Formal Conjectures problems with known solutions, and the six problems of IMO twenty twenty-six.

With GPT-5.6 Sol, h1 solves ninety on FATE-X, seventy-eight on the Formal Conjectures set, and all six IMO problems. The authors report these as state of the art, against published results up to late August.

With GPT-5.5, it solves six hundred seventy PutnamBench problems. All with one run per problem, no repetition, no parallelism.

A cheap open-weight model holds up too. DeepSeek V4 Flash solves forty-three FATE-X problems with a cap of about fourteen dollars per problem, beating Leanstral's published thirty-four. A configuration error turned reflection off for these runs, the appendix says, so they may understate it.

The open problems are more striking. Out of two hundred thirty-six targets marked open, h1 with GPT-5.6 Sol settled eight, including variants of two Erdős problems and three conjectures about OEIS sequences.

It also caught wrongly formalized statements. The Poincaré conjecture's formalization was missing a hypothesis, and the system simply disproved it as written. Most of these have since been fixed.

### Comparison

The core is same-model comparisons. On PutnamBench with DeepSeek V4 Flash, one extended h1 attempt solves five hundred ninety-eight, against five hundred ninety-seven for Goedel-Architect with four attempts and natural-language proof hints. Estimated cost: one dollar and eight cents per problem for h1, versus one dollar forty-seven.

Goedel-Architect's own simple baseline reaches only fifty-four point five percent with thirty-two attempts on a subset. The same model in h1 gets eighty-nine percent in one attempt on the full benchmark. A weak baseline overstates the need for workflows.

On Putnam twenty twenty-five, Numina switched to a multi-agent workflow with a thousand-dollar budget for the hardest problem, A5. h1 with GPT-5.2 alone, one run per problem, solves all twelve, spending about eighty-two dollars on A5.

There is an exception. On FormalQualBench with Opus 4.7, MerLean solves one more problem than h1, and repetition doesn't close the gap. With Opus 4.8, h1 solves thirteen, beating MerLean's ten.

That's no longer the same model, and the authors grant that MerLean would likely improve too. They expect newer models to absorb the strategies workflows prescribe, shrinking the gap.

Next, one long attempt versus several. On five open Erdős problems with Gemini 3.1 Pro, a single h1 attempt climbs with budget to eighty-two point five percent, at a mean of a hundred twenty-one dollars per proof. Three attempts of AlphaProof Nexus's basic agent reach seventy-five point four percent at a hundred ninety-one, and the full system seventy-six percent at three hundred seventeen.

This middle chart shows it. Counting failed attempts too, one h1 attempt hits eighty-two point five percent at a hundred thirty-eight dollars, while three h1 attempts need two hundred two to match. At bigger budgets repetition goes higher, so depth versus breadth depends on budget and problem.

But simple doesn't mean careless. With the same model, simple loops differ several-fold in solve rate and cost.

AxProverBase regenerates the whole proof every round. With Gemini 3 Flash at twenty dollars a problem, it solves eighteen on a hundred-problem Putnam subset, against sixty-nine for h1. And forty-two percent of its failed compilations die on a bad library import, which can't happen in h1, since all of Mathlib is already imported.

Then Claude Code. With Opus 4.8 on FATE-X, native Claude Code solves sixty-three, seventy-three with the authors' prompt and tools, and h1 eighty-three. The model can stop without calling the validator or quit after a rejection, while h1 just starts the next iteration.

Context management matters too, as the right-hand chart shows. Reflect too often and the cache goes to waste; wait too long and the context bloats. With Gemini 3 Flash on the hundred-problem Putnam subset, reflecting every turn solves fifty, every ten turns seventy-two, every two hundred turns sixty.

My read is that these comparisons separate what the workflow adds from what the model and compute add. At the same model and comparable cost, the simple loop matched or beat specialized systems in most tests.

My read is that for agent builders, the lesson is what a harness should own: not how the model proves, but cheap lookup, editing and checking, and the decision to keep going.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, frontier models are black boxes, so benchmark exposure in training can't be measured. They used models that predate the problems where possible.

Second, h1 works on one file. Projects bigger than a context window, like formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem, need cross-file management and parallel agents. And its tools cover only Lean.

Third, across seven hundred seven compared problems, specialized systems solved two that h1 didn't with the same model. On one, Erdős problem one twenty-five, all sixteen h1 attempts ended in Lean timeouts.

Also, h1's costs are computed from token usage and reference prices. And specialized systems can be faster: Numina solved the twelve Putnam problems in less total time than h1.

### Future work

The authors admit h1's interface is itself hand-designed, which sits awkwardly with the bitter lesson. For now, they argue, compute belongs in reasoning and human effort in the interface; later, models might build their own harnesses.

Next they'd add parallel agents, codebase management and richer tools, again without prescribing their use. Their advice is to try a frontier model in a simple loop at a realistic budget before designing a workflow.

My read is that the habit to take away is about evaluation. When you see a result like this, ask: same model, and at what cost?

My read is that the weak spots are cost and time. On one group-theory problem, MerLean with Opus 4.7 found a proof in about three hours, while eleven h1 attempts spent a combined six thousand dollars and two hundred fifty-five hours without one. Only GPT-5.6 Sol cracked it.
