## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇腾讯微信视觉团队和西安电子科技大学的论文，名字叫 WeVisCut，副标题是，把整个剪辑室装进一个 Agent。

它要回答的问题是，给一份写好的旁白稿，能不能让一个 9B 的小模型，自己规划镜头、找画面、配音、排时间线、检查返工，最后交付一条完整的长视频。

### 问题

先说难在哪。把旁白做成长视频，要拆分镜、找素材、合成配音、拼时间线，这些决定是互相牵连的。改了一个镜头的时长，后面的时间点都要跟着变，找来的画面不合适，可能要回头重新规划一串镜头。

而且这是一个很长的流程。论文附录里有一个完整案例，Agent 一共调用了 16 轮工具，跑了 31 分钟，交付了一条大约 395 秒的视频。中间任何一步出错，都得能发现，能修。

### 以前的方法

以前的 Agent 剪辑系统，大多是几个专门的 Agent 加一套工具流程，背后由通用大模型驱动。作者认为，这类做法没有回答一个问题，一个专门训练过的小模型，在质量和成本之间能做到什么程度。

也有人训练过专门的模型，比如 Crayotter 的后续工作，训练了一个 9B 的剪辑模型，但用的是人工构造的任务，只根据最终视频的偏好来学。

训练这种 Agent，最大的难点是数据。网上有大量做好的成片，但成片里看不到用了哪些工具，中间产物是什么，哪里出过错，又是怎么修的，而这些恰恰是训练 Agent 需要的监督。

### 核心想法

作者的思路分三块。第一，搭一个有状态的执行环境，论文叫 agent harness，所有工具调用都要经过它校验，生产进度记在一份共享的状态里。

第二，从成片倒推剪辑意图，再在这个环境里真的执行一遍，把成片变成可以执行的训练数据。

第三，用这些数据训练一个 9B 的模型，让它一个人分饰三角，调度、内容生成和质检。

### 方法

先看执行环境。模型每一轮只选一个工具，比如规划分镜、检索画面加配音、给重点镜头套动画模板、找背景音乐、合成时间线、质检、修复，最后拼接。选哪个工具，看的是当前状态，而不是一条固定的流程。

关键设计是，只有环境能把结果写进状态。如果一次调用缺少前提条件，就会被拒绝，并返回一个明确的阻塞原因。比如拼接成片的请求，不能代替还没渲染的镜头和还没找到的背景音乐，环境会告诉模型缺什么，模型补齐之后再重试。

附录里的案例正好演示了这一点。Agent 去拼接成片，被拒绝了，原因是 30 个镜头一个都还没渲染，背景音乐也没有。它先批量渲染 30 个镜头，再去找音乐，然后用一模一样的参数重新拼接，这次成功了。

质检和修复也是有边界的。质检发现问题，只修受影响的镜头，没问题的结果保留下来，比如改了一个装饰动画，就不用重新生成配音。修复的次数有上限。

再看数据从哪来。作者从 B 站收集了已经做好的旁白类视频，覆盖科技数码、商业财经、自然科学和人文社科，最后收进来 3750 个视频项目。

数据流水线分四步。第一步叫剪辑反演，从成片里恢复出带时间戳的旁白、镜头结构，以及每句话对应哪个镜头，相当于倒推出一份分镜脚本。注意，恢复的是视频想表达的结构，不是原剪辑师的具体操作。

第二步叫验证回放，拿这份分镜脚本，在和部署时一致的工具环境里真的执行一遍，每次工具的真实返回都会更新状态。第三步是观察、反思、修复，回放中发现问题，就做有限次数的局部修复。

第四步，把这些执行记录整理成训练样本。缺参数、顺序错误、交付失败这些情况都如实保留，不会被改写成成功。最后得到 134771 条样本，其中调度 15000 条，内容生成 88413 条，质检 31358 条。

训练分三个阶段，底座是 Qwen3.5-9B，用 LoRA 训练。第一阶段是监督微调，再加一轮拒绝采样微调，学生自己跑，出错时退回出错前的状态，由老师模型接着做完，只有通过运行检查的轨迹才留下来训练。

第二阶段用一种叫 Guided-OPD 的在线蒸馏，老师是 Qwen3.5-397B。每一轮由老师还是学生来生成，是随机抽的，老师出手的概率从 0.8 逐渐降到 0，训练最后完全由学生自己跑。

第三阶段是强化学习，作者叫 RCF-DAPO。核心是只在同一个角色、同一个生产状态下比较候选。比如调度这个角色，把状态恢复到同一时刻，采样几个不同的下一步，各自往下执行，再比较谁的奖励更高。

### 实验和效果

评测用的是训练时留出来的 100 个任务，每个系统跑 5 次。作者提出了五个指标，分别看旁白内容是否忠实，画面和旁白是否对得上，分镜计划有没有落实，长视频后半段质量能不能保持，以及成片能不能成功交付。总分是五项的简单平均。

第一组对比，所有模型用同一套工具和环境，只换模型。结果在表 1，WeVisCut 总分 90.5，比训练前的 Qwen3.5-9B 的 74.5 高了 16 分，五项全部提高。

同样的环境下，Claude Opus 5 总分 89.9，Gemini 3.1 Pro 是 84.6。WeVisCut 有四项均值比 Opus 5 高，画面和旁白匹配这一项略低，每一项的差距都不超过 1.3 分。

差别在成本。每交付一分钟成片，WeVisCut 生成 2377 个 token，Opus 5 是 78763 个，大约是它的三十三分之一。

各训练阶段的作用在表 3。监督微调把成片交付成功率从 70.4 拉到 87.6，是这一项上最大的一步提升。拒绝采样微调和在线蒸馏继续往上加，最后的强化学习把总分从 84.9 提到 90.5。

### 和以前方法的对比

第二组对比，是和其他完整的剪辑系统比，各用各的工具和流程，任务相同。表 2 里，WeVisCut 总分 90.5，Crayotter 是 84.9，VideoAgent 是 82.5。

成本差得更多。每一分钟成片，WeVisCut 花 2.1 分钟，估算 0.09 美元，Crayotter 是 8.6 分钟、0.97 美元，VideoAgent 是 10.4 分钟、0.58 美元。论文说明，这是整套流程的成本，不只是模型本身。

人工评测请了三位评审，盲评这三个系统的视频，按 1 到 5 分打分。WeVisCut 总体偏好是 4.56 分，另外两个都在 3.5 分左右。作者也说明，这些是描述性的均值，不能说明差异在统计上显著。

我的判断是，和 Opus 5 打平这个说法要打点折扣。总分 90.5 对 89.9，只差 0.6 分，而 WeVisCut 多数单项跨轮的标准差在 2 分上下，总分本身没有给不确定性。另外，画面和旁白匹配这一项，是由 Claude Opus 5 当评委打分的，而它同时也是被比较的对象。

我的判断是，更站得住的结论是成本，在同一套环境里，一个 9B 小模型用大约三十三分之一的 token，拿到了和顶级闭源模型接近的分数。还要注意，测试任务是从同一批 B 站视频里留出的项目，和训练数据来源相同，换一类视频效果如何，论文没有测。

### 局限

作者自己列了几条局限。第一，重点镜头的动画依赖现有的模板库，少见的视觉风格只能用最接近的模板来凑。

第二，数据覆盖不了所有领域和剪辑习惯，验证回放也只保证在他们注册的这套环境里能执行。第三，长视频的质量依赖画面检索是否稳定。

第四，画面和旁白匹配这个指标依赖视觉语言模型打分，作者做了隔离和校准，但承认消除不了评委的偏差。

### 未来方向

未来方向，作者提到可以自动生成模板来扩充模板库，也可以接入训练好的视频生成模型，减少对检索的依赖。论文首页给出了代码和模型权重的地址。

我的判断是，这篇最值得借鉴的不是剪辑本身，而是怎么给 Agent 造训练数据。从成品倒推意图，在真实环境里回放，把失败和修复都如实留下，这套办法可以用到其他只有成品、没有操作记录的领域。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Tencent's WeChat Vision team and Xidian University. It's called WeVisCut, and the subtitle is a good summary: the whole editing room in one agent.

The question is this. Hand a finished narration script to a nine-billion-parameter model. Can it plan the shots, find the footage, voice the narration, build the timeline, check its own work, and deliver a complete long-form video?

### Problem

Here's why that's hard. Turning narration into a long video means splitting it into shots, finding visuals, synthesizing speech and assembling a timeline, and those decisions depend on each other. Change one shot's length and every later timestamp moves. Pick the wrong footage and you may have to go back and replan a whole run of shots.

It's also a long process. One complete example in the appendix takes sixteen tool turns and thirty-one minutes to deliver a video of about three hundred and ninety-five seconds. Any step along the way can fail, and the agent has to notice and fix it.

### Prior work

Most existing agentic editors chain several specialized agents and tool workflows, driven by general-purpose models. The authors' point is that this leaves an open question: how far can a compact, task-trained model go on quality versus cost?

Some work has trained a dedicated model. A follow-up to Crayotter trains a nine-billion-parameter editing model, but on manually constructed tasks, learning only from preferences over the final videos.

The real bottleneck for training an agent like this is data. There are plenty of finished videos online, but a finished video doesn't show which tools were used, what the intermediate artifacts looked like, or what broke and how it was fixed. That's exactly the supervision an agent needs.

### Key idea

The approach has three parts. First, a stateful execution environment, which the paper calls the agent harness. Every tool call is validated by it, and production progress lives in one shared state.

Second, work backwards from finished videos to recover the editing intent, then actually execute that intent inside the harness, turning finished videos into executable training data.

Third, use that data to train one nine-billion-parameter model that plays three roles: orchestrator, content generator, and critic.

### Method

Start with the harness. Each turn, the model picks one tool: plan shots, retrieve visuals and synthesize narration, decorate highlight shots with animation templates, search for background music, compose the timeline, judge quality, repair, and finally stitch. Which tool comes next depends on the current state, not on a fixed pipeline.

The key design choice is that only the runtime writes results into the state. If a call is missing a prerequisite, it's rejected with a specific blocker. A request to stitch the final video can't stand in for shot renders or background music that don't exist yet. The runtime says what's missing, and the model produces it and tries again.

The appendix example shows exactly this. The agent asks to stitch the final video and gets rejected: none of the thirty shots has been rendered, and there's no background music. So it renders all thirty shots in one batch, finds the music, and reissues the identical stitch call. This time it succeeds.

Inspection and repair are bounded too. When the critic finds a problem, only the affected shots are revised, and everything that still works is kept. Fixing a decoration, for instance, doesn't mean regenerating the narration audio. And the number of repair attempts is capped.

Now the data. The authors collected finished narration videos from Bilibili, covering tech and digital products, business and finance, natural science, and the social sciences and humanities. Three thousand seven hundred and fifty video projects made it into the set.

The data pipeline has four stages. Stage one is editing inversion: from each finished video, recover the timestamped narration, the shot structure, and which sentence goes with which shot. In effect, a reconstructed storyboard. Note that this recovers what the video was meant to show, not what the original editor actually clicked.

Stage two is verified replay: execute that storyboard in a toolchain aligned with the deployment environment, with every real tool return updating the state. Stage three is observe, reflect, repair: when replay exposes a defect, apply a limited number of local fixes.

Stage four turns those executions into training examples. Missing arguments, wrong ordering and failed deliveries are kept as they happened, not rewritten as successes. The result is one hundred and thirty-four thousand seven hundred and seventy-one examples: fifteen thousand for the orchestrator, about eighty-eight thousand for the generator, and about thirty-one thousand for the critic.

Training happens in three phases on a Qwen3.5 9B backbone with LoRA. Phase one is supervised fine-tuning, followed by rejection-sampled fine-tuning: the student runs tasks itself, and when it fails, the state is rolled back to just before the error and a teacher model finishes the job. Only trajectories that pass the runtime checks are kept for training.

Phase two is an on-policy distillation method called Guided-OPD, with Qwen3.5 397B as the teacher. At each turn, a coin flip decides whether the teacher or the student acts. The teacher's share starts at zero point eight and decays to zero, so training ends with the student fully in control.

Phase three is reinforcement learning, a variant the authors call RCF-DAPO. The core idea is to compare candidates only within the same role and the same production context. For the orchestrator, the state is restored to the same moment, several different next actions are sampled and each is played forward, and the rewards are compared.

### Results

Evaluation uses one hundred tasks held out from training, with five runs per system. The authors define five metrics: whether the narration content stays faithful to the script, whether visuals match what's being said, whether the shot plan was actually realized, whether quality holds up late in a long video, and whether a video gets delivered at all. The overall score is the plain average of the five.

The first comparison keeps the tools and environment fixed and swaps only the model. In Table 1, WeVisCut scores ninety point five overall, sixteen points above its untrained Qwen3.5 9B starting point at seventy-four point five, with gains on all five metrics.

In that same environment, Claude Opus 5 scores eighty-nine point nine, and Gemini 3.1 Pro eighty-four point six. WeVisCut's mean is higher than Opus 5 on four metrics and slightly lower on visual-narration match, and no single metric differs by more than one point three.

The difference is cost. Per minute of delivered video, WeVisCut generates two thousand three hundred and seventy-seven tokens. Opus 5 generates seventy-eight thousand seven hundred and sixty-three. That's roughly one thirty-third as many.

Table 3 breaks down the training phases. Supervised fine-tuning lifts the delivery rate from seventy point four to eighty-seven point six, the biggest single step on that metric. Rejection sampling and distillation add more, and the final reinforcement learning stage takes the overall score from eighty-four point nine to ninety point five.

### Comparison

The second comparison pits WeVisCut against complete editing systems, each running its own tools and workflow on the same tasks. In Table 2, WeVisCut scores ninety point five, Crayotter eighty-four point nine, and VideoAgent eighty-two point five.

The cost gap is wider. Per minute of output, WeVisCut takes two point one minutes and an estimated nine cents. Crayotter takes eight point six minutes and ninety-seven cents, and VideoAgent ten point four minutes and fifty-eight cents. The paper notes these are costs of the whole workflow, not of the model alone.

For human evaluation, three raters scored videos from the three systems blind, on a one to five scale. WeVisCut's overall preference is four point five six, and the other two sit around three and a half. The authors are clear that these are descriptive means and don't establish statistical significance.

My read is that the "matches Opus 5" claim deserves a discount. Ninety point five versus eighty-nine point nine is a gap of zero point six, while most of WeVisCut's individual metrics vary by around two points across runs, and the overall score comes with no uncertainty at all. Also, the visual-narration metric is graded by Claude Opus 5 as the judge, the same model it's being compared against.

My read is that the claim that really holds up is cost. In the same environment, a nine-billion-parameter model reached scores close to a top closed model with about a thirty-third of the tokens. One more caveat: the test tasks are held-out projects from the same Bilibili collection as the training data. How it does on a different kind of video, the paper doesn't test.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, the highlight-shot animations depend on the existing template library, so an unusual visual style can only be approximated by the closest template.

Second, the data can't cover every domain or editing convention, and verified replay only guarantees executability inside their registered runtime. Third, long-video quality depends on how stable the footage retrieval is.

Fourth, the visual-narration metric relies on a vision-language model as judge. The authors isolate its evidence and calibrate it, but acknowledge that this can't remove judge bias.

### Future work

Looking ahead, the authors suggest generating templates automatically to grow the library, and plugging in a trained video generation model to depend less on retrieval. The first page links the code and the model weights.

My read is that the most reusable idea here isn't about editing at all. It's how to manufacture training data for an agent: reconstruct intent from finished products, replay it in a real environment, and keep the failures and repairs. That recipe could carry over to any field where you have finished outputs but no record of how they were made.
