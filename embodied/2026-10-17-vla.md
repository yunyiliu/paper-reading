## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇浙江大学的论文，名字叫 VLA-Delta，Delta 就是数学里表示变化量的那个符号。

它想解决的问题是，机器人模型常常照着记忆里的动作走，却没真正看懂，眼前的东西正在怎么变。

### 问题

先说 VLA，也就是视觉、语言、动作模型。它建立在预训练好的视觉语言大模型上，看画面、听指令，直接输出机器人的动作。

问题是，它们经常犯一些很低级的错。比如还没碰到东西，就先把夹爪合上了，或者明明没抓住，还继续往要放的位置走。

作者的解释是，大多数 VLA 直接从观察映射到示范里的动作，中间状态怎么变，没有专门的监督。于是模型容易记住哪种画面对应哪个动作，像在回放一条背下来的轨迹。

### 以前的方法

以前大概有三类补救办法。这张图把它们和这篇论文放在一起比较。

第一类是推理型 VLA，先用文字写出计划，比如先对准苹果，再合上夹爪。但它讲的是下一步该做什么，没有讲机器人和物体的物理状态，具体怎么变。

第二类是世界动作模型，简称 WAM，直接生成未来的画面。细节多了，但重建画面主要保证长得像，分不出哪些变化对任务是必要的，生成图片还有额外的推理开销。

第三类是把未来预测挪到隐空间，省掉画图的成本，但预测的仍然只是未来的视觉状态。

作者的总结是，这些方法要么监督高层的任务语义，要么监督未来的画面外观，很少直接监督物理状态的变化本身，比如物体的位姿、空间关系、机器人和物体的接触，怎么从现在变到未来。

### 核心想法

核心想法就一句话，把现在和未来之间的物理变化，直接当成学习目标。

作者认为，这种变化既要跟任务意图挂钩，又要落在具体的视觉信息上。光有任务语义，说不清物体怎么动，光有画面，又说不清哪些变化对任务重要。

还有一个关键设计，这些额外的监督只在预训练时使用。到了部署的时候，模型直接从当前画面生成动作，不用把中间的东西解码出来。

### 方法

先看整体结构。它沿用 GR00T N1 的设计，前面是视觉语言模型，用的是 Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct，后面是一个 9 层的 Diffusion Transformer，负责生成动作，论文叫它动作专家。

这张图是整体架构。作者在视觉语言模型的输入里，加了一组可以学习的查询向量，叫物理状态差分查询。模型读完画面和指令，取这组查询位置的最后一层输出，作为对变化的表示。

然后用三种监督信号去塑造这个表示，分两条路，一条是直接预测，一条是让模型逐字生成文字。

第一种叫结构化转移槽。它把任务相关的状态分成九类，任务进度、任务阶段、目标位置、夹爪位置、运动和相对位移、可以交互的位置、夹爪开合的变化、接触的变化，还有障碍物风险。

每一类交给一部分查询负责，后面接一个很轻的预测头。作者说，这能让这些查询形成一种软性的分工。

这张图是一个例子，把苹果放进容器。从靠近、对准、操作、松开到收回，每个时刻都标了进度、阶段、夹爪状态和接触状态。

第二种是稠密光流，就是同一个视角下，现在和之后的画面之间，每个像素移动了多少。它补的是细粒度的运动细节，比如夹爪怎么靠近，局部怎么对齐。

但光流本身分不出，哪些运动跟任务有关，所以要和另外两种信号一起用。

第三种叫未来状态差分文本。用一段连贯的自然语言，描述从现在到未来，任务进度怎么推进，空间怎么变，接触怎么变，要满足什么约束。

比如这一段，写的是当前阶段在把苹果运向容器，任务进度从 0.40 到 0.53，夹爪要一直夹紧，不能让苹果转动、打滑或者掉下去。

这里有个细节。生成这段文字的时候，文字只能看到查询向量，看不到原始的画面和指令。所以信息必须先压进查询里，才能写出这段描述。

最后，动作专家通过交叉注意力读取这组变化表示，再结合机器人自身的状态，用 flow matching，把一段随机噪声变成一段连续的动作。

训练分两段。预训练时，三种监督和动作一起学。到下游任务微调时，只用动作这一个目标，也就不需要给下游数据做额外的标注。

数据方面，作者从 DROID、MolmoAct 和 RH20T 三个数据集里挑出示范，一共大约 484 小时，2400 万帧。

标注靠一条自动流水线，用 Qwen3.8、SAM 3、UniDepth V2 和 MegaFlow，分别做阶段和语义标注、目标框、深度和光流，再用 GPT-5.6 复查难的样本，最后人工抽查。

标注质量方面，作者抽了 300 对样本做审核，各项的合格率在百分之 93 到百分之 99.33 之间。不过目标和夹爪的框，参考答案本身也是 GPT-5.6 画的，论文也说明了，这不算独立的人工标准答案。

### 实验和效果

实验在两个仿真基准和一台真实机器人上做。仿真是 LIBERO 和 RoboTwin 2.0，另外用 LIBERO-Plus 测泛化。真机是 AgileX PiPER，一台 6 自由度的双臂机器人。

还有一点要先说清楚，它的视觉语言模型，是用 ZR-0 的基础权重初始化的。

先看 LIBERO，表 1。VLA-Delta 平均成功率百分之 98.4，是所有对比方法里最高的。第二名是 ZR-0，97.8，Pi 0.5 是 96.9。

再看 LIBERO-Plus，有四种扰动，换说法、换光照、换背景、换布局。最突出的是换说法，VLA-Delta 是 89.0，比 Pi 0.5 的 82.6 高 6.4 个百分点。作者认为，这和差分文本提供的语义信息有关。

但在另外三种扰动下，它都不是第一。这张图里，光照、背景和布局，Pi 0.5 分别是 96.7、96.8 和 86.2，都比 VLA-Delta 的 95.1、94.8 和 83.2 高。

RoboTwin 2.0 是双臂任务，一共 50 个，分干净和随机两种设置。VLA-Delta 的平均成功率是 90.06 和 89.74，两种设置都是第一，比最强的基线 ZR-0 分别高 1.36 和 1.76 个百分点。

作者特别提到两个任务，按大小排三个积木，和把杯子挂到架子上，这两个都要多步操作，前一步的结果会影响下一步。在干净设置下，VLA-Delta 在这两个任务上是所有方法里最高的。不过挂杯子的成功率也只有百分之 40。

真机实验，看这张图。一共五个任务，避障、长流程、精细操作、空间推理，还有换了桌布之后的泛化，每个任务跑 40 次，一共 200 次。

五个任务的平均成功率是百分之 68.0，对比的是 Pi 0、Pi 0.5 和 ZR-0。它在空间推理和精细操作上最高，在长流程和泛化上排第二。

避障任务是把胡萝卜放到架子上，不能碰到杯子。VLA-Delta 的成功率是百分之 42.5，也是对比方法里最高的，但还不到一半。作者认为，这和差分文本里对障碍和约束的描述是一致的。

### 和以前方法的对比

消融实验在表 3，都在 LIBERO 上做。只用稠密的视觉语言特征，是 95.70，换成差分查询，是 96.25。

一个重要的对照是，只用动作监督做同样的预训练。作者用它来区分，涨分是来自预训练数据，还是来自额外的监督。结果提升很小，带查询的版本只从 96.25 到 96.45。

在这个基础上，单独加差分文本是 97.20，单独加结构化槽是 96.90，单独加光流是 97.35，三种全加是 98.40。

作者还把槽查询的内部表示画了出来。有结构化监督时，七组查询各自聚成清楚的团，而且在下游只用动作微调之后，这个结构还在。

我的判断是，这篇最有说服力的，不是榜单第一，而是这组消融。同样的数据，只用动作监督预训练几乎不涨，加上物理变化的监督才涨，这比较直接地支持了它的核心想法。

我的判断是，也要看到，它的视觉语言模型是从 ZR-0 初始化的，而 ZR-0 正是 LIBERO 和 RoboTwin 上最强的基线。它领先 ZR-0 的幅度，LIBERO 上 0.6 个百分点，RoboTwin 上不到 2 个百分点，并不大。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限一节，下面几条，是从论文自己的数据里能看到的。

第一，泛化并不是全面领先。LIBERO-Plus 的四种扰动里，有三种不如 Pi 0.5。

第二，真机的绝对成功率还不高。平均百分之 68.0，避障只有百分之 42.5，而且每个任务只测了 40 次。

第三，标注主要靠模型自动生成，质量审核只抽了 300 对，部分参考答案也来自 GPT-5.6。

第四，作者说部署时没有额外的推理开销，这是从结构上说的，因为不用解码中间目标，论文里没有给出推理速度的实测。

### 未来方向

论文也没有专门写未来方向。作者在结论里说，显式地学习物理状态的变化，能给下游的连续动作生成，打下一个有效的表示基础。代码已经在 GitHub 公开。

我的判断是，这篇的价值，在于给让 VLA 理解物理变化，找了一个便宜的做法。不生成未来画面，只在预训练时监督变化，部署的结构不变。接下来值得看的是，这种预训练能不能在更多真机任务、更多种机器人上复现。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Zhejiang University, and it's called VLA-Delta, Delta as in the symbol for change.

The problem it goes after: robot models that replay the motions they memorized, without really tracking how the scene in front of them is changing.

### Problem

Quick background. A VLA, or vision-language-action model, is built on a pretrained vision-language model. It looks at camera images, reads an instruction, and outputs robot actions directly.

The trouble is that these models make surprisingly basic mistakes. A policy may close its gripper before it reaches the object, or keep heading to the drop-off spot after the grasp has already failed.

The authors' explanation is that most VLAs map observations straight to the actions in the demonstrations, with no explicit supervision of how the robot and the objects should change along the way. So the model learns which picture goes with which action, and ends up looking like it's replaying a trajectory it memorized.

### Prior work

There have been roughly three kinds of fixes. This figure puts them side by side with the new approach.

The first is reasoning VLAs, which write out a plan in words first: align with the apple, then close the gripper. But that describes what to do next, not how the physical state of the robot and the objects actually changes.

The second is world action models, or WAMs, which generate future images. You get more visual detail, but reconstructing images mostly rewards getting the appearance right. It doesn't single out which changes the task actually needs, and generating images adds inference cost.

The third moves that future prediction into a latent space. That saves the cost of drawing pictures, but what's being predicted is still just the future visual state.

The authors' summary: these methods supervise either high-level task meaning or future appearance. They rarely supervise the physical transition itself, meaning how object poses, spatial relations, and robot-object contact change from now to the near future.

### Key idea

The key idea fits in one sentence. Make the physical change between the current state and a future state an explicit learning target.

The authors argue that this change has to be tied to the task's intent and grounded in what the camera sees. Task meaning alone can't say how objects move, and appearance alone can't say which changes matter.

And there's one more important choice. All this extra supervision is used only during pretraining. At deployment, the model generates actions straight from the current observation, without decoding any of the intermediate targets.

### Method

Start with the overall structure. It follows the GR00T N1 design: a vision-language backbone, here Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, followed by a nine-layer diffusion transformer that generates actions, which the paper calls the action expert.

Here's the architecture. The authors add a set of learnable query vectors to the backbone's input, called Physical State Difference Queries. After the model reads the images and the instruction, the final-layer outputs at those query positions become the representation of what's about to change.

That representation is then shaped by three supervision signals, through two pathways: direct prediction, and autoregressive generation, where the model writes text one token at a time.

The first signal is Structured Transition Slots. Task-relevant state is split into nine categories: task progress, task phase, target position, gripper position, motion and relative displacement, interactable position, gripper open and close transitions, contact transitions, and obstacle risk.

Each category is assigned a subset of the queries, with a lightweight prediction head on top. The authors say this nudges the queries into a soft division of labor.

This figure shows an example: putting an apple into a container. From approach and align through manipulate, release and retract, every step is labeled with progress, phase, gripper state and contact state.

The second signal is dense optical flow, meaning how far each pixel moves between the current frame and a later frame from the same camera. It fills in fine-grained motion detail, like how the gripper approaches and how things line up locally.

But optical flow by itself can't tell task-relevant motion from any other motion in the image, so it's used together with the other two signals.

The third signal is Future-State Difference Text. It's a coherent natural-language description of how, between now and the future, task progress advances, positions change, contact changes, and which constraints must hold.

For example, this passage says the current phase is carrying the apple toward the container, task progress goes from zero point four zero to zero point five three, and the gripper has to keep a secure grip, with no rotation, slipping or dropping.

Here's a subtle detail. When the model generates that text, the text can only attend to the query vectors, not to the raw images or the instruction. So the information has to pass through the queries before it can show up in the description.

Finally, the action expert reads that transition representation through cross-attention, combines it with the robot's own state readings, and uses flow matching to turn random noise into a continuous chunk of actions.

Training happens in two stages. In pretraining, the three signals are learned jointly with actions. When fine-tuning on a downstream task, only the action objective is used, so the downstream data needs no extra annotation.

For data, the authors select demonstrations from three datasets, DROID, MolmoAct and RH20T, about four hundred eighty-four hours and twenty-four million frames in total.

Labeling is done by an automated pipeline. Qwen3.8 handles stages and semantic labels, SAM 3 the bounding boxes, UniDepth V2 depth, and MegaFlow optical flow. GPT-5.6 reviews the hard cases, and people audit a sample at the end.

On label quality, the authors audited three hundred sample pairs, and each field passed at rates between ninety-three and ninety-nine point three three percent. But for the target and gripper boxes, the reference itself was drawn by GPT-5.6, and the paper notes that this isn't independent human ground truth.

### Results

Experiments cover two simulation benchmarks and a real robot. In simulation, it's LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0, plus LIBERO-Plus for generalization. The real robot is an AgileX PiPER, a six-degree-of-freedom dual-arm platform.

One thing to flag up front: the vision-language backbone is initialized from the base weights of ZR-0.

First, LIBERO, in Table 1. VLA-Delta averages a ninety-eight point four percent success rate, the highest of all the compared methods. Second is ZR-0 at ninety-seven point eight, and pi zero point five is at ninety-six point nine.

Next, LIBERO-Plus, with four kinds of perturbation: reworded instructions, lighting, background, and layout. The standout is language, where VLA-Delta scores eighty-nine point zero against eighty-two point six for pi zero point five, a gap of six point four points. The authors credit the semantic information in the difference text.

But on the other three perturbations, it doesn't come first. In this chart, pi zero point five scores ninety-six point seven, ninety-six point eight and eighty-six point two on lighting, background and layout, all above VLA-Delta's ninety-five point one, ninety-four point eight and eighty-three point two.

RoboTwin 2.0 is a set of fifty dual-arm tasks, with a clean setting and a randomized one. VLA-Delta averages ninety point zero six and eighty-nine point seven four, first in both, ahead of the strongest baseline, ZR-0, by one point three six and one point seven six points.

The authors single out two tasks: ranking three blocks by size, and hanging a mug on a rack. Both take several steps, where how one step turns out affects the next. In the clean setting, VLA-Delta has the highest success on both. Still, on mug hanging that's only forty percent.

Now the real robot, in this figure. Five tasks: obstacle avoidance, a long-horizon task, fine-grained manipulation, spatial reasoning, and generalization after the tablecloth is changed. Each task gets forty trials, two hundred in all.

Across the five tasks, the average success rate is sixty-eight point zero percent, compared against pi zero, pi zero point five and ZR-0. It's best on spatial reasoning and fine-grained manipulation, and second on the long-horizon and generalization tasks.

The obstacle task is placing carrots on a rack without touching the cups. VLA-Delta succeeds forty-two point five percent of the time, again the best of the compared methods, but still under half. The authors say this is consistent with the constraint descriptions in the difference text.

### Comparison

The ablations are in Table 3, all on LIBERO. Using dense vision-language features alone gives ninety-five point seven zero. Switching to difference queries gives ninety-six point two five.

One important control is pretraining on the same data with action supervision only. The authors use it to separate the effect of the pretraining data from the effect of the extra supervision. The gain is small: the query version goes from ninety-six point two five to ninety-six point four five.

On top of that, adding difference text alone gives ninety-seven point two zero, slots alone ninety-six point nine zero, optical flow alone ninety-seven point three five, and all three together ninety-eight point four zero.

The authors also plot the internal representations of the slot queries. With structured supervision, the seven query groups form tight, separate clusters, and that structure survives downstream fine-tuning that uses actions only.

My read is that the most convincing part of this paper isn't the leaderboard, it's this ablation. On the same data, action-only pretraining barely helps, and the gains arrive only with the physical-change supervision. That's fairly direct support for the core idea.

My read is also that the context matters. The backbone starts from ZR-0, which is the strongest baseline on both LIBERO and RoboTwin. The margin over ZR-0 is zero point six points on LIBERO and under two points on RoboTwin, which isn't large.

### Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations section, so the points below come from its own numbers.

First, generalization isn't a clean sweep. On three of the four LIBERO-Plus perturbations, it trails pi zero point five.

Second, absolute success on the real robot is still modest. Sixty-eight percent on average, forty-two point five percent on obstacle avoidance, and each task was run only forty times.

Third, the labels are mostly generated by models, the quality audit covered three hundred pairs, and some of the audit's references came from GPT-5.6 as well.

Fourth, the claim of no extra inference overhead is an architectural argument, since nothing intermediate is decoded. The paper doesn't report measured inference speed.

### Future work

The paper doesn't have a future-work section either. In the conclusion, the authors say that explicitly learning physical state changes gives downstream continuous action generation an effective representational foundation. The code is already public on GitHub.

My read is that the value here is a cheap way to make VLAs pay attention to physical change: no future images, supervision only in pretraining, and the same structure at deployment. What I'd watch next is whether this kind of pretraining holds up across more real-robot tasks and more kinds of robots.
