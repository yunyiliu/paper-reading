## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇 MBZUAI，也就是穆罕默德本扎耶德人工智能大学，和 Motphys 的论文，名字叫 DexDuet，讲的是怎么从人的一次演示里，让机器人学会双手的灵巧操作。

它抓住的问题很具体，机器人把东西移到了正确的位置，不代表它像人一样拿住了东西。

### 问题

先说背景。人用两只手干活很自然，比如一只手拿着瓶子，另一只手拧开盖子。从人的演示里学这种技能，是一个很自然的思路。

但人手和机器人的手，大小、关节结构、驱动方式都不一样，人的演示没法直接在机器人上回放。

现在常见的做法，是先把人手的动作映射到机器人手上，这一步叫 retargeting，然后在仿真里训练一个策略，让物体沿着人演示的轨迹走。

问题就出在这里。训练目标主要是追踪物体轨迹，物体走对了就算好。但即使物体轨迹跟得很准，机器人的手也可能握在物体的错误部位，或者手指在物体上打滑，操作就不稳。

作者的说法是，忠实地复现双手操作，不光要复现物体往哪走，还要保留手是怎么和物体接触的。

### 以前的方法

以前的方法，在三个地方有短板。第一是双手怎么协调。要么用一个统一的策略同时控制两只手，要么给每只手各训一个独立的策略。

用一个策略，一只手的学习和另一只手绑在一起。各训各的，一只手学到的东西，另一只手用不上。

第二是接触。有些方法也加了和接触有关的奖励，但都是逐帧比较接触点，不管这个接触在时间上是不是一直固定在物体同一个地方。所以机器人还是可以换一只手去拿，或者手指一路滑着，把物体带到位。

第三是难的阶段。为了让训练能起步，以前的方法会在训练初期帮物体一把，用一个叫虚拟物体控制器的东西，简称 VOC，把物体往演示的轨迹上拉，然后再慢慢撤掉这个帮助。

问题是，这个帮助在整段演示上是统一往下撤的。只要中间有一段特别难，比如两只手之间交接物体，整个训练就会被卡住。

### 核心想法

DexDuet 的核心想法，是把目标从物体跟得准，改成物体跟得准，同时手要拿在人拿的地方。

作者把拿住定义得很具体。手碰到物体的某个部件，而且接触点在这个部件上不移动，才叫拿住。人手拿住哪个部件，对应的机器人手也要拿住同一个部件。

围绕这个目标，有三个设计，分别对应前面说的三个短板，双手协调、接触保持、难阶段的训练。这张总览图就是这三块。

### 方法

先看第一块，分解的单手策略。两只手各有一个策略，但它们共用同一个神经网络，每只手除了看自己的状态，还会看搭档那只手的状态。

作者说，这样每只手可以扮演自己的角色，适应搭档的动作，同时共享学到的经验。

训练用的是强化学习里常用的 PPO 算法。PPO 会限制每次更新的幅度，如果用统一策略，一只手变化太大，这条样本对两只手都会被截断，另一只手也学不到了。所以 DexDuet 对每只手分别做截断。

第二块是两个交互奖励。在原有的物体追踪和动作模仿奖励之上，再加两项，都是把机器人手和人手放在物体的坐标系里来比。

第一项叫手部图奖励。在手上连出一张图，包括掌心朝向、手掌方向、每根手指从根部到指尖的方向，再比较指尖之间张开的距离，让机器人的手在物体周围的朝向和张开程度，都和人手一致。

第二项叫手物接触奖励。把手分成手掌、拇指和其余每根手指几个区域，对每个区域，比较它离物体部件有多远，以及它最靠近物体上的哪个点。

这样在接触发生之前，奖励就会引导手往正确的部位靠。人手没有碰的地方，机器人碰了，奖励就会降低。

第三块叫辅助参考状态初始化，简称 A-RSI，是一套课程学习的办法。它把演示切成等长的几段，每一段有自己的辅助强度。

从某一段开始的训练回合，如果成功率高，这一段的辅助就降下来，如果失败多，辅助就加回去。另外还有一个随训练逐步降到零的上限，保证最后的策略完全不靠辅助。

它还有两个配套做法。一是让回合从策略自己曾经到达过的状态开始，因为直接用 retargeting 得到的参考状态，手指常常和物体隔着空隙，或者穿进物体里，物理上不成立。

二是更多地从失败率高的段开始练，难的那段前面几段也会多练一些。这样难的阶段保住了辅助，也能多练几次，其余部分的训练不会被它拖住。

### 实验和效果

实验部分。作者在四个基准上测试，三个灵巧手操作基准，加上一个人形机器人操作挑战，一共涉及三个仿真器、五种机器人。每个基准都用它自己 baseline 的仿真器。

对比的 baseline 分别是 DexMachina、SPIDER、ManipTrans，以及人形挑战 V2D 里的 CHORD。DexMachina 用的是官方发布的模型，其余几个是作者按官方的代码或流程，重跑或者重新训练的。

指标有两类。一类看物体跟得准不准，主要是 ADD-AUC，越高越好。另一类是作者新提出的 Contact-AUC，看在人手拿住物体的那些时间窗里，机器人手的接触点漂移有多小，也是越高越好。

结果在表 1。在每个基准、每种机器人手上，DexDuet 的物体追踪都比对应的 baseline 好，ADD-AUC 高出 11 到 41 个点。

举个例子，在 DexMachina 基准上用 Inspire 手，ADD-AUC 从 52.5 提到 88.7，Contact-AUC 从 33.6 提到 79.6。

人形机器人这边，是 G1 人形机器人加 Dex3 手。论文报告，DexDuet 的 ADD-AUC 是 77.4，用 PPO 重新训练的 CHORD 是 39.8，几乎翻倍。Contact-AUC 是 72.6 比 16.0。

真机部分。作者把仿真里学到的动作，回放到两套真机上。两台 Franka 机械臂配 Allegro 手，举着一个瓶子调整它的朝向，把游戏手柄放到方块上。宇树 G1 人形机器人配 Inspire 手，把箱子放到地上，放下袋子，边走边推办公椅。

要注意，这里是开环回放，也就是把仿真里的动作序列直接播放出来，不看实时的物体状态。所以它说明的是动作能在真机上执行，不是闭环的真机控制。

### 和以前方法的对比

但这篇真正想证明的是接触。光看平均分还不够，因为物体跟得更准，接触分也可能跟着变高。

所以作者在 DexMachina 基准上，把 DexDuet 和 DexMachina 物体追踪水平差不多的两次运行配成对，ADD-AUC 相差不超过 3 个点，再比接触。在 122 对里，DexDuet 有 117 对接触保持得更好，Contact-AUC 的中位数高出 17.9 个点。

这张图能直观看到差别。上面一行是人手，中间是 baseline，下面是 DexDuet，彩色的斑块是手碰到物体的位置。作者说，baseline 的手常常离开人手拿的部位，或者手指在上面打滑。

作者还做了一个主观评测，11 名评测者看 24 对追踪水平相当、但接触分至少差 10 个点的运行，在 264 次判断里，有 251 次认为接触分高的那个，拿东西更像人。

接触保持得好，还有一个实际的好处。演示结束后，让策略继续保持 3 秒，看物体是否一直在最终位置 5 厘米以内。DexDuet 做到的比例是百分之 32.1，DexMachina 是百分之 15.6。

消融实验在表 3，回答每个部件各贡献了什么。去掉单手策略，换回统一策略，21 次配对运行里，有 20 次追踪变差。

把 A-RSI 换成以前那种统一的辅助课程，Contact-AUC 降低 6.3 个点，训练还要多花百分之 20 的更新次数。

去掉任意一个交互奖励，接触分掉得比追踪分多得多。比如去掉手物接触奖励，Contact-AUC 降 15.1 个点，ADD-AUC 只降 3.3 个点。两个奖励都去掉，21 次运行里有 6 次失败。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把怎么拿住，变成了一个可以量化、可以优化的目标。以前大家比的是物体跟得准不准，现在多了一个接触有没有保持住的维度，而且在追踪水平相同的时候，差距依然很明显。

我的判断是，也要看到比较的边界。四个基准各用各的仿真器和 baseline，作者自己也写了，数字不能跨基准比。另外 Contact-AUC 是作者新提出的指标，接触上的优势主要是用这把新尺子量出来的，好在主观评测给了一个独立的旁证。

### 局限

作者自己列了几条局限。第一，和 DexMachina、ManipTrans 一样，DexDuet 每段演示训练一个策略，在一块 GPU 上大约要一天，而且不能泛化到新的物体或者新的动作。

第二，策略观察的是仿真器里的物体状态，而且要求演示本身能提供准确的手和物体运动。

第三，真机上只是开环回放仿真里的结果。

### 未来方向

论文明确提到的下一步，是在真机上做闭环部署，让策略依靠估计出来的物体状态，而不是仿真器直接给的状态。

我的判断是，更关键的一步是泛化。一段演示一个策略，一个策略训一天，离真正好用的技能库还很远。我推测，接下来会有人尝试把这些单独的策略合成一个通用策略，不过论文本身没有做这件事。

我的判断是，即便如此，接触要保持住这个思路，以及 Contact-AUC 这样的指标，很可能会被后面做人手数据迁移的工作沿用，因为它衡量的，正是以前被忽略的那一部分。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from MBZUAI, the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, together with Motphys. It's called DexDuet, and it's about teaching two robot hands a dexterous skill from a single human demonstration.

The problem it targets is easy to miss. A robot can move an object exactly where it should go, and still not be holding it the way a person would.

### Problem

Some background. People use both hands all the time, like holding a bottle in one hand while twisting off the cap with the other. Learning those skills from human demonstrations is an obvious idea.

But robot hands differ from human hands in size, in joint structure, and in how they're actuated. You can't just replay a human demonstration on a robot.

The usual fix is to map the human hand motion onto the robot hand first, a step called retargeting, and then train a policy in simulation that moves the object along the demonstrated path.

And that's where things go wrong. The training goal is mostly tracking the object's trajectory: if the object ends up in the right place, the policy looks good. But even with accurate tracking, the robot's hand can grab the wrong part of the object, or its fingers can slide along it, and the manipulation becomes unstable.

The authors' point is that faithfully reproducing a two-handed task means reproducing not only where the object goes, but also how the hands touch it.

### Prior work

Earlier methods fall short in three places. The first is coordination. They either drive both hands with one unified policy, or train a separate, independent policy for each hand.

With one policy, each hand's learning is tied to the other's. With separate policies, nothing one hand learns is shared with the other.

The second is contact. Some methods do add contact-related rewards, but they compare contacts frame by frame, and none of them asks whether a contact stays fixed on the object over time. So a robot can still switch hands, or slide its fingers, and carry the object along anyway.

The third is the hard phases. To get training off the ground, earlier methods give the object a helping hand early on, using what's called a virtual object controller, or VOC, which pulls the object toward its demonstrated path. That help is then gradually withdrawn.

The catch is that the help is withdrawn uniformly across the whole demonstration. So if one stretch in the middle is really hard, like handing the object from one hand to the other, the entire training run stalls.

### Key idea

DexDuet's key idea is to change the goal from "track the object" to "track the object, and hold it where the human held it."

The authors define holding precisely. A hand holds a part when it touches that part and its contact points don't move on it. Whenever a human hand holds a part, the matching robot hand has to hold that same part.

Three design choices serve that goal, one for each of the gaps above: coordinating the hands, preserving contact, and getting through hard phases. This overview figure shows all three.

### Method

First, the factorized per-hand policy. Each hand gets its own policy, but the two share a single neural network, and each hand observes its partner's state as well as its own.

According to the authors, this lets each hand play its own role, adapt to its partner, and still benefit from shared learning.

Training uses PPO, a standard reinforcement learning algorithm that limits how far each update can move the policy. With a unified policy, if one hand changes too much on a sample, that sample gets clipped for both hands, and the other hand stops learning from it. So DexDuet clips each hand separately.

Second, two interaction rewards. On top of the usual rewards for tracking the object and imitating the retargeted motion, they add two terms, and both compare the robot hand with the human hand relative to the object.

The first is the hand graph reward. It builds a small graph over the hand: the palm normal, the palm direction, and the direction of each finger from base to tip, plus the gaps between fingertips. The robot hand is rewarded for matching the human hand's orientation and opening around the object.

The second is the hand-object contact reward. Each hand is split into regions, the palm, the thumb, and each other finger. For every region, it compares how far that region is from each object part, and which point on the part it's closest to.

That means the reward steers the hand toward the right spot before contact even happens. And if the robot touches somewhere the human didn't, its reward goes down.

Third, assisted reference-state initialization, or A-RSI, which is a training curriculum. It cuts the demonstration into equal-length segments and gives each segment its own level of assistance.

If episodes starting in a segment mostly succeed, that segment's assistance goes down. If they fail, it goes back up. A ceiling that falls to zero over training guarantees the final policy runs with no assistance at all.

Two more pieces support this. One: episodes start from states the policy itself has already reached, because the reference states from retargeting often leave a gap between fingers and object, or push fingers inside it, which isn't physically valid.

Two: episodes start more often in segments with high failure rates, and the segments leading up to a hard one get extra practice too. So the hard phase keeps its assistance and gets more practice, and the rest of the demonstration trains without waiting for it.

### Results

On to the experiments. The authors test on four benchmarks: three dexterous manipulation benchmarks and one humanoid manipulation challenge, covering three simulators and five robot embodiments. Each benchmark runs in its own baseline's simulator.

The baselines are DexMachina, SPIDER, ManipTrans, and CHORD on the humanoid challenge, V2D. For DexMachina they evaluate the released checkpoints; the others are rerun or retrained with their official code or recipes.

There are two kinds of metrics. One measures how closely the object follows its path, mainly ADD-AUC, where higher is better. The other is a new metric the authors introduce, Contact-AUC, which measures how little the robot's contact points drift during the windows where the human hand is holding the object. Higher is better there too.

The results are in Table 1. On every benchmark and with every robot hand, DexDuet tracks the object better than the corresponding baseline, by eleven to forty-one points of ADD-AUC.

For example, on the DexMachina suite with Inspire hands, ADD-AUC goes from fifty-two point five to eighty-eight point seven, and Contact-AUC from thirty-three point six to seventy-nine point six.

On the humanoid side, the robot is a G1 humanoid with Dex3 hands. The paper reports an ADD-AUC of seventy-seven point four for DexDuet, against thirty-nine point eight for CHORD retrained with PPO, close to double. Contact-AUC is seventy-two point six against sixteen.

Then the real robots. The authors replay motions learned in simulation on two real setups. Two Franka arms with Allegro hands reorient a bottle and place a gamepad on a block. A Unitree G1 humanoid with Inspire hands lowers a box to the floor, puts down a bag, and pushes an office chair while walking.

One caveat: this is open-loop replay. The motion sequence from simulation is simply played back, without watching the real object's state. So it shows the motions can run on real hardware. It isn't closed-loop control on a real robot.

### Comparison

But the paper's real claim is about contact. Averages alone don't settle it, because better object tracking could lift the contact score on its own.

So on the DexMachina suite, the authors pair up DexDuet and DexMachina runs whose tracking is about equal, with ADD-AUC within three points, and compare contact only. Of a hundred and twenty-two pairs, DexDuet preserves contact better in a hundred and seventeen, with a median gain of seventeen point nine points of Contact-AUC.

This figure makes the difference visible. The top row is the human, the middle row is the baseline, and the bottom row is DexDuet, with colored patches marking where each hand touches the object. The authors note that the baseline's hand often leaves the part the human holds, or its fingers slide on it.

They also ran a perceptual study. Eleven raters looked at twenty-four pairs of runs that track equally well but differ by at least ten points of Contact-AUC, and in two hundred fifty-one of two hundred sixty-four judgments, they picked the run with the higher contact score as handling the object more like the human.

Preserving contact pays off in a practical way too. After the demonstration ends, the policy keeps acting for three seconds, and the test is whether the object stays within five centimeters of its final pose. DexDuet manages that thirty-two point one percent of the time, DexMachina fifteen point six percent.

The ablations are in Table 3, and they show what each part contributes. Swap the per-hand policy back to a unified one, and tracking gets worse in twenty of twenty-one paired runs.

Replace A-RSI with the older single-level assistance curriculum, and Contact-AUC drops six point three points, with twenty percent more updates needed before training stops.

Remove either interaction reward, and the contact score falls much more than the tracking score. Without the hand-object contact reward, for instance, Contact-AUC drops fifteen point one points while ADD-AUC drops only three point three. Remove both, and six of twenty-one runs fail.

My read is that the paper's main contribution is turning "how you hold it" into something you can measure and optimize. The field has mostly compared how well the object is tracked. This adds a second axis, whether contact is preserved, and the gap holds up even when tracking is equal.

My read is also that the comparison has limits. Each benchmark has its own simulator and baseline, and the authors themselves say the numbers aren't comparable across benchmarks. And Contact-AUC is the authors' own new metric, so the contact advantage is mostly measured with their own ruler. The perceptual study does give some independent support.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, like DexMachina and ManipTrans, DexDuet trains one policy per demonstration, which takes about a day on one GPU, and it doesn't generalize to new objects or new motions.

Second, the policy observes the simulator's object state, and the demonstration has to supply accurate hand and object motion.

Third, on the real robots, they only replay simulated rollouts open-loop.

### Future work

The next step the paper names is closed-loop deployment on real robots, with the policy relying on an estimated object state rather than the simulator's.

My read is that the bigger step is generalization. One policy per demonstration, at a day per policy, is a long way from a usable skill library. My guess is that someone will try to merge these individual policies into one general policy, though the paper itself doesn't attempt that.

My read is that, even so, the idea of preserving contact, and a metric like Contact-AUC, are likely to be picked up by later work on transferring human hand data, because they measure exactly the part that used to be ignored.
