## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇香港中文大学 MMLab 的论文，合作单位还有 NovaXBot，名字叫 CaPtain。它讲的是，怎么把一个小的开源视觉语言模型，训练成一个靠写代码来控制机械臂的机器人 Agent。

先说结果。基础模型是 Qwen3.5-4B，原本在 LIBERO 上一个任务都完成不了，经过监督微调和多轮强化学习之后，在留出的测试局上，成功率到了 73.8%。

### 问题

先解释一个词，code-as-policy，简称 CaP。意思是，机器人不直接输出关节动作，而是由语言模型看着摄像头画面，写一段调用机器人接口的 Python 代码，执行完读回结果，再写下一段。

在写代码、上网、调用工具这些任务上，语言模型 Agent 已经有一套通用的训练配方，先用监督微调学会接口，再用多轮强化学习把它练熟。作者的问题是，机器人 CaP 能不能也用这套配方，把一个小模型训练出来，而不是在测试时依赖前沿大模型。

### 以前的方法

现有的 CaP Agent，基本都绕开了训练。一类是直接给闭源前沿模型写提示词，不更新权重。论文指出，这类系统每一局都要花钱调用大模型，没法针对新的机器人微调，也没法在机器人本地运行。

另一类，把真正的操作交给现成的 VLA，也就是视觉语言动作模型，或者交给抓取、放置这样的高层技能。代码只决定调哪个技能，至于怎么抓、从哪个方向靠近，还是技能自己决定。论文认为，这样 Agent 的上限被技能的训练数据框住了，强化学习最多学会怎么排顺序。

而小的开源模型，直接拿来控制机器人，基本完全失败。Qwen3.5-4B 在作者的框架里，LIBERO 的任务一个都没完成。

### 核心想法

CaPtain 的思路有两点。第一，只给模型最底层的接口，不给抓取、放置这类技能，也不用 VLA，每一个操作决定都由模型自己写出来，这样整个操作策略都能被训练到。

第二，多轮强化学习有个难点。一局里有几十次请求，大部分只是看一看、量一量，真正的物理效果，往往要过几轮才显现，而结果只有最后一个成功或失败。作者利用了机器人的一个特点，训练是在仿真里进行的，仿真器知道物体的精确位置、抓没抓住，所以可以让一个评估器读这些模型看不到的信息，给每一次请求单独记功。

### 方法

先看执行框架，叫 CaPtain-Harness。模型唯一的工具，是一个持续存在的 Python 会话，变量在请求之间一直保留。机器人模块提供 22 个能力，分成感知、测量、运动、规划和状态几类，比如用 SAM 3 做分割，用 Molmo2 指点物体，还有一个运动命令，让机械臂末端走到一个指定的位姿。

每调用一次机器人接口，框架都会返回一张文字回执，说明物理上实际发生了什么，比如为什么停下、走了多远、峰值力多大、夹爪开合了多少。一局最多 60 次请求，模型调用 finish 来宣布完成。

第一阶段是监督微调。训练数据是让 GPT-6 Astra 等前沿编码 Agent 在同一个框架里干活，生成的合成轨迹，格式和小模型部署时看到的完全一样。按附录的表 5，SFT 数据一共大约 2.6 万行，8.61 亿个 token。

这里有个细节。作者发现，模型会偷懒，直接抄历史里出现过的像素坐标，不去看图。所以训练时，以 0.2 的概率把历史里的坐标遮掉，逼它根据当前画面来定位。

第二阶段是强化学习，核心是仿真评估器，也就是 critic。每次请求之前，仿真器记录一份模型看不到的状态快照，从中算出 36 个特征，包括末端和夹爪的状态、目标完成了几个、各种距离、物体抬起了多高等等。critic 是五个小型 MLP 的集成，用来预测这一局最后成功的概率。

每一次请求的功劳，就是它让预测的成功率变化了多少。计算时请求序号保持不变，所以光是时间流逝，不会产生功劳。只看不动的请求，物理状态没变，功劳接近零，功劳就集中到了抓取和放置这些动作上。

奖励也区分了部署时真正在乎的结果。成功并且主动宣布完成，得 1 分，成功但没宣布，只得 0.25 分，失败了却宣布完成，扣 0.3 分。

训练时每次更新，累积 1024 局的梯度。作者发现，不累积的话，前后两次更新几乎互相正交。

critic 只在训练时用，测试时去掉，模型只靠摄像头画面和回执做决定。

### 实验和效果

训练只用 LIBERO 的 40 个原始任务。测试用官方留出的初始状态，一共 240 局，再加三个扰动测试集，LIBERO-PRO、LIBERO-Plus 和 LIBERO-MAX，这些扰动在训练里从来没出现过。

结果在表 1。LIBERO 上，基础模型是 0，SFT 之后是 55.4%，强化学习之后是 73.8%。要求模型自己宣布完成的严格成功率，从 52.1% 升到 67.5%。

强化学习的提升，在扰动集上也保持住了，幅度在 17.6 到 21.2 个百分点之间。LIBERO-PRO 三个短任务套件的平均，到了 78.5%。LIBERO-Plus 的七个扰动维度，全部有显著提升。

跨基准迁移在表 3，这些都没有用目标基准的数据训练。SimplerEnv 的 WidowX 任务上，成功率 83.3%。CaP-Bench 上是 75.0 分，论文报告，高于表里的 Gemini-3-Pro、GPT-5.2 和 Claude Opus 4.5。

真机上，作者用一个很粗糙的仿真孪生，把模型适配到 Astrabot XR1 机械臂，没有用任何真实数据。论文展示了两段真机过程，比如把天空颜色的方块，放进不是圆形的容器里，但没有给出真机的成功率。

迁移也有明显短板。需要双臂配合的 RoboTwin 2.0，成功率只有 22.6%，换了机械臂的 RoboDojo，只有 0.8%。

成本方面，作者估算每局的策略计算大约 0.03 到 0.06 美元，不含感知服务，而已发表的代码 Agent，报告的是每局 0.18 美元到 22 美元。

### 和以前方法的对比

表 2 把它和已发表的系统，放在 LIBERO-PRO 上比较。三个套件平均 78.5%，高于用 Gemini 3.7 Flash 的 K1 的 77.2%、WAA 的 75.6%，以及用 Claude Opus 4.6 的 ASPIRE 的 71.7%。在同样开源、不调用 VLA 的 Agent 里，也高于 27B 的 SPARK 的 66.8%。

但它明显低于最强的几个系统。用 Claude Opus 5 的 OpenRUA 是 91.8%，Claude Opus 4.7 加 π0.5 的 Harness VLA 是 87.7%，训练规划器配合 VLA 的 AgenticRoboRL 是 81.3%。

还要注意，这些基线数字直接取自各自的论文。附录列出了协议上的差别，有的允许更多回合，有的在测试时用了特权几何信息，或者 oracle 信号。

作者还比较了强化学习本身的收益。在 LIBERO-PRO 四个套件上，CaPtain 的强化学习带来 19.4 个百分点，AgenticRoboRL 在冻结的 VLA 外面做强化学习，是 5.9 个百分点。不过作者也说，两者的起点不同。

对 critic 的分析，用的是更早一个 9B 策略的离线数据。成功的抓取，功劳的中位数是正的 0.058，纯感知请求接近零。总功劳里，65% 给了运动，抓取和松开各 14%，感知只有 1.3%。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的，不是某一个分数，而是说明了机器人操作也能套用语言 Agent 的 SFT 加 RL 配方，而且仿真里的特权状态，正好补上了多轮强化学习里最难的记功问题。

我的判断是，也要看到，它离最强的系统还差十几个百分点，和外部系统的比较是跨论文、跨协议的。另外，挑选最终检查点时参考的评测，用到了 seed 11 的官方初始状态，它们也在 LIBERO 的测试局里。

### 局限

作者自己列了三条局限。第一，强化学习只在 LIBERO 的 40 个单臂任务上训练，需要双臂同时配合，或者在陌生机械臂上工作范围很紧的任务，还没有解决。

第二，critic 依赖仿真器的精确状态。作者测过，物体位置加 2 厘米噪声，和原来功劳的相关性降到 0.49，加 5 厘米降到 0.36，换成模型自己的感知，只有 0.21。所以直接在真机上做强化学习，还需要额外的状态估计。

第三，动作空间受限于这 22 个能力，和 SFT 数据里出现过的行为。需要持续用力的任务，比如 CaP-Bench 里要一直往下压的擦拭，现有的位置控制接口表达不了。

附录里还记了一个现象。强化学习后期，模型完成任务之后，不停地反复检查，不肯宣布完成，严格成功率从大约 60% 掉到 44%，所以作者选用了第 300 轮的检查点。

### 未来方向

作者提到的方向是，把 critic 扩展到判断什么时候该结束、双臂协调，以及多种机械臂的工作空间，以此缩小和前沿系统的差距。代码、数据和模型权重，论文说全部公开。

我的判断是，接下来值得看两件事。一是这套方法能不能在更多仿真环境上训练，摆脱单一基准的限制。二是能不能在真机上找到足够准的状态估计，让 critic 走出仿真。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from MMLab at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, together with NovaXBot. It's called CaPtain, and it asks how to turn a small open vision-language model into a robot agent that controls an arm by writing code.

The headline result: the base model, Qwen3.5-4B, solves zero LIBERO episodes out of the box. After supervised fine-tuning and multi-turn reinforcement learning, it succeeds on seventy-three point eight percent of held-out episodes.

### Problem

First, a term. Code-as-policy, or CaP, means the robot doesn't output joint commands directly. A language model looks at the camera images, writes Python against the robot's API, reads back what happened, and writes the next piece.

For coding, web and tool-use agents, there's now a standard training recipe. Supervised fine-tuning teaches the interface, then multi-turn reinforcement learning sharpens the behavior. The authors ask whether robot CaP can use the same recipe to train a small model, instead of leaning on a frontier model at test time.

### Prior work

Existing CaP agents mostly skip training. One group prompts closed frontier models without any weight updates. The paper points out that these pay for frontier calls in every episode, can't be fine-tuned for a new robot, and can't run on the robot's own computer.

Another group hands the actual manipulation to a pre-trained VLA, a vision-language-action model, or to high-level skills like grasp and place. The code picks which skill to call, but the skill decides how to grasp and from which direction to approach. The paper's argument is that the agent is then capped by the skill's training data, and RL can at best learn to sequence skills.

And small open models given direct control simply fail. In the authors' harness, Qwen3.5-4B completes none of the LIBERO episodes.

### Key idea

CaPtain rests on two ideas. First, give the model only low-level primitives. No grasp or place skills, no VLA. Every manipulation decision is written by the model itself, so the whole policy is exposed to training.

Second, credit assignment. A single episode has tens of requests, most of which just look or measure. Their physical effects often show up several turns later, and the only outcome is success or failure at the very end. But robot training happens in simulation, where exact object poses and grasp states are known. So a critic can read that hidden state, which the policy never sees, and give each request its own credit.

### Method

Start with the execution environment, CaPtain-Harness. The model's only tool is a persistent Python session, so variables survive from one request to the next. The robot module exposes twenty-two capabilities across perception, measurement, motion, planning and state. For example, SAM 3 for segmentation, Molmo2 for pointing, and a move command that sends the end effector to an explicit pose.

Every robot call returns a short text receipt saying what physically happened: why the motion stopped, how far it moved, the peak force, and how far the gripper opened or closed. An episode allows up to sixty requests, and the model ends it by calling finish.

Stage one is supervised fine-tuning. The data are synthetic trajectories produced by frontier coding agents, mainly GPT-6 Astra, working inside the same harness, in exactly the format the small model sees when deployed. According to Table 5 in the appendix, the SFT mix is about twenty-six thousand rows and eight hundred sixty-one million tokens.

One detail stands out. The authors found the model would cheat by copying pixel coordinates from earlier turns instead of looking at the image. So during training, earlier coordinates are masked with probability zero point two, forcing it to ground each new call in the current frame.

Stage two is reinforcement learning, built around a simulator critic. Before each request, the simulator records a hidden snapshot, and thirty-six features are computed from it: end-effector and gripper state, how many goals are satisfied, various distances, how high the target has been lifted, and so on. The critic is an ensemble of five small MLPs that predicts the chance the episode will end in success.

A request's credit is how much it changes that predicted chance. The request index is held fixed inside the difference, so the mere passage of time earns nothing. A request that only perceives doesn't change the physical state, so its credit is near zero, and credit concentrates on grasps and placements.

The rewards also separate the outcomes that matter for deployment. Success plus declaring completion earns one. Success without declaring it earns only zero point two five. Declaring completion on a failed state costs zero point three.

Each optimizer step accumulates gradients over one thousand twenty-four episodes. The authors found that without accumulation, consecutive updates were nearly orthogonal.

The critic is only used in training. At test time it's removed, and the policy acts on camera images and receipts alone.

### Results

Training uses only the forty original LIBERO tasks. Evaluation uses the official held-out initial states, two hundred forty episodes, plus three perturbation suites, LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-Plus and LIBERO-MAX, whose perturbations never appear in training.

Table 1 has the main numbers. On LIBERO, the base model scores zero, SFT gets to fifty-five point four percent, and RL to seventy-three point eight. Strict success, which also requires the model to declare it's done, rises from fifty-two point one to sixty-seven point five.

The RL gain holds up under perturbation, between seventeen point six and twenty-one point two points across the suites. The three short-horizon LIBERO-PRO suites average seventy-eight point five percent, and all seven LIBERO-Plus perturbation dimensions improve significantly.

Zero-shot transfer is in Table 3, with no training on the target benchmarks. On the SimplerEnv WidowX tasks, success is eighty-three point three percent. On CaP-Bench it scores seventy-five, which the paper reports is above Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5.2 and Claude Opus 4.5 in that table.

On real hardware, the authors adapted the model to an Astrabot XR1 arm using only a coarse simulated twin, with no real-world data. The paper shows two real episodes, such as putting the cube that has the color of the sky into the container that isn't round. It does not report a real-robot success rate.

Transfer also has clear weak spots. On RoboTwin 2.0, where many tasks need two arms, success is twenty-two point six percent. On RoboDojo, with a different arm, it's zero point eight percent.

On cost, the authors estimate three to six cents of policy compute per episode, not counting perception services. Published code agents report eighteen cents to twenty-two dollars per episode.

### Comparison

Table 2 compares against published systems on LIBERO-PRO. CaPtain's three-suite average of seventy-eight point five is above K1 with Gemini 3.7 Flash at seventy-seven point two, WAA at seventy-five point six, and ASPIRE with Claude Opus 4.6 at seventy-one point seven. Among open-weight agents that don't call a VLA, it's above the 27B SPARK at sixty-six point eight.

But it's clearly behind the strongest systems. OpenRUA with Claude Opus 5 reaches ninety-one point eight, Harness VLA with Claude Opus 4.7 plus pi zero point five reaches eighty-seven point seven, and AgenticRoboRL, a trained planner around a VLA, reaches eighty-one point three.

Keep in mind that these baseline numbers are taken from each system's own paper. The appendix lists protocol differences: some allow far more turns, and some use privileged geometry or oracle signals at test time.

The authors also compare the gain from RL itself. Across all four LIBERO-PRO suites, CaPtain's RL adds nineteen point four points, while AgenticRoboRL's RL over a frozen VLA adds five point nine. The authors note the two start from different baselines.

The critic analysis uses offline rollouts from an earlier 9B policy. Successful grasps get a median credit of plus zero point zero five eight, while perception-only requests sit near zero. Of the total credit, sixty-five percent goes to motions, fourteen percent each to grasps and releases, and just one point three percent to perception.

My read is that the value here isn't any single score. It's showing that robot manipulation can follow the same SFT-then-RL recipe as language agents, and that privileged simulator state neatly solves the hardest part of multi-turn RL, which is credit assignment.

My read is also that it trails the best systems by more than ten points, and the external comparisons cross papers and protocols. And the evaluations used to pick the final checkpoint included the official seed-eleven initial states, which are also among the LIBERO test episodes.

### Limitations

The authors list three limitations. First, RL is trained only on the forty single-arm LIBERO tasks. Tasks that need simultaneous two-arm coordination, or tight reach on an unfamiliar arm, are not yet handled.

Second, the critic relies on exact simulator state. In their test, adding two centimeters of noise to object poses drops the correlation with the original credit to zero point four nine, five centimeters drops it to zero point three six, and using the policy's own perception gives zero point two one. So RL directly on hardware would need a separate state estimator.

Third, the action space is bounded by those twenty-two capabilities and by what the SFT data demonstrates. Tasks that need sustained force, like the CaP-Bench wipe task that requires pressing down continuously, can't be expressed with guarded position commands.

The appendix also records a failure mode. Late in RL, the model kept re-checking solved scenes instead of calling finish, and strict success fell from about sixty percent to forty-four. That's why the authors use the checkpoint from round three hundred.

### Future work

The authors' stated direction is to extend the critic to the decision of when to stop, to two-arm coordination, and to workspaces across multiple robot arms, to close the remaining gap to frontier systems. The paper says all code, data and model weights are released.

My read is that two things are worth watching. Whether this can be trained across more simulation environments, beyond a single benchmark. And whether a good enough state estimator on real hardware can take the critic out of simulation.
