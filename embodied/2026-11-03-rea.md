## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇新加坡国立大学 HPC-AI 实验室和 InfRec 等单位合作的论文，名字叫 REA，意思是路由式的具身 Agent 运行时，副标题是，大脑和小脑的异步控制。

它要回答一个问题，一个又慢又聪明的大模型，和一堆又快又窄的机器人策略，怎么配合干活，而且不让机器人停下来等大模型。

### 问题

先说背景。现在的大模型 Agent，已经很会当机器人的大脑了，能写一段操作程序，调用分割、抓取规划和逆运动学，也能判断任务做没做完。

但它又慢又贵。ASPIRE 的修复循环，每个 LIBERO 任务中位数要花 43 分钟，用 Codex 加 GPT-6 给 60 个 LIBERO-Pro 任务写程序，消耗了 8900 万个输入 token。

让大模型直接一步一步控制机器人，也不行。作者试过让模型直接输出动作，一种接口 1987 个回合只成功了 1 个，另一种接口 600 个回合一个都没成功。

另一头是 VLA，也就是视觉语言动作模型，还有全身控制器。它们每秒能出几次到几十次动作，比 Agent 思考快得多，但泛化差。在 LIBERO 上超过百分之九十的 VLA，换到加了扰动的 LIBERO-Pro 测试集上，就崩了。

所以自然的想法是，大脑负责想，小脑负责快速执行。但作者说有两个障碍。第一，Agent 和 VLA 各用各的仿真器、观测格式和成功标准，很难放在同一批回合上比较。

第二，异步协调很难。论文这张图画了三种接法。如果 Agent 主导、去调用快策略，快策略每一步都要等 Agent，作者测到，机器人每个回合要空等 2.6 秒，占控制时间的百分之二十。

反过来，如果快策略主导、出错时才叫 Agent，一个反复出现的错误，会一次次把大脑叫醒，而大多数失败根本不报错。作者测到，按异常触发的路由器，在 590 个回合里只升级了 6 次，但实际失败了 166 个。这叫静默失败。

### 以前的方法

以前的做法有几类。一类是让大模型当规划层，比如 SayCan。一类是代码即策略，让 Agent 直接写控制程序，比如 Code as Policies，还有 ASPIRE，把修复和进化搜索用到了 LIBERO 上。

还有双系统机器人模型，比如 GR00T N1、Helix、Gemini Robotics 1.5，用一个慢的视觉语言模型，配一个快的动作模块。作者指出，这些系统里，每一层都是同一套技术栈训练出来的模型。

### 核心想法

REA 的核心想法，是一条规则，任何正在运行的策略，都不等大脑。

大脑提前离线把策略准备好，只在需要的时候在线介入。系统分三层，System 2 是大脑，一个 Agent，负责生成策略，给建议或者做判断。System 1 是具身路由器，决定跑哪个策略，什么时候问大脑。System 0 是小脑，放快策略，可以是冻结的程序、VLA，或者全身控制器。

注意，这里虽然借用了 System 1、System 2 的名字，但含义不一样。路由器本身不输出任何动作，任何策略都能当 System 0，大脑是一个独立开发的 Agent。

### 方法

具体靠三个设计。第一个叫路由记忆。正式测试之前，路由器先把每个候选策略在开发回合上跑一遍，开发回合和测试回合不重叠，记下每个策略在每个任务上的成功率和平均步数。

到了测试时，选哪个策略就变成查表，成功率最高的先上，一样高就选步数少的。整个选择过程，不用调用大脑。

第二个叫边界触发的判断门。既然大多数失败不报错，那就在每个策略返回的时候，让大脑看两帧画面，一帧是回合开始，一帧是当前，再加上任务指令，判断做完了没有。

判断做完了，就停下来，保护已经完成的结果，因为再跑一遍，可能把做好的事弄坏。判断没做完，就升级。

第三个是共享预算的升级阶梯。阶梯就是按路由记忆排好的策略顺序，比如先 Fix，再 Evo，再回到 Fix，这是 ASPIRE 的两种写程序的策略。每次升级都从当前状态接着跑，所有运行和大脑调用，共用同一份步数和时间预算，所以一个回合永远不会超过单个策略的预算。

论文第一张图就是个例子。任务是打开炉子，Fix 程序跑完没报错，但炉子没开。视觉判断说，旋钮没有明显转开，机器人还在动，判为没做完，于是升级到 Evo，跑完再判断，这次做完了，停止。

实验里有三个变体。REA-M 只用路由记忆，遇到异常或超时才升级，完全不在线调用大脑。REA-A 让 Agent 在开局时选策略，异常时选恢复策略。REA-V 用路由记忆选策略，用视觉判断门决定停还是升级。

在 LIBERO-Pro 上，System 0 的策略，是 coding Agent 写好的冻结 Python 程序，调用分割、抓取和逆运动学工具，跑在沙箱里。

### 实验和效果

主实验在 LIBERO-Pro，60 个任务，每个任务 50 个测试种子，一共 3000 个回合。相对标准的 LIBERO，这是一个分布外的测试集。

结果在表 1。单个方法里最强的，是 Agents SDK 加 GPT-6，成功率百分之94.7。REA-M 是百分之95.9，高 1.2 个百分点，作者报告这个差异的 p 值是 0.004。离事后按任务挑最佳方法的上限 96.9，差 1 个百分点。

作者强调，增益来自路由，而不是策略池更大。在 37 个对齐的任务上，策略池里只有和单方法基线完全相同的程序，REA-M 是 95.3，最强单方法是 93.7。

而且更省。每次成功平均用 1003 步，最强单方法是 1343 步，少了百分之二十五。路由记忆每个程序只用 10 个开发种子，不花任何模型 token。

速度上，这张图对比了几种接法。让路由器等 Agent 建议的阻塞式顾问，每回合控制时间 13.33 秒。REA-M 是 9.96 秒，少了百分之二十五，成功率还高 1.8 个百分点。

还有一个有意思的对比。让 Agent 在开局选策略的 REA-A，成功率和 REA-M 一样，都是 95.9，但每回合慢 1.39 秒。也就是说，查表选策略和问大脑一样准，但不用等。

判断门的作用，在 ASPIRE 的 Fix 程序上看得最清楚。原始成功率百分之66.3，加上路由记忆到 71.9，再加视觉判断门到 74.9。判断门多带来 3.1 个百分点，代价是每次成功的耗时，变成大约 2.1 倍。

在更弱的 GPT-6 零样本程序上，REA-V 把成功率从 19.3 提到 26.3。

接着是 VLA 实验，在 RoboTwin 2.0 的 100 个场景上，50 个干净，50 个随机化。路由器从 7 个 VLA 模型里选一个，成功率百分之八十四，再把一个 Python 代码策略加进候选池，到百分之八十六。最强的单个 VLA 基线 MEDex，是百分之八十。

迁移实验。在 BEHAVIOR-1K 上，REA 解出 50 个回合里的 27 个，Agents SDK 是 25 个，作者说这个差距不显著。在真实的 Unitree G1 人形机器人上，任务是走到椅子前推椅子，REA 10 次成功 8 次，Agents SDK 5 次，Codex 4 次，ASPIRE 2 次。

在 AlohaMini2 机械臂把积木放进盒子的任务上，REA 10 次全部成功，ASPIRE 是 8 次。

### 和以前方法的对比

作者还测了大脑放在哪里最合适。同一套程序库上，让 Agent 在开局和恢复时选策略，成功率只多 0.5 个百分点。让大脑读场景文字描述来判断完成，反而降了 1.3 到 2.2 个百分点。让大脑直接当控制器，600 个回合一个都没成功。

再看纯在线的 Agent。Show-Harness 这种让 GPT-6 看图决定每一步移动的方法，在 LIBERO-Pro 的 3000 个回合里，只成功了 78 个，也就是百分之2.6。

作者的结论是，大脑应该提前用，或者在策略返回的时候用，永远不要放在正在运行的策略里面。

我的判断是，这篇的价值不在某个新模型，而在于把调度这件事单独拎出来，做成一层。查表就能和问大脑一样准，说明很多时候，慢的大模型没必要站在控制回路里。

我的判断是，也要看到，LIBERO-Pro 上的提升绝对值不大，比最强单方法高 1.2 个百分点。上限其实由大脑写出的程序决定，作者自己也说，不同方法的成功率差了百分之二十到四十七，路由器只决定能拿到其中多少。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限章节，但正文里能找到不少需要注意的地方。

第一，视觉判断本身很吵，准确率几乎不比一直回答做完了强，有一组还更低。作者认为判断门有用，是因为它说做完了的时候比较准，但这个解释没有被检验。

第二，判断门要花时间，每次成功的耗时大约翻倍。作者建议按程序库来选门，策略很少失败时，用免费的异常门就够了。

第三，部分实验规模小。真机每种方法只有 10 次，BEHAVIOR-1K 上的优势不显著。RoboTwin 的 200 场景实验，同一配置两个晚上差了 36 个场景，只有并排跑的那一组能公平比较。

另外，RoboTwin 的 VLA 实验，只测了开局选一次策略，没有测恢复门。

### 未来方向

作者希望，REA 能成为一个共享平台，用来开发和比较具身大脑和快策略。

我的判断是，接下来值得看两件事，一是把 VLA 实验也接上判断门和升级阶梯，二是在更长、更开放的真机任务上，提前测好的成功率还管不管用。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the HPC-AI Lab at the National University of Singapore, InfRec and several partners. It's called REA, a Routed Embodied Agent runtime, for asynchronous brain-cerebellum control.

The question is a practical one. You have a slow, smart model and a set of fast, narrow robot policies. How do you get them to work together without the robot standing still while the big model thinks?

### Problem

Start with the brain. Foundation-model agents are now decent robot planners. They can write a manipulation program that calls segmentation, grasp planning and inverse kinematics, and they can tell whether a task succeeded.

But they're slow and expensive. The paper gives some numbers. ASPIRE's repair loop spent a median of forty-three minutes per LIBERO task, and a Codex plus GPT-6 pipeline used eighty-nine million input tokens to write programs for sixty LIBERO-Pro tasks.

And the brain can't just be the controller. When the authors had models emit actions directly, one interface solved one episode out of one thousand nine hundred eighty-seven, and another solved zero out of six hundred.

At the other end are VLAs, vision-language-action models, and whole-body controllers. They act a few to tens of times per second, far faster than an agent deliberates, but they generalize poorly. VLAs that score over ninety percent on LIBERO collapse on LIBERO-Pro's perturbed test set.

So the obvious design is a brain that thinks and a cerebellum that reacts. The authors see two obstacles. First, agent systems and VLA systems each live on their own simulator, observation format and success criterion, so it's hard to test them on the same episodes.

Second, asynchronous coordination is hard, and this figure shows three ways to wire it. If the agent drives and calls the fast policy, the fast loop waits on every agent decision. The authors measured the robot sitting idle two point six seconds per episode, twenty percent of its control time.

Flip it, so the fast policy drives and calls the agent on errors, and a persistent error wakes the brain again and again, while most failures raise no error at all. An exception-triggered router escalated only six times in five hundred ninety episodes, even though one hundred sixty-six failed. Those are silent failures.

### Prior work

Prior work comes in a few flavors. Large models as the planning layer: SayCan, PaLM-E, Inner Monologue. Code as policy, where the agent writes the controller: Code as Policies, Voyager, and ASPIRE, which scaled repair and evolutionary search to LIBERO.

Then there are dual-system robot models like GR00T N1, Helix and Gemini Robotics 1.5, which pair a slow vision-language model with a fast action component. The authors' point is that in those systems, every level is a learned model from one stack.

### Key idea

REA's key idea fits in one rule: no running policy waits on the brain.

The brain prepares policies offline and steps in online only when needed. There are three levels. System 2 is the brain, an agent that generates policies and gives advice or verdicts. System 1 is the embodied router, which decides which policy to run and when to consult the brain. System 0 is the cerebellum, the fast policies: frozen programs, VLAs or whole-body controllers.

The names are borrowed, but the meaning is different. The router emits no actions itself, any policy can serve as System 0, and the brain is an independently developed agent.

### Method

Three pieces make it work. The first is route memory. Before evaluation, the router runs every candidate policy on development episodes, with seeds that never overlap the test seeds, and stores each policy's success rate and mean steps per task.

At test time, picking a policy is a lookup. The most reliable policy goes first, and ties go to the shorter one. No brain call is involved.

The second is a boundary-triggered judge gate. Since most failures are silent, the router asks the brain each time a policy returns. It shows two frames, the start of the episode and the current view, plus the instruction, and asks: is this done?

If the verdict is done, the episode stops, which protects work already finished, because running the task again can undo it. If it's not done, the router escalates.

The third is an escalation ladder with a shared budget. The ladder is the route-memory order of policies, say Fix, then Evo, then Fix again, two of ASPIRE's program-writing strategies. Each rung starts from the current state, and every run and every brain call draws on one shared step and time budget, so an episode never exceeds a single policy's budget.

Figure one walks through an example. The task is turning on the stove. The Fix program returns with no error, but the stove is still off. The visual judge says the knob isn't visibly turned and the robot is still moving, so it's not done. The router escalates to Evo, the judge checks again, this time it's done, and the episode stops.

The experiments use three variants. REA-M uses route memory only and escalates on an exception or a timeout, with no online brain calls at all. REA-A asks an agent to pick the first policy, and a recovery policy when an exception fires. REA-V uses route memory to pick policies and the visual judge to decide whether to stop or climb the ladder.

On LIBERO-Pro, the System 0 policies are frozen Python programs written by coding agents. They call SAM 3 for segmentation, ContactGraspNet for grasps and PyRoki for inverse kinematics, all inside a sandbox.

### Results

The main benchmark is LIBERO-Pro: sixty tasks, fifty test seeds each, three thousand episodes. Relative to standard LIBERO, it's an out-of-distribution test set.

The results are in Table one. The strongest single method is Agents SDK with GPT-6, at ninety-four point seven percent. REA-M reaches ninety-five point nine, one point two points higher, and the authors report a p-value of zero point zero zero four. A hindsight oracle that picks the best method per task gets ninety-six point nine, one point above REA.

The authors stress that routing, not a bigger pool, makes the gain. On thirty-seven aligned tasks, where the pool holds only the exact programs the single-method baselines ran, REA-M gets ninety-five point three against ninety-three point seven.

It's also cheaper. REA-M needs one thousand and three steps per success, against one thousand three hundred forty-three for the best single method, twenty-five percent fewer. Route memory uses ten development seeds per program and no model tokens.

On speed, this figure compares the wiring options. A blocking advisor, where the router waits for the agent's advice, takes thirteen point three three seconds of control time per episode. REA-M takes nine point nine six, twenty-five percent less, with one point eight points more success.

Here's an interesting comparison. REA-A, which asks an agent to choose at dispatch, matches REA-M's ninety-five point nine percent, but it's one point three nine seconds slower per episode. Looking up a table picks as well as asking the brain, without the wait.

The judge gate shows its value on ASPIRE's Fix programs. They start at sixty-six point three percent. Route memory lifts that to seventy-one point nine, and the visual judge to seventy-four point nine. That's three point one extra points from the gate, at about two point one times the time per success.

On a much weaker library, zero-shot GPT-6 programs, REA-V lifts success from nineteen point three to twenty-six point three percent.

Next, VLAs. On one hundred RoboTwin 2.0 scenes, half clean and half randomized, the router picks one of seven VLA models and reaches eighty-four percent. Add a Python code policy to the pool and it reaches eighty-six. The strongest single VLA baseline, MEDex, gets eighty.

Transfer. On BEHAVIOR-1K, REA solves twenty-seven of fifty episodes against twenty-five for the Agents SDK agent, a gap the authors say is not significant. On a real Unitree G1 humanoid that has to walk to a chair and push it, REA succeeds eight times out of ten, against five for Agents SDK, four for Codex and two for ASPIRE.

On an AlohaMini2 arm placing a block into a box, REA goes ten for ten, against eight for ASPIRE.

### Comparison

The authors also ask where the brain should sit. On the same program library, letting the agent choose at dispatch and recovery adds just half a point. Having the brain judge completion from text captions of the scene costs one point three to two point two points. Using it directly as the controller solves zero of six hundred episodes.

Then there's the fully online agent. Show-Harness, which has GPT-6 choose every move from camera images, solves seventy-eight of three thousand LIBERO-Pro episodes, two point six percent.

The authors' conclusion: use the brain ahead of time, or when a policy returns, never inside a running policy.

My read is that the contribution isn't a new model. It's pulling dispatch out into its own layer. If a lookup table picks as well as the brain does, then much of the time the slow model has no business sitting in the control loop.

My read is also that the headline gain on LIBERO-Pro is small, one point two points over the best single method. The ceiling is set by the programs the brain writes. The authors say so themselves: success differs by twenty to forty-seven points across methods, and the router only decides how much of that ceiling you reach.

### Limitations

There's no separate limitations section, but the text contains several caveats.

First, the visual judge is noisy. Its final verdicts are barely more accurate than always answering done, and in one setting less accurate. The authors argue the gate helps because its done verdicts are precise, but they call that a consistent explanation, not a tested mechanism.

Second, the judge gate costs time, roughly doubling the time per success. The authors suggest choosing the gate per library: when policies rarely fail, the free exception gate is enough.

Third, some studies are small. The real-robot runs are ten trials per method, and the BEHAVIOR-1K margin isn't significant. In the two-hundred-scene RoboTwin study, the same configuration moved by thirty-six scenes between two nights, so only the side-by-side pair is a fair comparison.

Also, the RoboTwin VLA study only tests the initial policy choice. The recovery gate isn't evaluated there.

### Future work

The authors hope REA becomes a shared platform for building and comparing embodied brains and fast policies.

My read is that two things are worth watching. Whether the VLA setup gets the judge gate and the escalation ladder too, and whether success rates measured ahead of time still hold up on longer, more open-ended real-robot tasks.
