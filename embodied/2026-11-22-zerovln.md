## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇北京航空航天大学、深圳的 Zhuoyu Technology 和浙江大学合作的论文，名字叫 ZeroVLN，意思是，不给视觉语言模型这个大脑做导航方面的监督微调，也能学会导航。

一句话概括，它让大模型用自然语言说出想怎么走，交给一个小的动作模块去执行，再用强化学习把两边一起练好。

### 问题

先说背景。VLN，视觉语言导航，就是给机器人一句话，比如走出房间，上楼，停在第二个平台，让它看着摄像头画面自己走过去。这篇做的是连续环境的版本，叫 VLN-CE，机器人要一步步真的移动，而不是在预先画好的点之间跳。

现在有代表性的做法，是拿一个通用的视觉语言模型当大脑，后面接一个动作模块，再用导航数据对大脑做监督微调，也就是 SFT，让它学会输出几何目标，比如画面里的一个像素点，或者一串数字形式的移动命令。

问题在于，作者观察到，这样微调之后，大脑的权重被改动得很厉害，通用的视觉能力也明显下降。

### 以前的方法

论文拿两种代表方法做对照。一个是 NaVILA，它把大模型微调成直接输出数字化的移动命令，比如左转 45 度，在这篇的仿真设置里由仿真器直接执行。

另一个是 DualVLN，它是快慢双系统，大脑被微调去预测画面里的像素目标点，再由一个连续动作模块负责走过去。

两者的共同点是，都要用 SFT 改造大脑，让它输出导航专用的格式。论文这张图画得很直观，SFT 把大脑的输出拉向规定好的答案，比如一对像素坐标，或者一句左转 45 度。

### 核心想法

作者说灵感来自 DeepSeek-R1-Zero。R1-Zero 说明，推理能力可以不经过 SFT，直接在基础模型上用强化学习练出来。

那导航能不能也这样。他们的做法是，不教大脑输出坐标，而是给它一套工具，让它用自然语言说出自己想怎么走。

移动工具叫 run action head，有三个参数，目标是什么，在哪个方位，以及一句局部计划。论文的例子是，目标是女人身后那幅大装饰画，方位是正前方，计划是朝挂画的那面墙往前走。另外还有转身观察的 turn，和结束导航的 stop。

这句话交给一个专门的动作专家去执行。大脑负责想，动作专家负责走，这就是快慢系统。

### 方法

先看动作专家。它是一个 12 层的 flow-matching 模型，可训练参数大约 3480 万，视觉编码器来自 Qwen3.5-4B，是冻结的。Flow matching 可以理解成，从随机噪声出发，一步步把它变成一段动作序列。

大脑的那句话怎么传过去。做法是在大模型的输入后面接上 16 个可学习的查询向量，大模型读完上下文和工具调用之后，这些向量在最后一层的状态，就作为语义条件交给动作专家，再配上参考画面和当前画面。

训练分两个阶段。第一阶段只训练动作专家，大脑完全冻结。难点在于，大脑说的是自然语言，动作专家的训练数据从哪来。

作者的办法叫请求落地。先让冻结的大脑在仿真环境里真的跑起来，收集它实际说出的移动请求，再用一个只在训练时使用的规划器，借助由场景网格得到的语义地图，把每句请求落到一个能到达的局部目标上，规划出一条参考轨迹。

这样动作专家学的，正好是大脑真正会说的话。附录里有个对照很能说明问题，人工构造的指令模板，动作专家能学到百分之九十五以上的准确率，但闭环导航的成功率完全没有提高。

还有一个细节，叫执行前缀监督。动作专家每次预测 16 步，但只执行前 4 步，就重新看画面再预测。所以作者只在这 4 步上算损失，不去管反正会被丢掉的后 12 步。图 9 显示，在同样的参考轨迹上，这样训练比监督全部 16 步效果更好。

第二阶段是联合强化学习，大脑和动作专家一起练。每个导航任务采样多条完整的尝试，一条尝试的总回报，减去其他几条的平均回报，就是它的优势，这个方法叫 RLOO。

关键是，这条尝试里的每一轮决策、每一段动作，都用同一个优势来更新。这叫轨迹级的功劳分配，看的是整条尝试最后的结果。

奖励有四项，最终成功，第一次到达目标区域，离目标的距离有没有缩短，以及有没有新发现和任务相关的物体。后两项是过程奖励，作用是即使所有尝试都失败了，也能分出好坏，学习信号不至于消失。

还有一点，两个模型共享同一个优势，但梯度各走各的，动作专家的梯度不会直接改动大脑的工具调用。

具体设置，大脑是 Qwen3.6-35B-A3B，强化学习训练 200 步，每步 64 个任务，每个任务 8 条尝试。导航训练只用了 34445 个任务，而且不用数据集自带的专家轨迹。

### 实验和效果

评测用了三个连续环境的导航基准，SAGE-Bench、R2R-CE 和 RxR-CE，主要看成功率。为了公平，作者把 NaVILA 和 DualVLN 用同一个大脑、同样的 34445 个训练任务重新训了一遍。

结果在表 1。ZeroVLN 在 SAGE-3D、R2R 和 RxR 上的成功率分别是 51.3%、52.7% 和 51.4%，在对照组里三项都是最高。

对比 DualVLN，三个基准上都高出 30 个百分点以上。对比 NaVILA，分别高出 27.8、6.2 和 2.7 个百分点。

有个细节很有意思。完全不训练，只给原始大模型几个简单的移动工具，在 SAGE-3D 上就有 39.5% 的成功率，比经过 SFT 的 NaVILA 和 DualVLN 都高。作者认为，这说明通用大模型本来就能做出有用的导航决策。

强化学习这一步贡献很大。只做第一阶段时，R2R 的成功率是 17.0%，联合强化学习之后到了 52.7%，RxR 从 18.7% 到了 51.4%。

再看大脑被改了多少。论文用权重的相对变化量来衡量，ZeroVLN 只有 0.01497%，DualVLN 是 3.21%，NaVILA 是 7.67%。

通用能力方面，在 12 个视觉和空间推理基准上，原始大脑平均 59.47 分，ZeroVLN 是 59.16 分，几乎没掉。两个 SFT 方法在同样的评测下都是零分。问 NaVILA 哪个物体离得最近，它回答的是，下一步左转 45 度。

作者也考虑到零分可能只是答题格式的问题，于是直接读模型对选项字母的概率。结果 ZeroVLN 是 62.00%，原始大脑是 62.86%，而两个 SFT 方法接近随机水平。说明受损的不只是答题格式，能力本身也退化了。

论文还给了一个上楼的例子。机器人的移动请求两次被挡住，大脑就转身换个角度，重新下请求，最后到了第二个平台，在第 25 轮停下。

### 和以前方法的对比

消融实验在图 12。只训练动作专家，提升有限。只训练大脑，成绩先涨后跌，最后掉到初始值以下，作者的解释是大脑的请求变了，动作专家没跟上。两个一起练，才能持续提升。

功劳怎么分也做了对比。如果每一轮单独算优势，模型更容易经过目标，但成功率在训练中反而下降。轨迹级分配的成功率和路径效率都更好。

再和用了更多数据的已发表方法比。在 SAGE-3D 上，ZeroVLN 的 51.3% 超过了 NaVILA-SAGE 的 46.0%，训练任务只有对方的大约十四分之一。

但在 R2R 和 RxR 上，JanusVLN 用了至少 16.9 万个训练任务，成功率是 60.5% 和 56.2%，都比 ZeroVLN 高。所以它在这两个基准上并不是最强，优势在于数据少，而且大脑没被破坏。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的不是分数，而是接口的选择。让大脑说人话，动作模块听人话，大脑就还是那个通用模型，同一个大脑以后可以接不同的动作专家。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限章节，下面几点来自论文自己的结果和设置。第一是路径效率。在 R2R 和 RxR 上，ZeroVLN 的 SPL，也就是按路径长度打折的成功率，是 29.2% 和 28.1%，明显低于 NaVILA 的 41.8% 和 43.0%。作者承认，更多的探索和重试让路径变长了。

第二，训练依赖仿真里的特权信息。参考轨迹要用场景网格做出的语义地图来规划，距离奖励也要知道到目标的可通行距离。论文的结果都来自 VLN-CE 仿真环境，没有真机实验。

第三，算力成本不低。强化学习用了 8 个 16 卡的节点，每一步要跑 512 条长程导航。

### 未来方向

作者在结论里提出，这套思路可以推广到导航以外，让一个共享的通用大脑，通过不同的专用动作专家，学会多种物理技能。代码和模型，论文首页给了 GitHub 和 Hugging Face 的地址。

我的判断是，下一步最该看的是真机。训练时用的特权地图，在真实场景里能不能找到替代，路径效率能不能补上，这两点决定它能不能走出仿真。

我的判断是，这篇的方向和最近 Agent 领域的趋势是一致的，把身体控制当成工具调用，用强化学习而不是 SFT 去适配，保住通用模型的能力。这条路值得继续跟。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Beihang University, Zhuoyu Technology in Shenzhen, and Zhejiang University. It's called ZeroVLN, and the claim is in the title: a robot can learn to navigate without supervised fine-tuning of its vision-language model brain.

The short version. The big model says in plain language where it wants to go, a small action module carries that out, and reinforcement learning trains the two together.

### Problem

Some background first. Vision-language navigation, or VLN, means giving a robot an instruction like "leave the room, go up the stairs, stop on the second landing," and having it get there from its camera view. This paper works on the continuous version, VLN-CE, where the agent actually moves step by step instead of hopping between pre-drawn points.

The representative recipe today takes a general vision-language model as the brain, attaches an action module, and fine-tunes the brain on navigation data, so it outputs geometric goals: a pixel in the image, say, or a numeric movement command.

The problem, the authors observe, is that this fine-tuning moves the brain's weights a lot, and its general visual abilities drop noticeably.

### Prior work

The paper uses two representative methods as controls. One is NaVILA, which fine-tunes the model to output numeric movement commands like "turn left forty-five degrees," executed directly by the simulator in this paper's setup.

The other is DualVLN, a slow-fast system. Its brain is fine-tuned to predict a pixel goal in the image, and a continuous action module drives there.

What they share is that both use supervised fine-tuning to reshape the brain into producing a navigation-specific format. This figure makes the point: fine-tuning pulls the brain's outputs toward prescribed answers, a pair of pixel coordinates, or "turn left forty-five degrees."

### Key idea

The authors say they were inspired by DeepSeek-R1-Zero, which showed that reasoning can emerge from a base model through reinforcement learning, with no supervised fine-tuning first.

So, can navigation work the same way? Their answer: don't teach the brain to output coordinates. Give it tools, and let it say in natural language how it wants to move.

The movement tool is called run action head, with three arguments: the target, where it is, and a short local plan. The paper's example: the target is the large decorative painting behind the woman, it's straight ahead, and the plan is to move forward toward the wall with the painting. There's also a turn tool to look around, and a stop tool to end the episode.

That request goes to a dedicated action expert. The brain decides, the action expert moves. That's the slow-fast split.

### Method

First, the action expert. It's a twelve-layer flow-matching model with about thirty-five million trainable parameters, and a frozen vision encoder taken from Qwen three point five, four B. Flow matching, roughly, starts from random noise and turns it, step by step, into a sequence of actions.

How does the brain's sentence reach it? Sixteen learnable query vectors are appended to the model's input. After the model reads the context and the tool call, the final-layer states of those queries become the semantic condition for the action expert, alongside a reference image and the current image.

Training has two stages. In stage one, only the action expert is trained, and the brain is completely frozen. The hard part: the brain speaks natural language, so where does the action expert's training data come from?

The answer is what they call request grounding. They let the frozen brain actually run in simulation and collect the movement requests it really makes. Then a planner that's only used in training takes a semantic map built from the scene mesh, grounds each request in a reachable local goal, and plans a reference trajectory to it.

So the action expert learns exactly the language the brain will actually use. An appendix experiment shows why this matters. With hand-built instruction templates, the action expert reaches over ninety-five percent accuracy on those templates, yet closed-loop navigation success doesn't improve at all.

One more detail: execution-prefix supervision. The action expert predicts sixteen steps but executes only the first four before looking again and replanning. So the loss covers only those four steps, not the twelve that get thrown away anyway. Figure nine shows that, on the same reference trajectories, this beats supervising all sixteen.

Stage two is joint reinforcement learning, training the brain and the action expert together. For each navigation task they sample several full attempts. An attempt's advantage is its total return minus the average return of the other attempts. That's called RLOO, leave-one-out.

The key choice: every decision and every action chunk in that attempt is updated with the same advantage. That's trajectory-level credit assignment. What counts is how the whole attempt turned out.

There are four rewards: final success, first arrival in the goal region, progress in distance to the goal, and newly discovered task-relevant objects. The last two are process rewards. Even when every attempt fails, they still separate better attempts from worse ones, so the learning signal doesn't vanish.

Also, the two models share that advantage but keep separate gradient paths, so the action expert's gradients don't directly change the brain's tool calls.

The setup: the brain is Qwen three point six, thirty-five B A three B. Reinforcement learning runs two hundred steps, with sixty-four tasks per step and eight attempts per task. Navigation training uses only thirty-four thousand four hundred forty-five tasks, and no expert trajectories from the datasets.

### Results

Evaluation covers three continuous navigation benchmarks, SAGE-Bench, R2R-CE and RxR-CE, mainly by success rate. For a fair comparison, the authors retrained NaVILA and DualVLN with the same brain and the same thirty-four thousand four hundred forty-five training tasks.

Table one has the results. ZeroVLN's success rates on SAGE-3D, R2R and RxR are fifty-one point three, fifty-two point seven, and fifty-one point four percent, the highest of the controlled group on all three.

Against DualVLN, it's ahead by more than thirty percentage points on every benchmark. Against NaVILA, the margins are twenty-seven point eight, six point two, and two point seven points.

Here's an interesting detail. With no training at all, just the original model and a few simple movement tools, success on SAGE-3D is already thirty-nine point five percent, higher than both fine-tuned baselines, NaVILA and DualVLN. The authors take this as evidence that a general model can already make useful navigation decisions.

The reinforcement learning stage does a lot of the work. After stage one alone, R2R success is seventeen percent; after joint RL it's fifty-two point seven. RxR goes from eighteen point seven to fifty-one point four.

Now, how much did the brain change? Measured as relative weight displacement, ZeroVLN moves the weights by just zero point zero one four nine seven percent. DualVLN moves them three point two one percent, and NaVILA seven point six seven percent.

On general ability, across twelve visual and spatial reasoning benchmarks, the original brain averages fifty-nine point four seven, and ZeroVLN fifty-nine point one six, nearly unchanged. Both fine-tuned baselines score zero under the same evaluation. Asked which object is nearest, NaVILA answers: the next action is turn left forty-five degrees.

The authors checked whether those zeros were only a formatting failure, by reading the model's probabilities for the answer letters directly. ZeroVLN gets sixty-two percent, the original brain sixty-two point eight six, and both fine-tuned baselines land close to chance. So the damage goes beyond answer formatting; the ability itself has degraded.

The paper also shows a staircase example. Twice the robot's movement request comes back blocked. The brain turns to change its view, reissues the request, reaches the second landing, and stops in round twenty-five.

### Comparison

The ablations are in figure twelve. Training only the action expert gives limited gains. Training only the brain helps at first, then falls below where it started; the authors attribute that to the brain's requests drifting while the action expert stays fixed. Training both together keeps improving.

They also compare how credit is assigned. With a separate advantage for each round, the model reaches the goal region more often, but success actually declines during training. Trajectory-level credit gives better success and better path efficiency.

Next, published methods trained on more data. On SAGE-3D, ZeroVLN's fifty-one point three percent beats NaVILA-SAGE's forty-six, with roughly one fourteenth as many training tasks.

But on R2R and RxR, JanusVLN, trained on at least a hundred sixty-nine thousand tasks, reaches sixty point five and fifty-six point two percent, both higher than ZeroVLN. So it isn't the strongest on those two benchmarks. Its edge is less data and an intact brain.

My read is that the most valuable part of this paper isn't the score, it's the choice of interface. If the brain speaks plain language and the action module listens to plain language, the brain stays a general model, and the same brain could later drive different action experts.

### Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations section, so these points come from its own results and setup. First, path efficiency. On R2R and RxR, ZeroVLN's SPL, success weighted by path length, is twenty-nine point two and twenty-eight point one percent, well below NaVILA's forty-one point eight and forty-three. The authors acknowledge that more exploration and retries make the paths longer.

Second, training relies on privileged simulator information. Reference trajectories are planned with a semantic map built from the scene mesh, and the distance reward needs the traversable distance to the goal. All reported results come from the VLN-CE simulation setting; there are no real-robot experiments.

Third, compute isn't cheap. Reinforcement learning ran on eight sixteen-device nodes, with five hundred twelve long-horizon rollouts per step.

### Future work

In the conclusion, the authors suggest the idea extends beyond navigation: one shared general brain that picks up different physical skills through specialized action experts. The first page lists a GitHub repository and a Hugging Face model.

My read is that the next thing to watch is real robots. Whether the privileged maps used in training can be replaced in real scenes, and whether path efficiency can catch up, will decide whether this leaves simulation.

My read is also that this fits a wider trend in agent work: treat physical control as a tool call, adapt with reinforcement learning instead of supervised fine-tuning, and keep the general model's abilities intact. It's a line worth following.
