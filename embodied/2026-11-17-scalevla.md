## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇清华大学、北京大学、上海交通大学和 Galbot 合作的论文，名字叫 ScaleVLA，它把机器人策略里的视觉语言骨干，一直放大到了 5520 亿参数。

它问的问题很直接，前沿大模型在预训练里积累的知识，能不能变成机器人实实在在的动作能力。

### 问题

先解释一个词。VLA，全称 Vision-Language-Action，视觉语言动作模型，输入是摄像头画面和一句语言指令，输出是机器人的动作。

论文的出发点是，最近对 GPT-6 Astra 的研究显示，通用大模型可以生成机器人动作、完成操作。作者认为，这些能力主要不是从机器人示教里学来的，看起来来自大规模预训练积累的视觉、空间和推理知识。

可是大多数 VLA 用的还是小骨干，比如 GR00T N1.7，用的是 20 亿参数的 Cosmos-Reason2。所以问题就来了，如果把骨干换成前沿规模，学出来的机器人策略会不会更强。

### 以前的方法

以前的工作里，有两条路和这篇直接相关。第一条是把大模型直接当策略，让它选技能、写计划、生成控制代码。论文提到，对 GPT-6 Astra 的评测显示，这样确实能完成有用的操作，但反复推理和检查动作，让每一次决策都又慢又贵。

第二条是学习型 VLA，从示教数据里直接学动作。已经有工作对比过不同骨干，这篇的不同在于，把骨干一路比到 5520 亿参数，同时固定动作模块和示教预算。

### 核心想法

ScaleVLA 的想法可以用一句话概括，不让大模型推理，只借用它的表征。每生成一段动作，只读一次骨干，把内部特征直接映射成动作，全程不生成任何文字。

这张图是整体结构。左边的骨干提供预训练积累的知识，并且是冻结的，右边的动作模块从零开始训练，从机器人示教里学连续控制。

### 方法

骨干用的是 DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash，一个混合专家架构的模型，总共 5520 亿参数，但处理每个 token 时，只激活其中一小部分。

它的语言部分分成 20 层因果编码器和 20 层解码器。ScaleVLA 只运行编码器，在两者的交界处取出特征，计算到这里就停止，解码器完全不运行。

动作模块沿用 GR00T N1.7 的架构，全部随机初始化。骨干特征先经过一个适配器，统一到 2048 维，再送进一个 32 层的扩散 Transformer，也就是 DiT，里面自注意力和交叉注意力交替进行。

训练用的是 flow matching，简单说，就是学习一条从随机噪声走向示教动作的路径。推理时从噪声出发，走四步得到一段动作，四步共用同一份骨干特征，所以每段动作，骨干只运行一次。

这篇最关键的是对比设计。从 20 亿到 5520 亿的骨干，动作模块的架构、初始化、示教数据和训练预算都保持一致，也不加载任何预训练好的机器人动作模型。

### 实验和效果

先看 LIBERO 仿真，这是一套语言指令下的机械臂操作任务。四个任务集的平均成功率，ScaleVLA 是 96.1，320 亿的 Cosmos-Reason2 是 94.0，20 亿的是 90.7。差距主要出现在 Goal 和 Long 两组，一组要理解指令，一组要串联多个步骤。

再看泛化。LIBERO-PRO 给环境和物体加上扰动，而且不重新训练。所有模型都大幅下降，ScaleVLA 平均 45.1，较小骨干里最高只有 31.3，这是所有仿真基准里提升最大的一项。

RoboCasa 测的是训练时没见过的厨房场景。每个任务 30 条示教时，ScaleVLA 是 30.7，较小骨干最高 25.6。100 条示教时，ScaleVLA 是 54.9，较小骨干最高 45.6。

DexJoCo 是双手灵巧操作，五个任务用同一个策略一起学。物体随机时，ScaleVLA 平均 42.1，较小骨干最高 38.1。全部随机时，分别是 24.4 和 22.1，绝对成功率其实还很低。

样本效率在表 7，训练步数固定为 5000 步。在 LIBERO Goal 上，ScaleVLA 每个任务只用 10 条示教，成功率 87.8，比 20 亿骨干用 50 条示教的 85.6 还高。只给 5 条示教时，20 亿骨干是 39.4，ScaleVLA 是 77.4。

然后是真机实验，任务是插充电器。双臂机器人的右臂抓起充电器递过去，左臂对准插座插入再松开。作者比较了三种骨干，20 亿的 Cosmos-Reason2、派 0.5，还有 ScaleVLA，动作模块、数据和训练预算都相同。

结果在表 8。在接近训练分布的十次试验里，三者分别成功 4 次、8 次和 9 次。在超出训练分布的十五次试验里，分别成功 5 次、7 次和 8 次。论文说，分布内的失败全都发生在插入阶段。

速度上，只统计分布内成功的试验，ScaleVLA 插入阶段的中位时间是 58.1 秒，派 0.5 是 81.2 秒，Cosmos-Reason2 是 85.9 秒。完成整个任务分别是 114.4 秒、132.0 秒和 147.1 秒。

延迟也没有想象中夸张。在 RoboCasa 上，ScaleVLA 每段动作的中位延迟是 216 毫秒，20 亿骨干是 115 毫秒，参数是它的 276 倍，延迟是 1.9 倍。不过 ScaleVLA 用了四块 H200，其他骨干只用一块。

### 和以前方法的对比

和已发表的方法比，LIBERO 平均成功率，派 0 是 94.2，CoT-VLA 是 83.9，OpenVLA 是 76.5，ScaleVLA 是 96.1。要注意，这三个数字是论文引用的已发表结果，不是用同一套流程重新训练的。

更有意思的是和派 0.5 的对比。它的骨干虽小，但在大规模机器人数据上预训练过，所以这组实验问的是，机器人预训练能不能替代前沿规模的表征。作者的结论是，差距缩小了，但没有追平。

我的判断是，真机这组结果只能看方向。摘要强调的 4 次到 9 次，比的是 20 亿骨干，而和派 0.5 相比，十次里只多成功一次，十五次里也只多一次，这个样本量不足以证明稳定的差距。

我的判断是，这篇也不能读成参数越大越好。在 LIBERO-PRO 上，320 亿的 Qwen3-VL 是 22.8，反而低于它 20 亿版本的 29.3。而且 5520 亿这个点同时换了模型家族和架构，RoboCasa 延迟测试里的输入 token 也从 212 个变成 568 个，提升不能全部归功于规模。

### 局限

第一，样本效率的结论依赖固定的训练预算。表 9 显示，只有 5 条示教时，把 20 亿骨干训练到 4 万步，Goal 成功率从 39.4 升到 69.6，ScaleVLA 训练到 2 万步是 77.2。作者也写明，固定预算下的样本效率，不能等同于收敛之后的比较。

第二，示教变多以后，优势会缩小。在表 7 的同一组实验里，和 20 亿骨干相比，Goal 上 ScaleVLA 的领先，从 5 条示教时的 38.0 个百分点，缩小到 50 条示教时的 9.8 个百分点。Long 上则从 38.2 个百分点缩小到 4.4 个百分点。

第三，不是每个任务都赢。DexJoCo 的微波炉任务，在物体随机时，ScaleVLA 是 73.3，是五个骨干里最低的，最高的 320 亿骨干达到 89.3。

第四，实验规模有限。每个策略只训练了一个随机种子，真机只测了一个任务，成功与否由操作者判断。

第五，延迟和成功率不是在同一套部署上测的。延迟用 vLLM 测量，论文说明，它的特征没有和产出成功率的部署逐项比较。其他骨干两条路径的输入在 16 个观测上做过核对，这句话没有包括 ScaleVLA。

### 未来方向

作者认为，前沿视觉语言模型不只能做推理 Agent，也可以成为快速学习型机器人策略的表征基础。他们也指出，对长流程任务来说，更多针对任务的示教仍然有价值。

我的判断是，下一步最值得看的，是在同一个模型家族里做规模梯度，用多个随机种子和更多真机任务，把规模本身的作用单独分离出来。另外，四块 H200 的算力怎么落到真实机器人上，也是绕不开的问题。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Tsinghua University, Peking University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Galbot. It's called ScaleVLA, and it pushes the vision-language backbone of a robot policy all the way to five hundred fifty-two billion parameters.

The question behind it is easy to state. Can the knowledge a frontier model picks up in pretraining turn into real physical skill on a robot?

### Problem

Quick definition first. A VLA, or vision-language-action model, takes camera images and a language instruction, and it outputs robot actions.

The motivation comes from recent studies of GPT-6 Astra, which show a general-purpose model generating robot actions and performing manipulation. The authors point out that those abilities don't seem to come mainly from robot demonstrations. They appear to draw on broad knowledge from large-scale pretraining.

Yet most VLAs still sit on small backbones. GR00T N one point seven, for example, uses the two-billion-parameter Cosmos-Reason2. So the question becomes: what happens to a learned robot policy if you swap in a frontier-scale backbone?

### Prior work

Two lines of earlier work matter here. One treats the big model as the policy itself, letting it pick skills, write plans or generate controller code. Citing evaluations of GPT-6 Astra, the paper says this can produce useful manipulation, but repeated reasoning and action review make each decision slow and costly.

The other line is the learned VLA, which learns from demonstrations to map observations straight to actions. Earlier work has compared backbones too. What's different here is the range, all the way up to five hundred fifty-two billion parameters, with the action model and the demonstration budget held fixed.

### Key idea

The idea fits in one line: borrow the big model's representations, but don't ask it to reason. For each chunk of actions, ScaleVLA reads the backbone once and maps those internal features directly to motion, with no text generated at all.

Here's the architecture. On the left, a frozen backbone supplies what pretraining already knows. On the right, an action head trained from scratch learns continuous control from robot demonstrations.

### Method

The backbone is DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a mixture-of-experts model with five hundred fifty-two billion parameters in total. Only a fraction of those are active for any given token.

Its language model is split into a twenty-layer causal encoder and a twenty-layer decoder. ScaleVLA runs only the encoder and reads the features where the two meet, so the decoder never runs.

The action head follows the GR00T N one point seven design and starts from random weights. Backbone features pass through an adapter that brings them to a width of two thousand forty-eight, then into a thirty-two-layer diffusion transformer that alternates self-attention and cross-attention.

Training uses flow matching, which learns a path from random noise to the demonstrated action. At run time the model starts from noise and takes four steps to produce an action chunk. All four steps share the same backbone features, so the backbone runs just once per chunk.

The experimental design is what makes this paper useful. Across backbones from two billion to five hundred fifty-two billion parameters, the action architecture, initialization, demonstrations and training budget all stay fixed, and no pretrained robot-action model is loaded.

### Results

Start with LIBERO, a simulated suite of language-conditioned manipulation tasks. Averaged over its four task suites, ScaleVLA scores ninety-six point one, the thirty-two-billion Cosmos-Reason2 scores ninety-four, and the two-billion version scores ninety point seven. Most of the gap is on the Goal and Long suites, which depend on understanding the instruction and chaining several steps.

Generalization is next. LIBERO-PRO perturbs the scene and the objects, with no retraining. Every policy falls sharply, but ScaleVLA averages forty-five point one, while the best smaller backbone reaches thirty-one point three. That's the largest gain on any simulation benchmark in the paper.

RoboCasa tests kitchen scenes the policy never saw in training. With thirty demonstrations per task, ScaleVLA gets thirty point seven against a best of twenty-five point six for smaller backbones. With one hundred demonstrations, it's fifty-four point nine against forty-five point six.

DexJoCo covers two-handed dexterous work, with one policy learning five tasks together. Under object randomization, ScaleVLA averages forty-two point one against thirty-eight point one. Under full randomization it's twenty-four point four against twenty-two point one, so absolute success is still low.

Sample efficiency is in Table 7, with training fixed at five thousand steps. On LIBERO Goal, ScaleVLA with ten demonstrations per task reaches eighty-seven point eight, beating a two-billion backbone with fifty demonstrations at eighty-five point six. With only five demonstrations, the two-billion backbone gets thirty-nine point four and ScaleVLA gets seventy-seven point four.

Then the real robot. The task is plugging in a charger: the right arm grasps it and hands it over, and the left arm lines the plug up with a power strip, inserts it and lets go. Three backbones are compared, the two-billion Cosmos-Reason2, pi zero point five, and ScaleVLA, all with the same action head, data and training budget.

Table 8 has the scores. Near the training distribution, out of ten trials, they succeed four, eight and nine times. Outside it, out of fifteen trials, they succeed five, seven and eight times. The paper says every in-domain failure happened during insertion.

Speed is measured on successful in-domain trials only. ScaleVLA's median insertion takes fifty-eight point one seconds, against eighty-one point two for pi zero point five and eighty-five point nine for Cosmos-Reason2. Full completion takes one hundred fourteen point four, one hundred thirty-two, and one hundred forty-seven point one seconds.

Latency is less dramatic than you might expect. On RoboCasa, ScaleVLA's median is two hundred sixteen milliseconds per action chunk, against one hundred fifteen for the two-billion backbone. That's two hundred seventy-six times the parameters for one point nine times the latency. The catch is that ScaleVLA runs on four H200 GPUs, and the others use one.

### Comparison

Against published methods on LIBERO, pi zero averages ninety-four point two, CoT-VLA eighty-three point nine, and OpenVLA seventy-six point five, compared with ninety-six point one for ScaleVLA. Those three numbers are cited from earlier papers, not retrained under this paper's setup.

The more interesting comparison is pi zero point five. Its backbone is small but was pretrained on large-scale robot data, so this tests whether robot pretraining can substitute for frontier-scale representations. The authors conclude that it narrows the gap without closing it.

My read is that the real-robot numbers show a direction, not a settled gap. The abstract's jump from four to nine successes is against the two-billion backbone. Against pi zero point five, ScaleVLA wins by one trial out of ten and one trial out of fifteen, and that sample is too small to prove a stable difference.

My read is also that this isn't simply a case of bigger is better. On LIBERO-PRO, the thirty-two-billion Qwen3-VL scores twenty-two point eight, below the twenty-nine point three of its two-billion sibling. And the five-hundred-fifty-two-billion point also changes the model family and the architecture, and in the RoboCasa timing runs the input grows from two hundred twelve tokens to five hundred sixty-eight, so scale can't take all the credit.

### Limitations

First, the sample-efficiency result depends on a fixed training budget. In Table 9, with five demonstrations, training the two-billion backbone to forty thousand steps lifts Goal from thirty-nine point four to sixty-nine point six, while ScaleVLA reaches seventy-seven point two at twenty thousand steps. The authors themselves caution that fixed-budget sample efficiency shouldn't be equated with a comparison after convergence.

Second, the advantage shrinks as demonstrations grow. In the same Table 7 sweep, ScaleVLA's lead over the two-billion backbone on Goal falls from thirty-eight points at five demonstrations to nine point eight points at fifty. On Long, it falls from thirty-eight point two points to four point four.

Third, it doesn't win everywhere. On DexJoCo's Microwave task under object randomization, ScaleVLA scores seventy-three point three, the lowest of the five backbones, while the thirty-two-billion model reaches eighty-nine point three.

Fourth, the evaluation is small. Each policy was trained with a single seed, the real-robot test covers one task, and a human operator judges success.

Fifth, latency and success rates come from different deployments. Timing was measured on vLLM, and the paper notes its features weren't compared element by element with the deployment that produced the success rates. The prompts were checked across the two paths on sixteen observations for the other backbones, but that statement excludes ScaleVLA.

### Future work

The authors argue that frontier vision-language models can be more than reasoning agents, and can serve as the representational foundation for fast, learned robot policies. They also note that long-horizon tasks still benefit from more task-specific demonstrations.

My read is that the next experiment to watch is a scaling ladder inside a single model family, with multiple seeds and more real-robot tasks, so the effect of scale can be isolated. And there's a practical question too: how four H200 GPUs of compute ever make it onto a real robot.
