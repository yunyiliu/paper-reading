## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇智元机器人 AgiBot 的论文，合作方是北京航空航天大学和新加坡国立大学，名字叫 Rollback，就是回滚。

它做的事情，用一句话说，就是让机器人在世界模型里读档重来，回到关键的那一步，换个动作再试，看结果有什么不同。

论文报告，在三个真机任务上，成功率比最强的基线分别高了 10.67、11.11 和 11.85 个百分点。

### 问题

先说背景。机器人在真实世界里做强化学习很贵，每试一次都要占用机器人，失败了还得有人去复位。

所以大家开始用世界模型，可以理解成一个学出来的模拟器，给它动作，它预测接下来会看到什么，策略就能在想象里练。

但作者指出，现有做法还是照搬真机的习惯，从头往前跑一整条轨迹，跑完，中间的状态就扔掉了。

这就浪费了世界模型一个真机没有的能力，倒带。真实的积木塔推倒了就回不去，世界模型却可以回到某个中间时刻，换个动作重来。

### 以前的方法

以前的方法大致两类。一类直接在真机上做强化学习，比如 RECAP，问题还是真机交互贵，复位要人工。

另一类在世界模型里做强化学习，比如 RISE，它往前推演，再用一个学出来的进度价值模型给动作打分。

作者认为这类方法很难稳定提升，只往前推演，很少回到真正关键的决策点，学出来的价值模型也可能有偏差。

还有一个更隐蔽的问题。世界模型一般离线训练好就固定了，可策略越练越好，恰恰会走到模拟器见得最少的状态。

### 核心想法

Rollback 的核心想法，就是把世界模型从一个只会往前播放的模拟器，变成一个可以倒带的训练环境。

这张图对比了三种做法。左边是真机强化学习，交互成本高。中间是在世界模型里一条条往前推演，训练不稳定。右边是 Rollback，回到关键决策，探索不同的未来，再从结果的差异里学习。

这样做有个好处。从同一个时刻分出去的几个动作，前面的经历完全一样，只有这一步不同，所以结果不同，就可以归到这一步的动作上。

### 方法

方法分三块，看这张总览图。第一块是倒带式的树搜索，第二块是用这棵树来估计每个动作的价值，第三块是世界模型和策略一起进化。

先说第一块。世界模型用的是 GE-Sim 2.0，给它一个动作片段，它同时预测三个摄像头的画面，和每只手臂七个关节加一个夹爪的状态。

搜索从一条已有的轨迹出发，可以是演示数据，也可以是机器人自己的尝试。每个动作片段的交界处，都是一个可以分叉的决策点，世界模型的状态都缓存下来，再分叉时不用重新生成前面那一段。

在哪儿分叉，用的是蒙特卡洛树搜索，思路来自 AlphaGo。成功率高的分支多探索，试得少的分支也留一点机会。

新的动作由策略自己提出，每次回到节点，换一个随机种子再采样，所以探索的都是当前策略觉得说得通的动作。

每条想象出来的轨迹跑到底，由一个奖励模型判断成败。它是 GE-Sim 2.0 的世界裁判，作者针对每个任务，用标注过的机器人视频做了微调。

第二块，用树来估值。一个动作好不好，就看从它分出去的所有后续里，成功的比例是多少。

图里的例子，三个动作从同一个状态出发，第一个的后续四次成功三次，是 0.75，第二个是 0.5，第三个全失败，是 0。

然后把每个动作的值，除以兄弟里最好的那个，分成五档。兄弟动作全落在同一档的，没有比较信号，直接丢掉。这和 GRPO 的组内相对比较很像，只是这里的一组，是从同一个状态采样出来的几个动作。

好处是不用另外训练价值模型。奖励模型只要判断一条完整的轨迹最后成没成功，这比从中间状态预测成功率容易训练。

策略怎么学，做法沿用 RECAP。策略是 π0.5，把档位写进提示里，真实轨迹也一起用，成功的算最高档，失败的算最低档。搜索和部署时，都让策略按最高档出动作。

第三块，世界模型和策略一起进化。策略变强之后，会走到世界模型训练数据之外的地方，世界模型在那里预测不准，策略就可能钻这个空子。

论文举的例子是，积木本来应该倒，世界模型却预测它还立着，搜索就会把这次失败当成成功来学。

解决办法是，真机上新跑出来的轨迹，不管成败，都拿来继续微调世界模型。每次用 LoRA 微调 400 步，再做 100 步蒸馏，保持快速生成。作者把这个叫做 test-time physics grounding，可以理解成边用边校准物理。

工程上，这几件事同时跑，谁也不等谁，只通过一个共享的数据池交换数据。实验里用了 48 张 H200 显卡，40 张做搜索，8 张训练策略，真机只有一台。

### 实验和效果

实验在 AgiBot 的 Genie-02 机器人上做，三个真机任务。第一个是抽积木，从叠好的积木塔里一次抽一块，塔不能倒。第二个是扔球进桶，考的是出手的动作和时机。第三个是桌面冰壶，人和机器人各推两个壶，按离中心的远近算分。

每个任务选六个初始位置，冰壶是九个，每个位置测 15 次，抽积木每个位置测 25 次。

基线都从同一个 π0.5 出发，算力预算相当，有监督微调，真机强化学习 RECAP，以及作者在同一个世界模型上复现的 RISE。RECAP 和 RISE 也都是一台机器人，训练大约 8 小时。

这一点很关键。RISE 和 Rollback 用同一个世界模型，所以两者的差距，反映的是倒带搜索和树状估值本身，而不是世界模型的好坏。

结果在表 1。Rollback 在三个任务上都是最高，抽积木 69.33%，扔球 40%，冰壶 64.44%。

三个任务平均是 57.93%，最强的基线 RISE 是 46.72%，只做监督微调是 40.30%。

作者还算了一笔账。平均来看，真机强化学习比监督微调多 3.92 个百分点，在世界模型里往前推演又多 2.49 个，而同一个世界模型加上倒带，再多 11.21 个，比前两步加起来还多。

相对提升最大的是扔球，比 RISE 高 38.5%。作者的解释是，扔球的成败取决于出手那一下，比较同一状态下的不同出手，最容易找出更好的那个。

消融在表 2，只在抽积木上做。把世界模型冻住、不再更新，成功率从 69.33% 掉到 64.67%，平均连续抽出的块数从 2.6 掉到 1.9。

如果不用子树的平均值，而是直接拿单次推演的奖励当标签，成功率掉得更多，到 59.33%。作者认为，单次推演的结果随机性太大。

再看训练曲线。扔球任务，Rollback 从 25.56% 稳定涨到 40%，RISE 在第 2 小时反而先掉到 24.44%，最后是 28.89%。冰壶上，Rollback 从 51.11% 涨到 64.44%，RISE 只比起点高 1.48 个百分点。

这张图是冰壶上的一次倒带。在真实尝试的第 200 步回滚，真实的那一推把壶送进了大本营，想象里换一个更轻的推法，壶就停在了前面。这样就能确定，第二推是决定结果的那一步。

最吸引眼球的结果是和人比，每项都是三个人的平均。扔球，人的成功率是 31.48%，Rollback 训练 6 小时后超过人，8 小时到 40%。冰壶，人是 58.39%，Rollback 4 小时超过人，最后是 64.44%。

要注意，它最初学的是遥操作示范，而遥操作的成功率只有 16.53% 和 38.94%，远低于人。作者说，策略从来没看过人怎么玩，超过人的部分，来自它在想象里比较自己的不同动作。

最后是世界模型准不准，表 4。在冰壶的 64 个验证片段上，用同样的动作开环回放，校准过的世界模型，画面误差平均从 19.52 降到 12.96。

不过论文也写明了，这 64 个片段本来就在校准用的数据里，冻住的模型没见过，也就是说，校准版是在自己训练过的片段上接受测试的。

### 和以前方法的对比

和 RISE 这类方法比，Rollback 有三点不同，回到关键决策点分叉，不训练价值模型，世界模型边用边修正。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把倒带这个只有模拟器才有的能力，用到了真机强化学习上，而且和 RISE 用的是同一个世界模型，提升主要来自方法本身。

我的判断是，也要留个心眼。RISE 是作者自己复现的，每个任务总共只测 90 到 150 次，表 1 也没给置信区间，这 11 个点左右的差距，还需要别人复现。

### 局限

作者在附录里写了局限。第一，Rollback 依赖一个针对每个任务训练的奖励模型。

第二，世界模型的误差会沿着推演越积越大。在抽积木上，画面误差从前四分之一的 6.8，涨到后四分之一的 11.8，作者说，这和他们选择从真实观测分叉是一致的。

第三，每个策略只针对一个任务，只用一台机器人训练。

另外，按初始位置拆开看，Rollback 并不是处处最好。抽积木的第二个位置，它是 56%，监督微调和 RISE 都是 80%。冰壶的第五个位置，它是 20%，监督微调是 60%。

### 未来方向

作者提到三个方向，把成功判断器和世界模型一起学，扩展到多任务策略，以及让多台机器人共用一个搜索集群。

我的判断是，这套思路的上限，取决于世界模型能不能在接触多、时间长的任务上保持准确。像扔球，作者自己也说，成败基本由出手那一个动作片段决定。

我的判断是，对做真机强化学习的团队，这篇给了一个用显卡换真机试验的具体做法，真实数据只能靠加机器人，搜索却可以靠加显卡扩展。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from the robotics company AgiBot, with Beihang University and the National University of Singapore. It's called Rollback.

The one-line version: let the robot rewind inside a world model, go back to the moment that mattered, try a different action, and see what changes.

The paper reports that on three real-robot tasks, this beats the strongest baseline by ten point six seven, eleven point one one, and eleven point eight five percentage points.

### Problem

Some background first. Reinforcement learning on a real robot is expensive. Every attempt ties up the robot, and every failure needs a person to reset the scene.

So people turned to world models. Think of one as a learned simulator: give it an action, and it predicts what the cameras will see next. The policy can then practice in imagination.

But the authors point out that most of this work still behaves like real-world training. Roll a whole trajectory forward from the start, and throw the intermediate states away when it's done.

That wastes the one thing a world model can do and the real world can't: rewind. Once a real tower falls, it stays fallen. A world model can go back to a moment in the middle and try something else.

### Prior work

Prior work falls into two camps. One trains directly on the real robot, like RECAP. The cost problem stays: real interaction is slow, and resets are manual.

The other trains inside a world model, like RISE. It rolls forward in imagination and uses a learned progress value model to score actions.

The authors argue these methods struggle to improve steadily. Forward-only rollouts rarely revisit the decisions that matter, and a learned value model can be biased.

There's a subtler problem too. World models are usually trained offline and then frozen. But as a policy improves, it moves into exactly the states the simulator has seen least.

### Key idea

The key idea of Rollback is to turn the world model from a simulator that only plays forward into a training environment you can rewind.

This figure lines up three approaches. On the left, real-world RL, with costly physical interaction. In the middle, forward rollouts in a world model, with unstable training. On the right, Rollback: go back to key decisions, explore alternative futures, and learn from the difference in outcomes.

Here's why that helps. Actions that branch from the same moment share everything that came before. Only this one step differs. So if the outcomes differ, you can pin it on that action.

### Method

The method has three parts, shown in this overview. First, a tree search that backtracks. Second, using that tree to value each action. Third, the world model and the policy improving together.

Part one. The world model is GE-Sim 2.0. Give it an action chunk, and it predicts the next frames from three cameras, plus the robot's own state: seven arm joints and a gripper per arm.

Search starts from an existing trajectory, either a demonstration or one of the robot's own attempts. Each boundary between action chunks is a decision point you can branch from. The world-model state at each node is cached, so a new branch doesn't regenerate everything before it.

Where to branch is decided by Monte Carlo tree search, inspired by AlphaGo. Branches with higher success get explored more, and less-visited ones still get a chance.

The policy itself proposes the new actions. Each time the search returns to a node, it samples again with a fresh noise seed, so it explores actions the current policy considers plausible.

Each imagined rollout runs to the end, and a reward model says success or failure. That's the world judge from GE-Sim 2.0, which the authors fine-tune for each task on labeled robot videos.

Part two: valuing actions with the tree. How good is an action? Look at every continuation below it, and count the fraction that succeeded.

In the figure's example, three actions start from the same state. The first one's continuations succeed three times out of four, so zero point seven five. The second gets zero point five. The third fails every time, so zero.

Each value is then divided by the best sibling's value and sorted into five bins. If all siblings land in the same bin, there's nothing to compare, and the group is dropped. It's a lot like the group-relative comparison in GRPO, except the group here is a set of actions sampled from the same state.

The payoff is that there's no separate value model to train. The reward model only has to judge whether a finished rollout succeeded, which is easier than predicting success from a midway state.

How does the policy learn from the bins? It follows RECAP. The policy is pi zero point five, with the bin written into its prompt. Real trajectories go in too: successes get the top bin, failures the bottom. During search and deployment, the policy is always asked for top-bin actions.

Part three: the world model and the policy co-evolve. As the policy gets better, it wanders outside the world model's training data. The world model's predictions get worse there, and the policy can learn to exploit those errors.

The paper's example: a block that should topple, but the world model predicts it stays upright. Search would then label that failure as a success.

The fix is to keep fine-tuning the world model on the new real trajectories, successes and failures alike. Each update is four hundred steps of LoRA fine-tuning, then one hundred steps of distillation to keep generation fast. The authors call this test-time physics grounding: correcting the physics while the simulator is in use.

On the engineering side, all of this runs at once. Nothing waits for anything else, and data moves only through a shared pool. The experiments use forty-eight H200 GPUs, forty for search and eight for policy training, and a single physical robot.

### Results

The experiments run on AgiBot's Genie-02 robot, with three real tasks. First, block extraction: pull Jenga blocks out of a tower one at a time without knocking it over. Second, ball tossing: throw a ball into a bucket, which comes down to the throwing motion and the timing of the release. Third, tabletop curling: a human and the robot each deliver two stones, scored by how close they end up to the center.

Each task uses six starting positions, nine for curling. Each position gets fifteen trials, or twenty-five for block extraction.

All the baselines start from the same pi zero point five, with comparable compute. There's supervised fine-tuning, real-world RL with RECAP, and the authors' own reproduction of RISE on the same world model. RECAP and RISE also get one robot and about eight hours of training.

That point matters. RISE and Rollback share a world model, so the gap between them reflects backtracking search and tree-based values, not a better simulator.

Results are in Table one. Rollback is best on all three tasks: sixty-nine point three three percent on block extraction, forty percent on ball tossing, and sixty-four point four four percent on curling.

Averaged over the three, that's fifty-seven point nine three percent, versus forty-six point seven two for RISE, the strongest baseline, and forty point three for supervised fine-tuning alone.

The authors also break down where the gains come from. On average, real-world RL adds three point nine two points over supervised fine-tuning. Forward rollouts in the world model add another two point four nine. Adding backtracking on that same world model adds eleven point two one, more than the first two combined.

The biggest relative gain is on ball tossing, thirty-eight point five percent over RISE. The authors' explanation: the throw is decided by the release, and comparing different releases from the same state is the most direct way to find a better one.

The ablations are in Table two, on block extraction only. Freezing the world model drops success from sixty-nine point three three to sixty-four point six seven percent, and the average run of consecutive extractions from two point six to one point nine.

Using the reward from a single rollout as the label, instead of the subtree average, hurts more: success falls to fifty-nine point three three percent. The authors put this down to single rollouts being too noisy.

Now the training curves. On ball tossing, Rollback climbs steadily from twenty-five point five six to forty percent. RISE actually dips to twenty-four point four four after two hours and ends at twenty-eight point eight nine. On curling, Rollback goes from fifty-one point one one to sixty-four point four four, while RISE ends only one point four eight points above where it started.

This figure shows one rewind on curling. The search rolls back to step two hundred of a real attempt. The real push sends the stone into the house. An imagined alternative pushes more gently, and the stone stops short. That isolates the second push as the decision that settled the outcome.

The eye-catching result is the comparison with people, averaged over three human players. On ball tossing, humans hit thirty-one point four eight percent. Rollback passes them after six hours and reaches forty percent at eight. On curling, humans score fifty-eight point three nine percent. Rollback passes them after four hours and ends at sixty-four point four four.

Keep in mind it started from teleoperated demonstrations, and teleoperation itself only reached sixteen point five three and thirty-eight point nine four percent, far below the human players. The authors say the policy never sees the humans play. The margin comes from comparing its own alternative actions in imagination.

Finally, how accurate is the world model? Table four. Replaying the recorded actions of sixty-four curling validation episodes open-loop, the grounded world model cuts average pixel error from nineteen point five two to twelve point nine six.

The paper is upfront that those sixty-four episodes are part of the data the grounded model was adapted on, while the frozen model never saw them. In other words, the grounded model is being tested on episodes it was trained on.

### Comparison

Compared with methods like RISE, Rollback differs in three ways: it branches from key decision points, it trains no value model, and its world model keeps getting corrected.

My read is that the real contribution is taking rewind, something only a simulator can do, and putting it to work in real-robot RL. And because it shares a world model with RISE, the gain mostly comes from the method itself.

My read is also that some caution is warranted. RISE is the authors' own reproduction. Each task is tested on only ninety to one hundred fifty trials in total, and Table one has no confidence intervals. A gap of around eleven points needs independent replication.

### Limitations

The authors list the limitations in the appendix. First, Rollback depends on a reward model trained for each task.

Second, world-model error grows over a rollout. On block extraction, pixel error rises from six point eight in the first quarter to eleven point eight in the last, which the authors say is consistent with their choice to branch from real observations.

Third, each policy is trained for a single task, with one physical robot.

Also, broken down by starting position, Rollback isn't best everywhere. At block extraction's second position it scores fifty-six percent, while supervised fine-tuning and RISE both score eighty. At curling's fifth position it gets twenty percent, versus sixty for supervised fine-tuning.

### Future work

The authors name three directions: learn the success detector jointly with the world model, extend Rollback to multi-task policies, and scale to multiple robots sharing one search cluster.

My read is that the ceiling here depends on whether world models stay accurate over long, contact-heavy tasks. In ball tossing, by the authors' own account, a single action chunk around the release largely decides the outcome.

My read is that for teams doing real-robot RL, this paper shows a concrete way to trade GPUs for robot trials. Real data only grows with more robots, but search scales with more GPUs.
