## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇 Ropedia 团队的论文，名字叫 DONIC，讲的是用一个策略同时控制两只灵巧机器人手，照着人类的示范动作去操作物体。

先说结果。不做任何微调，同一个模型在没见过的轨迹上成功率 86.79%，在没见过的物体上 57.32%，论文报告，比两个基线里较强的那个分别高出 77.36 和 45.73 个百分点。

### 问题

先解释一下任务，叫参考跟踪，reference tracking。给机器人一段参考动作，包括两只手每一刻的手腕位置、手指关节角度，以及物体应该怎么移动，机器人要在物理仿真里把它真正做出来，让物体按这条轨迹动起来。

难点在于，生活里很多事要两只手配合，而且分工不对称，比如一只手扶住东西，另一只手去打开、旋转或者挪动它。

但以前的跟踪模型，基本只做单手。

作者说，做双手有两个挑战。第一是数据少，公开数据里，两只手持续同时接触物体、而且手和物体都完整追踪的序列很稀缺。

第二是训练难。一个共享策略要学很多段轨迹，每段里两只手的角色、接触的时机都在变。只看手的姿态准不准，不能保证物体真被拿动了，而要求两只手同时满足接触条件，又会把其中一只手的进展掩盖掉。

### 以前的方法

以前的路线大概两类。一类是一条轨迹训一个策略，比如 ManipTrans、DexMachina、ConTrack，用强化学习去修正从人手映射过来的动作，换一条新轨迹，就要重新训练。

另一类是通用策略。比如 DexTrack，把强化学习和专门策略挖出来的成功示范结合起来，Dexplore 则在参考动作周围扩大探索，但它们主要是单手。双手方面，BiDexHD 是每个操作类别单独训一个策略，泛化范围更窄。

### 核心想法

DONIC 的思路是，一个策略管两只手，跨操作类别、跨不同来源的人类数据一起训练，测试时直接跟踪新的参考动作，不再训练。

为此它有三块。一个新数据集 BiHOI，专门补双手数据，一个以物体为中心、按手区分的奖励，还有一个叫 Clip-Centered Advantages 的训练技巧，简称 CCA。

### 方法

先看数据。BiHOI 用 32 台标定好的 RGB 相机围着桌面拍，一共 1575 段序列，229 个物体，14.77 小时。物体包括家居用品、容器、餐具、工具和电子产品。

它的特点是两只手同时接触多。在至少一只手接触物体的帧里，65.91% 是两只手同时接触，作者分析的公开数据里，最高的是 H2O，38.57%。

拍完之后，他们重建出两只手和物体的三维运动，再把人手动作映射到 Wuji 机器人手上，和物体轨迹配成机器人能用的参考动作。

再看控制。策略看到的是机器人自身状态、物体位置和速度、接下来的参考目标、物体形状这些信息，输出的不是绝对动作，而是对下一帧参考姿态的一个有界修正，再交给 PD 控制器去执行。

第一个关键设计是奖励。物体跟踪的奖励全程都在，看物体的位置、朝向和速度是否和参考一致，速度这一项，是为了惩罚参考在动、物体却停着不动的解。

接触奖励则按手来算。只有参考动作要求某只手的指尖去碰物体时，这只手才参与接触奖励，再在参与的手之间取加权平均。这样一只手没接触上，不会把另一只手的奖励抹掉，该松手的时候，也不会因为还贴着物体而得分。

第二个关键设计是 CCA。先解释优势值，就是一个动作比预期好多少，强化学习靠它决定往哪个方向更新。很多段轨迹混在一起训练时，价值网络对不同轨迹估得准不准不一样，会让每段轨迹的优势值整体偏高或偏低，难的轨迹可能一直拿到负的优势值，哪怕里面有相对成功的动作。

CCA 的做法很简单，算完标准的 GAE 优势之后，按轨迹分组，每个值减去这段轨迹的平均值。同一段里动作之间的相对好坏不变，但每段轨迹特有的整体偏移被去掉了。它只减均值，不除标准差，也不改奖励和价值网络。

第三块是训练循环，沿用 DexTrack 的做法。先给每条参考轨迹训一个专门策略，成功的执行经过回放验证，存进示范库。通用策略一边用 PPO 做强化学习，一边对示范库做行为克隆，也就是直接模仿成功的动作。通用策略自己的成功执行，验证后也会加回示范库。

### 实验和效果

实验设置。训练数据来自 GRAB、GigaHands、OakInk2、TACO 和 H2O 五个公开数据集，筛完一共 1845 段，全部在 Isaac Gym 仿真里，控制两只 Wuji 机器人手。

测试集也是从公开数据里预留的，一个是训练物体的 53 段新轨迹，一个是 164 段涉及没见过物体的片段，都不参与训练。

成功的标准比较严。物体和手腕的位置误差、角度误差、手指关节误差，两只手都要各自达标才算成功。另一个指标叫完成度，看的是每一帧里有多少比例两只手同时达标。

对比的基线是 DexTrack 和 Dexplore 的双手改造版。作者用它们公开的代码，改成控制两只 Wuji 手，用同样的公开训练数据、同样的仿真器和测试集。

结果在表 2。在没见过的轨迹上，DONIC 成功率 86.79%，完成度 67.62%，较强的基线 Dexplore-bi 成功率只有 9.43%，DexTrack-bi 是 0。

在没见过的物体上，DONIC 成功率 57.32%，Dexplore-bi 是 11.59%。手腕位置误差也从 19.46 厘米降到 4.58 厘米。

也有一项没赢，在没见过的轨迹上，DONIC 的物体旋转误差比 Dexplore-bi 大。作者强调，成功率和完成度要求两只手都达标，所以这些提升反映的是两只手真的配合成功，而不只是一只手准。

消融实验也在这张表里。去掉 CCA，成功率在两个测试集上分别从 86.79% 掉到 81.13%，从 57.32% 掉到 48.17%。去掉接触门控，也就是判断接触时只看力，不再要求指尖离物体 3 厘米以内，成功率分别掉到 81.13% 和 48.78%。

再加上自己采的 BiHOI，训练集从 1845 段变成 3420 段，两个测试集上七项指标全部变好，成功率到了 88.68% 和 59.76%。

### 和以前方法的对比

总结一下区别。逐条轨迹训练的方法，换一条轨迹就得重新训。DexTrack、Dexplore 这类通用跟踪器主要为单手设计，改成双手后，在这个设置下成功率都不到 12%。DONIC 的贡献，是一个检查点、不微调，就能跟踪新的双手轨迹和新物体。

我的判断是，最值得借鉴的是两个很朴素的训练细节，按手、按需要去给接触奖励，以及按轨迹去掉优势值的整体偏移。它们并不依赖双手这个场景，多种任务混在一起做强化学习时，都可能用得上。

我的判断是，也要看清这个对比的分量。两个基线都是单手方法改成双手的版本，DexTrack-bi 成功率是 0，至少说明这组对比里基线偏弱。论文没有和 BiDexHD 这类原生双手方法在同一个测试集上比，所以 77 个百分点的领先，要打个折扣来看。

### 局限

作者自己写明了局限。定量实验全部在仿真里，是单个刚体物体的参考跟踪，而且依赖重建、再映射出来的参考动作。

没有参考动作、只给目标的操作，以及没见过的操作类别，都不在评测范围内。多物体的录像只保留一个主要物体，可动的和会变形的物体也没有单独建模。

接触判断用的是净接触力加距离的近似，而净接触力并不能确认，到底碰到的是哪个东西。

另外，论文第一张图里有一栏真实世界评测的画面，是金属工具、托盘和盒子，但正文没有给出真实机器人上的定量结果。

### 未来方向

作者说，下一步要扩展到更长时间、多个物体的操作，在独立的基准上评测，并研究迁移到真实的双手机器人上。

我的判断是，这篇论文把双手灵巧操作的数据和训练流程都搭起来了，BiHOI 这类双手同时接触多的数据，对后面做双手的团队很实在。不过加入 BiHOI 只带来两个百分点左右的成功率提升，数据的价值，还要在更难的测试上验证。

我的判断是，真正的考验是上真机。仿真里跟得住，不代表在真实世界的摩擦、遮挡和传感器噪声下也行，这一步论文还没有给出答案。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from the team at Ropedia. It's called DONIC, and it's about one policy that drives two dexterous robot hands at once, following human demonstrations to manipulate objects.

Here's the headline result. With no fine-tuning, a single model succeeds on eighty-six point seven nine percent of unseen trajectories and fifty-seven point three two percent of clips with unseen objects. The paper reports that's seventy-seven point three six and forty-five point seven three percentage points above the stronger of its two baselines.

### Problem

First, the task. It's called reference tracking. You hand the robot a reference motion: where each wrist is, what angle every finger joint has, and how the object should move, frame by frame. The robot has to actually execute that in a physics simulator, so the object really follows the path.

What makes it hard is that a lot of everyday work needs two hands, and the hands don't do the same job. One hand holds something steady while the other opens it, turns it, or moves it.

But the existing trackers are mostly single-hand.

The authors name two obstacles. The first is data. Public datasets have very few fully tracked sequences where both hands stay in contact with the object for a sustained stretch.

The second is training. One shared policy has to learn many clips, and in each one the hands swap roles and make contact at different times. Accurate hand poses don't guarantee the object actually moves. But demand that both hands meet the contact conditions together, and you hide the progress one hand is making on its own.

### Prior work

Prior work splits roughly in two. One camp trains a policy per trajectory, like ManipTrans, DexMachina and ConTrack, using reinforcement learning to fix up motion transferred from human hands. New trajectory, new training run.

The other camp builds generalist policies. DexTrack combines reinforcement learning with successful demonstrations mined by trajectory-specific controllers, and Dexplore widens exploration around the reference motion. But these are mostly single-hand. On the two-hand side, BiDexHD trains a separate policy for each operation category, which is a narrower kind of generalization.

### Key idea

DONIC's idea: one policy for both hands, trained jointly across operation categories and across different sources of human data, then at test time it just tracks new references. No more training.

To get there it has three pieces. A new dataset, BiHOI, to fill the two-hand data gap. An object-centered reward computed per hand. And a training trick called Clip-Centered Advantages, or CCA.

### Method

Start with the data. BiHOI is shot with thirty-two calibrated RGB cameras around a tabletop. It has one thousand five hundred seventy-five sequences, two hundred twenty-nine objects, and fourteen point seven seven hours of interaction. The objects include household items, containers, utensils, tools and electronics.

What sets it apart is how often both hands are in contact. Among frames where at least one hand touches the object, sixty-five point nine one percent have both hands touching. The highest of the public datasets the authors analyzed is H2O, at thirty-eight point five seven.

After capture, they reconstruct both hands and the object in 3D, then map the human hand motion onto Wuji robot hands, paired with the object's trajectory, to get references a robot can use.

Now the controller. The policy sees the robot's own state, the object's pose and velocity, the upcoming reference targets, and the object's shape. It doesn't output absolute actions. It outputs a bounded correction to the next reference pose, which a PD controller then executes.

The first key design is the reward. The object-tracking reward is always on. It checks whether the object's position, orientation and velocity match the reference, and the velocity term is there to punish solutions where the reference is moving but the object just sits still.

Contact reward is computed per hand. A hand only joins the contact reward when the reference says its fingertips should be touching the object, and the engaged hands are then averaged with weights. So if one hand misses contact, it doesn't wipe out the other hand's reward. And when a hand is supposed to let go, it doesn't get paid for staying stuck to the object.

The second key design is CCA. Quick definition: the advantage says how much better an action was than expected, and reinforcement learning uses it to decide which way to update. When many clips are trained together, the value network is more accurate on some clips than others, which shifts each clip's advantages up or down as a block. Hard clips can end up with consistently negative advantages, even for the moves that went relatively well.

The fix is simple. After computing the standard GAE advantages, group them by clip and subtract each clip's mean. The relative ranking of actions within a clip is untouched, but the clip-specific offset is gone. It only subtracts the mean, doesn't divide by a standard deviation, and doesn't touch the reward or the value network.

The third piece is the training loop, borrowed from DexTrack. First, a specialist policy is trained for each reference, and its successful runs are replay-verified and stored in a demonstration library. The generalist then trains with PPO reinforcement learning while also doing behavior cloning on that library, which just means imitating the successful actions directly. As the generalist succeeds on its own, those runs are verified and added back to the library.

### Results

The setup. Training data comes from five public datasets, GRAB, GigaHands, OakInk2, TACO and H2O, filtered down to one thousand eight hundred forty-five clips. Everything runs in the Isaac Gym simulator, with two Wuji robot hands.

The test sets are also held out from the public data. One has fifty-three new trajectories of training objects. The other has one hundred sixty-four clips involving objects never seen in training. Neither is used for training.

The success bar is strict. Object and wrist position error, rotation error, and finger-joint error all have to pass, for each hand independently. A second metric, completion, measures the share of frames where both hands pass at the same time.

The baselines are two-hand adaptations of DexTrack and Dexplore. The authors took their released code, adapted it to drive two Wuji hands, and used the same public training data, the same simulator, and the same test splits.

The results are in Table 2. On unseen trajectories, DONIC hits eighty-six point seven nine percent success and sixty-seven point six two percent completion. The stronger baseline, Dexplore-bi, succeeds only nine point four three percent of the time, and DexTrack-bi scores zero.

On unseen objects, DONIC's success rate is fifty-seven point three two percent, against eleven point five nine for Dexplore-bi. Wrist position error drops from nineteen point four six centimetres to four point five eight.

There's one metric it doesn't win. On unseen trajectories, DONIC's object rotation error is higher than Dexplore-bi's. The authors stress that success and completion both require both hands to pass, so the gains reflect hands actually working together, not just one accurate hand.

The ablations are in the same table. Remove CCA, and success drops from eighty-six point seven nine to eighty-one point one three on unseen trajectories, and from fifty-seven point three two to forty-eight point one seven on unseen objects. Remove the contact gate, meaning contact is judged by force alone, without requiring the fingertip to be within three centimetres of the object, and success falls to eighty-one point one three and forty-eight point seven eight.

Add their own BiHOI data, and the training set grows from one thousand eight hundred forty-five clips to three thousand four hundred twenty. All seven metrics improve on both test sets, with success reaching eighty-eight point six eight and fifty-nine point seven six percent.

### Comparison

So how does this differ from what came before? Per-trajectory methods have to retrain for every new trajectory. Generalist trackers like DexTrack and Dexplore were designed mainly for one hand, and adapted to two hands in this setup, both stay under twelve percent success. DONIC's contribution is one checkpoint, no fine-tuning, tracking new two-hand trajectories and new objects.

My read is that the most reusable parts are two plain training details: giving contact reward per hand, only when it's needed, and removing each clip's advantage offset. Neither depends on the two-hand setting, and both could help whenever many different tasks are mixed into one reinforcement learning run.

My read is also that the comparison deserves some caution. Both baselines are single-hand methods adapted to two hands, and DexTrack-bi scoring zero at least suggests the baselines here are weak. The paper doesn't compare against a native two-hand method like BiDexHD on the same test set, so take the seventy-seven point lead with a discount.

### Limitations

The authors spell out the limitations. All quantitative experiments are in simulation, tracking a single rigid object, and they rely on reconstructed, retargeted reference motions.

Goal-only manipulation without a reference, and operation categories never seen in training, are outside the evaluation. Multi-object recordings keep only one main object, and articulated or deformable objects aren't modeled as such.

Contact is judged with a proxy of net contact force plus distance, and net force can't tell you what the fingertip actually hit.

Also, the paper's first figure has a panel of real-world evaluation shots, with a metal tool, a tray and a box, but the text reports no quantitative results on real robots.

### Future work

Next, the authors want to extend DONIC to longer, multi-object manipulation, evaluate it on an independent benchmark, and study transfer to physical two-hand robots.

My read is that this paper sets up both the data and the training pipeline for two-hand dexterous manipulation, and data like BiHOI, with lots of simultaneous two-hand contact, is a real asset for teams working on this. Still, adding BiHOI raised success by only about two percentage points, so its value has to be shown on harder tests.

My read is that the real test is hardware. Tracking well in simulation doesn't mean it holds up under real friction, occlusion and sensor noise, and the paper doesn't answer that yet.
