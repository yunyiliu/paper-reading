## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇卡内基梅隆大学牵头的论文，合作方还有博世人工智能中心、英伟达等，名字叫 EgoX，标题里有个很有意思的词，叫 Embodiment Dreaming，可以翻成具身做梦。

它想回答的问题是，人、人形机器人、四足机器人、机械臂，身体完全不一样，它们的操作经验，能不能放在一起，训练一个共用的模型。

### 问题

先说背景。语言和视觉模型靠大规模预训练进步很快，机器人也想走这条路。但现在常见的做法，是每种机器人、每个任务、每个场景都单独采数据、单独训练，成本高，经验也没法复用。

难点在于，不同身体的数据差别太大。形态不同，摄像头视角不同，关节数量不同，动作的含义也不同。人手有五根手指，夹爪只有两根，有的机械臂上装的干脆是一把固定的铲子。怎么把这些经验合在一起，又不互相干扰，就是这篇要解决的问题。

### 以前的方法

以前的跨本体学习，大致有几种做法。一种是共享一个主干网络，每种机器人配自己的输入编码器或者动作输出头。一种是只用一个动作头，再告诉它当前是哪种机器人。还有一种是用图像编辑，把不同机器人在画面里的样子对齐。

作者的评价是，这些方法要么覆盖的身体种类比较窄，要么给每种身体一整套单独的接口，身体内部哪些部分可以共享，并没有说清楚。

另一条线，是用人的第一视角数据来教机器人，也就是 egocentric 数据，戴着头显，录下人自己干活的画面和手的动作。这类工作大多是把人的经验，迁移到某一种目标机器人上。

### 核心想法

EgoX 的出发点可以归结成一句话，迁移应该发生在语义上对应的部分之间，而感知和控制的接口，保留每种身体自己的。

什么叫语义上对应。整个身体虽然差很多，但很多部件扮演的角色是一样的。人和机器人都有手腕，都有末端，比如手指尖，或者夹爪和工具的接触点。

论文这张图，是同一个抹酱任务，分别由人、真实的人形机器人、仿真里的人形机器人和轮式双臂机器人完成，每一列是对应的阶段。

所以作者把人、真实机器人、仿真机器人放在同等的地位，都当作一种本体，英文叫 embodiment，每种本体贡献自己的观察和动作。

### 方法

方法分三块。第一块是统一的数据表示。所有本体用同一套坐标约定，躯干的位置，放在一个贴着地面的根坐标系里，手腕的位置，放在主摄像头所在的第一视角坐标系里，指尖的位置，放在各自手腕的坐标系里。

末端用的是关键点，也就是指尖这些部位的三维位置，而不是电机关节的角度。这样五指手、夹爪和工具，就能用可以互相比较的几何方式来描述。

第二块是模型，叫 MXT+，模块化的跨本体 Transformer。在每种本体里，每一种观察，比如图像、躯干、左手腕、右手指尖，都有自己的 tokenizer，也就是把信号转成 token 的编码器。每一种动作，也有自己的动作专家负责输出。

中间是所有本体共享的 Transformer 主干，跨本体的知识主要靠它传递。模型每次输出接下来 60 步的动作，按每秒 30 步算，就是 2 秒。

第三块，就是标题里的 Embodiment Dreaming，可以理解成，让模型预想自己的身体接下来会变成什么样。

为什么要加这个。只靠模仿演示里的动作来训练，并不要求模型去预判身体状态会怎么变化。所以作者加了一个辅助任务，从当前的表示出发，去预测未来几个时刻的第一视角图像、机器人关节状态和末端的关节状态，压缩成的隐向量。

预测的目标不是像素，而是由编码器的一个滑动平均副本算出来的，而且不往目标那边回传梯度，这个思路和 JEPA 系列模型一样。这样既不用重建高维的图像，目标本身也变化得比较慢，比较稳。

关键在于，这个做梦的分支只在训练时用，部署的时候不需要它，也就不增加推理的开销。

训练分两步。先在混合数据上预训练，每一步从每种本体各取一批样本，一起更新。目标本体在目标任务上的演示，不放进预训练，留到第二步微调的时候才用。

### 实验和效果

数据都是团队自己采的。人和真实机器人的数据，都用 Apple Vision Pro 采集，人是自己动手干活，机器人是由人戴着头显遥操作。总共 4851 条轨迹，34.38 个小时，其中人的演示有 2396 条，接近一半。

测试用了六种机器人，五种真实的，一种仿真的。有宇树 G1 人形机器人配五指灵巧手，xArm7 机械臂配四指的 LEAP Hand，前腿装了夹爪的四足机器人 LocoMan，Galaxea 的 R1 Lite 轮式双臂机器人，装着固定铲子的宇树 Z1 机械臂，还有仿真里的 G1。

任务有五个，从冰箱或者台面上拿酱、再把酱抹到面包上的长流程任务，把抹刀收进容器，按颜色分积木，把 T 形块插进槽里，还有铲猫砂。任务和机器人搭配起来，一共 13 个组合。

对比的基线有两个，一个是 ACT，只在目标数据上从零训练，一个是 π0.5，从它自己的预训练权重出发，在同样的目标数据上微调。

每个真实组合测 20 次，仿真组合测 50 次，分成分布内和分布外两种条件。分布外会换物体的形状、大小、颜色，换容器、酱或者盘子，或者换摆放位置。评分用的是任务进度分，每完成一个阶段给分，整个任务成功再加一分，最后归一化到 0 到 1。

结果在这张图。跨本体预训练让 MXT+ 的平均任务进度，从 0.63 提到 0.80。分布内 13 个组合里有 11 个提升，分布外 13 个全部提升。

分布外的提升更明显，从 0.53 提到 0.77。在所有方法里，预训练后的 MXT+ 平均分最高，13 个组合里有 12 个排第一。

换成成功率来看，排序也一样。预训练让 MXT+ 的平均成功率从 0.35 提到 0.56，π0.5 是 0.27，ACT 是 0.11。

预训练数据的组成也有影响。只用人的数据预训练，分布外 6 个组合里就有 5 个提升。再加上其他机器人的数据，再加上目标机器人在其他任务上的数据，分布外的平均提升越来越大。全部加上之后，和不预训练相比，分布内高 0.12，分布外高 0.23。

再看做梦这一项。把做梦相关的模块全部去掉，就是原来的 MXT。在 7 个组合上，加上做梦，平均任务进度从 0.63 提到 0.78，7 个组合全部提升。

提升最大的是 G1 人形机器人铲猫砂，按附录里的成功率，20 次里从成功 3 次，变成成功 16 次。

### 和以前方法的对比

模块化到底有没有用，作者也做了对照。粗粒度的版本，所有摄像头共用一个 tokenizer，所有位姿和关节信号拼在一起用一个，所有动作用一个专家输出，其余都不变。

结果是，预训练之后，MXT+ 在每个设置上都不比粗粒度版本差。而粗粒度版本经过预训练，在 xArm7 铲猫砂和两个仿真厨房任务上，反而变差了。收抹刀这个任务，预训练后的粗粒度版本任务进度直接是零，MXT+ 还保留了相当一部分。

作者的解释是，这个任务一开始要先走路，速度指令很关键，但它在 51 维的动作里只占很小一部分。作者推测，单独的动作专家，有助于把这一部分保住。

作者还看了模型内部。在铲猫砂任务上，比较不同本体在共享主干里的表示，用一种叫 Gromov-Wasserstein 的距离，衡量它们几何结构的相似度。预训练让平均距离下降了百分之 21.3，做梦让它下降了百分之 38.5，说明不同的身体做同一件事，内部表示变得更像了。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把共享什么切得很细，不是整个身体对整个身体，而是手腕对手腕、指尖对指尖，再加上一个只在训练时用的做梦目标。微调时的 MXT+ 只有 5000 万到 9000 万参数，π0.5 是 34 亿，在这些任务上，小模型反而排在前面。

还有一点要注意。分布外测试里的一些新物体和新摆放，在其他本体的预训练数据里出现过，尤其是人的演示。论文自己写明，分布外的结果应该理解为跨本体迁移的测试，而不是对所有训练数据里都没出现过的情况的泛化。

我的判断是，π0.5 微调时只用了目标数据，没见过这些演示，所以分布外和 π0.5 的对比，对 EgoX 是偏有利的。另外，每个真实组合只测 20 次，其中分布外只有 7 次，单个组合的数字波动会比较大。

### 局限

作者自己列了四条局限。

第一，数据都是团队自己采的，规模有限，在大得多的数据集上效果怎么样，还没有验证。

第二，仿真只有一种人形机器人和两个厨房任务，仿真经验的多样性有限。

第三，接入一种新的身体，还需要人工指定模态的对应关系和坐标变换，还要接上它自己的控制接口。

第四，各个本体的数据量差别很大，但预训练每一步，从每种本体取的样本一样多。像四足机器人 LocoMan，总共只有 37 条演示，会被反复使用，可能过拟合，反过来拖累共享的预训练。

### 未来方向

对应这些局限，作者提的方向是，更大、更均衡的数据，更广的仿真覆盖，自动接入新的本体，以及考虑数据量和多样性的采样策略。

我的判断是，这篇给跨本体学习提供了一个很实用的思路。人戴着头显干活录下来的数据，和各种机器人的数据，用同一套手腕、指尖的语言记下来，就能互相帮忙。人的数据最容易采，这条路值得继续看。

我的判断是，真正的考验是规模。现在是 34 个小时、团队自己采的数据，接入新机器人还要手工配置。数据量放大很多倍之后，这种细粒度模块化的优势还在不在，论文还没有回答。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is led by Carnegie Mellon University, with co-authors from the Bosch Center for AI and NVIDIA among others. It's called EgoX, and its title contains a phrase worth remembering: embodiment dreaming.

The question behind it is simple. A human, a humanoid, a robot dog and a robot arm have completely different bodies. Can their experience still be pooled to train one shared model?

### Problem

Some background first. Language and vision models got much better through large-scale pretraining, and robotics wants the same recipe. But the usual practice is still to collect data and train a model separately for every robot, every task and every scene. That's expensive, and the experience never gets reused.

The hard part is how different the data is. Bodies differ, camera viewpoints differ, joint counts differ, and actions mean different things. A human hand has five fingers, a gripper has two, and one of the arms in this paper just has a scoop bolted on. Combining all that without the sources getting in each other's way is the problem this paper takes on.

### Prior work

Earlier cross-embodiment work falls into a few camps. Some share one backbone and give each robot its own input encoders or action heads. Some use a single action head that is told which robot it's driving. Others edit the images so different robots look alike on screen.

The authors' critique is that these methods either cover a fairly narrow range of bodies, or give each body its own complete interface, which leaves open how much the parts inside a body can actually share.

A separate line of work learns from egocentric human data: a person wears a headset and records their own view and hand motion while doing a task. Most of that work focuses on transferring human experience to one particular target robot.

### Key idea

EgoX starts from one premise. Transfer should happen between parts that play the same role, while sensing and control interfaces stay specific to each body.

What does "play the same role" mean? Bodies differ a lot as a whole, but many of their parts do corresponding jobs. Humans and robots both have wrists, and both have end-effectors: fingertips, or the contact points of grippers and tools.

This figure shows the same sauce-spreading task done by a human, a real humanoid, a simulated humanoid and a wheeled two-armed robot. Each column is the matching stage of the task.

So the authors put humans, real robots and simulated robots on equal footing. Each is an embodiment, and each contributes its own observations and actions.

### Method

The method has three parts. Part one is a shared data format. Every embodiment uses the same coordinate conventions: torso pose in a ground-level root frame, wrist poses in the frame of the main egocentric camera, and fingertip positions in each wrist's own frame.

End-effectors are described by keypoints, the 3D positions of things like fingertips, rather than by motor joint angles. That gives hands, grippers and tools a comparable geometric description.

Part two is the model, MXT+, a modular cross-embodiment Transformer. Inside each embodiment, every kind of observation, say the camera image, the torso, the left wrist or the right fingertips, gets its own tokenizer, the encoder that turns a signal into tokens. And every kind of action gets its own action expert to produce it.

In the middle sits a Transformer trunk shared by all embodiments, and that's the main path for cross-embodiment transfer. Each prediction is a chunk of the next sixty actions, which at thirty steps per second is two seconds.

Part three is the embodiment dreaming from the title. Think of it as making the model anticipate what its own body will look like next.

Why add it? Imitating demonstrated actions doesn't require the model to anticipate how its body's state will change. So the authors add an auxiliary task: from the current representation, predict compressed latent versions of the egocentric image, the robot's joint states and the end-effector's joint states at a few future moments.

The targets aren't pixels. They come from a slowly updated moving-average copy of the encoders, with no gradient flowing into the target, the same idea used in the JEPA family of models. So there's no high-dimensional image reconstruction, and the target itself changes slowly and stably.

The key point is that the dreaming branch is used only in training. At deployment it's dropped, so it adds no inference cost.

Training has two stages. Pretraining runs on the mixed data, and each step takes a batch from every embodiment and updates on all of them together. Demonstrations of the target task on the target robot are held out of pretraining and used only for fine-tuning in stage two.

### Results

All the data was collected by the team. Human and real-robot data were both recorded with an Apple Vision Pro: humans doing the tasks with their own hands, and robots teleoperated by a person wearing the headset. In total that's four thousand eight hundred fifty-one trajectories, thirty-four point three eight hours, and two thousand three hundred ninety-six of them, close to half, are human demonstrations.

Six robots were tested, five real and one simulated. There's a Unitree G1 humanoid with five-fingered hands, an xArm7 arm with the four-fingered LEAP Hand, the LocoMan quadruped with grippers on its front legs, Galaxea's R1 Lite wheeled two-armed robot, a Unitree Z1 arm with a fixed scoop, and a simulated G1.

There are five tasks: a long kitchen task where the robot gets sauce from a fridge or a counter and spreads it on bread, putting a spreader away into a container, sorting blocks by color, inserting a T-shaped block into a slot, and scooping cat litter. Paired with the robots, that's thirteen task and robot combinations.

There are two baselines. ACT is trained from scratch on the target data only. Pi zero point five starts from its own pretrained weights and is fine-tuned on the same target data.

Each real combination gets twenty trials, and each simulated one gets fifty, split into in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions. Out of distribution means new object shapes, sizes or colors, a different container, sauce or plate, or new starting positions. Scoring uses a task progress score: points for each stage completed, one bonus point for full success, normalized to between zero and one.

The results are in this figure. Cross-embodiment pretraining lifts MXT+'s average task progress from zero point six three to zero point eight zero. In distribution, eleven of thirteen combinations improve, and out of distribution, all thirteen do.

The out-of-distribution gain is bigger, from zero point five three to zero point seven seven. Across all methods, pretrained MXT+ has the highest average and ranks first on twelve of the thirteen combinations.

Success rates tell the same story. Pretraining raises MXT+'s average success rate from zero point three five to zero point five six, against zero point two seven for pi zero point five and zero point one one for ACT.

What goes into pretraining matters too. Pretraining on human data alone already improves out-of-distribution performance on five of six combinations. Adding other robots' data, and then the target robot's data from other tasks, makes the average out-of-distribution gain grow. With everything included, the model beats no pretraining by zero point one two in distribution and zero point two three out of distribution.

Now the dreaming. Remove all the dreaming components and you get the earlier model, MXT. Across seven combinations, adding dreaming raises average task progress from zero point six three to zero point seven eight, and all seven improve.

The biggest jump is the G1 humanoid scooping cat litter. By the success rates in the appendix, it goes from three successes out of twenty trials to sixteen.

### Comparison

Does the modular design actually matter? The authors test a coarse version: one tokenizer for all cameras, one for all pose and joint signals concatenated together, and one expert for all actions, with everything else unchanged.

After pretraining, MXT+ matches or beats the coarse version in every setting. The coarse version, meanwhile, gets worse with pretraining on the xArm7 scooping task and both simulated kitchen tasks. On the spreader cleanup task, the pretrained coarse version scores zero task progress, while MXT+ keeps a good share of its performance.

The authors' explanation is that this task starts with walking, so the velocity command is critical, yet it's a small slice of a fifty-one-dimensional action space. They suggest a dedicated action expert helps keep it from getting lost.

They also look inside the model. On the scooping task, they compare how different embodiments are represented in the shared trunk, using the Gromov-Wasserstein distance to measure how similar their geometric structure is. Pretraining cuts the average distance by twenty-one point three percent, and dreaming cuts it by thirty-eight point five percent. Different bodies doing the same task end up with more similar internal representations.

My read is that the most valuable thing here is how finely the paper decides what to share: not whole body to whole body, but wrist to wrist and fingertip to fingertip, plus a dreaming objective that only exists during training. The fine-tuned MXT+ has fifty to ninety million parameters, pi zero point five has three point four billion, and on these tasks the small model comes out ahead.

One caveat matters. Some of the new objects and layouts in the out-of-distribution tests do appear in other embodiments' pretraining data, especially the human demonstrations. The paper says plainly that these results should be read as a test of cross-embodiment transfer, not of generalization to situations absent from all training data.

My read is that since pi zero point five was fine-tuned only on the target data and never saw those demonstrations, the out-of-distribution comparison with it tilts in EgoX's favor. Also, each real combination has only twenty trials, just seven of them out of distribution, so individual numbers can swing a lot.

### Limitations

The authors list four limitations.

First, all the data was collected by the team, so the scale is limited, and performance on much larger datasets hasn't been tested.

Second, the simulation side covers one humanoid and two kitchen tasks, which limits the variety of simulated experience.

Third, adding a new body still means manually specifying which modalities correspond, setting up the coordinate transforms, and wiring in its own control interface.

Fourth, the embodiments have very different amounts of data, yet pretraining takes the same number of samples from each one at every step. The LocoMan quadruped has only thirty-seven demonstrations in total, so they get reused a lot, which may cause overfitting and drag down the shared pretraining.

### Future work

Matching those limits, the authors point to larger and more balanced datasets, broader simulation coverage, automated integration of new embodiments, and sampling strategies that account for how much data each source has and how diverse it is.

My read is that this gives cross-embodiment learning a practical recipe. Data from a person wearing a headset and data from many robots, written down in the same language of wrists and fingertips, can help each other. Human data is the easiest to collect, so this direction is worth following.

My read is that the real test is scale. This is thirty-four hours of in-house data, and each new robot still needs hand configuration. Whether fine-grained modularity keeps its edge once the data grows many times larger is a question the paper doesn't answer yet.
