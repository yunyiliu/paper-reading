## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇香港科技大学（广州）牵头的论文，合作方有 OLA Dimensions、北京智源人工智能研究院和清华大学。论文叫 SLA，意思是空间、语言、动作模型。

它想回答的问题是，机器人大模型看世界基本是二维的，能不能不改模型结构，在大规模预训练阶段，就让它学会三维空间感。

### 问题

先说背景。VLA，也就是视觉、语言、动作模型，是现在机器人操作里很受关注的一类做法。它拿一个预训练好的视觉语言模型，再接一个专门输出动作的模块，叫动作专家，看画面、听指令，然后控制机械臂。

问题在于，这类模型的视觉能力，大多来自二维的图文数据，预训练时并没有专门学三维几何。可是抓东西、递东西，靠的恰恰是精确的空间位置。

作者做了一个探测实验。把现成的 VLA 模型 OpenVLA-OFT 完全冻住，只训练一个小的深度预测头，看能不能从它的视觉特征里读出深度。

这张图就是结果。没做对齐的特征，读出来的深度图基本是一团噪声，说明里面没有像样的三维结构。做了空间对齐的特征，读出来的深度图就明显好得多。

### 以前的方法

以前补三维能力，最直接的做法，是在下游微调时加上深度图或者点云输入。论文列了三个难处，可靠的三维传感数据难拿，常常有噪声、不完整，不同机器人的传感器类型、位置和标定情况差别很大，而且现有的大规模机器人数据集，有一部分根本没有深度信息。

另一条路，是作者团队之前的工作，叫 Spatial Forcing。它不加输入，而是在训练时，让 VLA 的视觉特征去对齐一个三维模型的特征，这叫隐式对齐，不需要深度数据。

但 Spatial Forcing 只用在下游微调阶段，空间能力是给某个具体任务临时加上去的，没有变成基础模型本身的能力。

### 核心想法

SLA 的想法很直接，把这种隐式空间对齐，从微调阶段提前到继续预训练阶段。继续预训练，就是在一个已经训好的基础模型上，用更多数据再练一轮，保留原来的知识，同时补上新的能力。

他们从 π0.5 这个预训练好的机器人策略模型出发，在大规模机器人数据上继续预训练，同时加上空间对齐，到了下游微调，再继续保留这个对齐。

关键在于，这套监督不改模型结构，也不动动作生成的部分。所以可以直接复用 π0.5 的权重，部署的时候，模型和原来一模一样。

### 方法

具体怎么做。先把机器人的多视角图像，送进一个冻结的三维感知模型，作者用的是 OmniVGGT。它给每张图输出像素级的三维特征，作为老师信号。

然后把 VLA 中间层的视觉 token，经过一个小的投影网络，和老师特征算余弦相似度，让两者尽量一致。总的训练目标，就是原来的动作损失，加上一个带权重的对齐损失。

为什么选 OmniVGGT。因为混在一起的数据集，有的带相机参数和深度图，有的只有普通彩色图像。OmniVGGT 能接收任意组合的几何信息，缺的部分用占位 token 补上，有多少就用多少。

第二个设计，叫自适应层选择。以前要手动挑某一层视觉特征去对齐，但不同数据适合的层不一样。SLA 给每一层的每个视觉 token 都学一个门控，自动决定这一层贡献多少，再把各层加起来去对齐。

第三个设计，叫状态丢弃。机器人自身的状态信息，比如机械臂的位置，非常精确，但模型可能偷懒，记住某个状态对应某条轨迹，而不去看画面。所以训练时，随机把整段状态输入去掉，逼它用视觉。

老师模型、投影网络和门控，都只在训练时用，推理时全部丢掉，也不需要深度图或者相机标定作为输入。

数据方面，他们整合了六个公开数据集，按处理前的统计，一共大约 171 万条示范、14330 个小时，有真机数据，也有仿真数据。

为了让不同机器人能一起训练，动作统一用末端执行器的位姿来表示，也就是夹爪在空间里的位置和朝向，坐标轴的方向也统一成一套。旋转用旋转向量来表示，相对旋转用旋转矩阵复合来算，避免欧拉角直接相减带来的异常值。

他们还对高度重复的仿真数据做了降采样，过滤掉灵巧手和移动底盘的数据，并且把下游评测要用的数据排除在外，防止泄漏。

### 实验和效果

评测覆盖三个仿真基准、一个真机基准，再加上八种作者自己设计的真机部署条件。

先看 RoboTwin 2.0，这是一个双臂操作基准，有 50 个任务。只用干净场景的示范训练，在加了随机干扰的 Clean2Random 设置下，SLA 成功率 72.3%，比 π0.5 高 26.3 个百分点，两种设置的平均是 79.2%，在表里最高。

LIBERO-Plus 测的是抗干扰能力，比如换相机角度、换光照、换背景。SLA 加权成功率 87.4%，比 π0.5 高 3 个百分点。

RoboCasa-Panda 是厨房场景的单臂任务。每个任务只用 50 条示范，SLA 达到 68.4%，比表里一些用了 300 条、甚至 3000 条示范的方法还高。

真机基准 RoboDojo-Real，一个模型同时适配三种机械臂。SLA 平均成功率 33.9%，π0.5 是 12.8%。

要说明的是，在这个真机基准上，作者还额外给策略加了一个记忆机制，把过去几秒的画面也作为输入，用来应对需要记忆的长任务。

再看八种真机部署条件，平台是双臂的 Piper 和单臂的 Franka。包括人把水果扔出去，让机器人捡回来，目标在抓取时被人移动，桌子在升降，还有用 100 条 Piper 示范加上 10 条 Franka 示范，迁移到另一台机器人上。

结果在表 11。SLA 每一项都最高，平均 83.8%，π0.5 是 59.8%，高 24 个百分点。比如目标被人移动时，π0.5 是 42%，SLA 是 76%。只用 10 条 Franka 示范迁移时，π0.5 是 48%，SLA 是 78%。

最能说明核心想法的，是这组数据规模实验。作者分别用 5%、25%、50% 和 100% 的预训练数据，在预训练和微调两个阶段，分别打开或关闭空间对齐。

这张图就是结果。两个阶段都对齐，成功率从 49.2% 涨到 72.3%。两个阶段都不对齐，只是从 43.8% 到 48.2%。用全量数据时，只在预训练对齐是 63.2%，只在微调对齐是 57.1%。也就是说，光加数据涨得很少，两个阶段都做对齐，效果最好。

消融实验都在 RoboTwin 上做。固定对齐某一层，最好的是第 12 层，自适应融合比它的平均成功率再高 3.5 个百分点。状态丢弃的概率取 25% 最好，平均 79.2%，完全不丢弃只有 38.1%。

### 和以前方法的对比

和作者自己之前的 Spatial Forcing 比，也就是只在微调阶段做对齐，SLA 在 RoboTwin 上的平均成功率高 10.5 个百分点。八种真机条件的平均，Spatial Forcing 是 68%，SLA 是 83.8%。

和其他强方法比，优势并不总是很大。LIBERO-Plus 上，SLA 只比 Qwen-RobotManip 高 0.3 个百分点，在机器人和语言两个干扰维度上还低于它。RoboTwin 干净场景下，SLA 不如 ME-Dex-1.0。RoboDojo-Real 的 ARX X5 机械臂上，它排第二，落后于 InternW0-Δ。

我的判断是，这篇的贡献更多在训练配方，而不是新架构。它给出了一个可以复用的思路，空间监督可以作为预训练目标，叠加到现有的 VLA 上，部署时不增加任何成本。

我的判断是，有几处对比要谨慎看。RoboDojo-Real 上 SLA 额外加了记忆机制，论文没有说明对比方法是否也用了同样的机制，提升里有多少来自空间对齐，分不清楚。八种真机条件是作者自己设计的场景，每项 50 个物体实例，论文也没有报告重复实验的误差范围。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限章节，下面几条来自它自己报告的结果。第一，绝对水平还不高，真机基准 RoboDojo-Real 上，平均成功率只有 33.9%，在 Piper X 机械臂上只有 10%。

第二，长流程任务还是短板。附录里的 RoboDojo 仿真基准，SLA 平均分最高，但长流程这个维度低于大多数对比方法。记忆维度虽然是它提升最大的地方，但仍有四个任务一次都没成功。

第三，对超参数比较敏感。状态丢弃的概率从 25% 改成 50%，平均成功率就从 79.2% 掉到 53.9%。而且所有消融实验，都只在 RoboTwin 这一个基准上做。

第四，成本不低，继续预训练要在 48 张 H100 上跑大约 110 个小时。

### 未来方向

论文没有专门写未来工作。作者在结论里的判断是，空间理解应该在继续预训练阶段培养，再在下游适配阶段加强。

我的判断是，下一步值得看两件事，这套配方换到别的 VLA 底座上是否同样有效，以及数据再多时曲线还会不会往上走。从这张图看，到全量数据时曲线还在上升。

我的判断是，对做机器人的团队来说，这篇最实用的一点是，不用改部署结构，也不用给策略加深度输入，就能给现有模型补上空间感。不过真机基准上三成多的成功率也提醒我们，离可靠落地还有距离。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from a team led by the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in Guangzhou, working with OLA Dimensions, the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence, and Tsinghua University. It's called SLA, short for Spatial-Language-Action model.

The question it asks: robot foundation models mostly see the world in 2D. Can you give them a sense of 3D space during large-scale pre-training, without changing the model at all?

### Problem

Some background first. A VLA, or vision-language-action model, is one of the most closely watched approaches to robot manipulation today. You take a pretrained vision-language model, attach a module that outputs motor commands, called an action expert, and it looks at the scene, reads the instruction, and drives the arm.

The trouble is that these models get their vision from 2D image and text data. Nothing in that pre-training teaches 3D geometry directly. But grasping and handing things over depend on exactly that: precise positions in space.

The authors ran a probing test. They froze an off-the-shelf VLA, OpenVLA-OFT, and trained only a small head to predict depth from its visual features, to see how much 3D information was in there.

This figure shows the result. From features without alignment, the predicted depth maps are basically noise, with no meaningful 3D structure. From features trained with spatial alignment, the depth maps are clearly much better.

### Prior work

The obvious fix is to feed depth maps or point clouds in during fine-tuning. The paper lists three problems with that. Reliable 3D sensor data is hard to get and often noisy or incomplete. Robots differ in sensor type, placement and calibration. And parts of the big public robot datasets have no depth at all.

The other route is the authors' own earlier work, Spatial Forcing. Instead of adding inputs, it trains the VLA's visual features to match the features of a 3D model. That's called implicit alignment, and it needs no depth data.

But Spatial Forcing only runs during fine-tuning on a specific task. Spatial skill becomes a task-specific add-on, not a property of the foundation model.

### Key idea

SLA's idea is simple: move that implicit spatial alignment from fine-tuning up into continual pre-training. Continual pre-training means taking an already trained foundation model and training it further on more data, keeping what it knows while adding a new capability.

They start from pi zero point five, a pretrained robot policy, continue pre-training it on a large pool of robot data with spatial alignment switched on, and keep the alignment on again during downstream fine-tuning.

The key point is that this supervision changes neither the architecture nor the action-generation part. So the pi zero point five weights can be reused directly, and the deployed model is exactly the same as before.

### Method

Here's how it works. The robot's multi-view images go into a frozen 3D perception model, OmniVGGT. It produces pixel-level 3D features for each image, which are resampled to the VLA's visual token resolution and used as the teacher signal.

Visual tokens from the VLA's intermediate layers pass through a small projection network and are pushed toward the teacher features with a cosine-similarity loss. The total objective is the usual action loss plus a weighted alignment loss.

Why OmniVGGT? Because the mixed datasets are uneven. Some come with camera parameters and depth, others only with plain color images. OmniVGGT accepts whatever geometric information is available and fills the gaps with placeholder tokens.

The second design is adaptive layer selection. Normally you'd hand-pick one layer to align, but the best layer can differ between datasets. SLA learns a gate for every visual token at every layer, which decides how much each layer contributes, and sums the layers before alignment.

The third design is state dropout. The robot's own state, like where the arm is, is very precise, and a model can take a shortcut, memorizing which state goes with which trajectory instead of looking at the scene. So during training, the whole state input is randomly removed, forcing the model to use vision.

The teacher, the projection network and the gates are only used in training and are thrown away at inference. The policy doesn't need depth maps or camera calibration as inputs either.

On data, they combine six public datasets. Before processing, that's roughly one point seven million demonstrations and fourteen thousand three hundred thirty hours, mixing real robots and simulation.

So different robots can train together, actions are expressed as end-effector poses, meaning the position and orientation of the gripper in space, with one shared set of axis directions. Rotations use rotation vectors, and relative rotations are computed by composing rotation matrices, which avoids the huge outliers you get when you subtract Euler angles across the wrap-around point.

They also downsample highly repetitive simulation data, filter out dexterous-hand and mobile-base data, and keep any data used by the downstream benchmarks out of pre-training to avoid leakage.

### Results

Evaluation covers three simulation benchmarks, one real-robot benchmark, and eight real-world deployment conditions the authors designed themselves.

First, RoboTwin two point oh, a two-arm manipulation benchmark with fifty tasks. Trained only on clean-scene demos and tested in the randomized Clean2Random setting, SLA succeeds seventy-two point three percent of the time, twenty-six point three points above pi zero point five. Its average over both settings is seventy-nine point two, the best in the table.

LIBERO-Plus tests robustness to perturbations like new camera angles, lighting and backgrounds. SLA's weighted success rate is eighty-seven point four percent, three points above pi zero point five.

RoboCasa-Panda is single-arm kitchen tasks. With only fifty demos per task, SLA reaches sixty-eight point four percent, higher than some methods in the table that used three hundred or even three thousand demos per task.

On the real-robot benchmark RoboDojo-Real, one model is adapted to three different arms at once. SLA averages thirty-three point nine percent success, against twelve point eight for pi zero point five.

One caveat on that benchmark: the authors also gave the policy a memory mechanism, feeding in frames from the past few seconds, to handle long tasks where the robot has to remember what happened earlier.

Then the eight real-world conditions, on a two-arm Piper platform and a single-arm Franka. They include a person throwing fruit out of the bowl for the robot to pick back up, the target being moved during the grasp, a table that rises and lowers, and transfer to another robot using a hundred Piper demos plus just ten Franka demos.

The results are in Table eleven. SLA is best in every condition, averaging eighty-three point eight percent versus fifty-nine point eight for pi zero point five, a twenty-four point gain. When a person moves the target, pi zero point five gets forty-two percent and SLA gets seventy-six. With only ten Franka demos, it's forty-eight versus seventy-eight.

The experiment that speaks most directly to the core idea is the data-scaling study. The authors use five, twenty-five, fifty and one hundred percent of the pre-training data, and switch spatial alignment on or off separately in pre-training and in fine-tuning.

Here's the chart. With alignment in both stages, success climbs from forty-nine point two to seventy-two point three percent. With alignment in neither, it only moves from forty-three point eight to forty-eight point two. At full data, alignment only in pre-training gives sixty-three point two, and only in fine-tuning gives fifty-seven point one. In other words, more data alone helps little, and aligning in both stages works best.

The ablations are all on RoboTwin. Among single fixed layers, layer twelve is best, and adaptive fusion beats it by another three point five points on average. A state dropout rate of twenty-five percent works best at seventy-nine point two, while no dropout at all gives just thirty-eight point one.

### Comparison

Against the authors' earlier Spatial Forcing, which aligns only during fine-tuning, SLA is ten point five points higher on the RoboTwin average. Across the eight real-world conditions, Spatial Forcing averages sixty-eight percent and SLA eighty-three point eight.

Against other strong methods, the lead isn't always large. On LIBERO-Plus, SLA beats Qwen-RobotManip by only zero point three points, and trails it on the robot and language perturbations. In RoboTwin's clean setting, it's behind ME-Dex one point oh. And on the ARX X5 arm in RoboDojo-Real, it's second, behind InternW0-Delta.

My read is that the contribution is a training recipe rather than a new architecture. It shows a reusable idea: spatial supervision can be a pre-training objective layered onto an existing VLA, with zero cost at deployment.

My read is also that a few comparisons deserve caution. On RoboDojo-Real, SLA adds a memory mechanism, and the paper doesn't say whether the baselines had the same, so it's hard to tell how much of the gain comes from spatial alignment. The eight real-world conditions are the authors' own setups with fifty object instances each, and no error bars from repeated runs are reported.

### Limitations

The paper has no dedicated limitations section, so these come from its own reported results. First, absolute performance is still modest: on the RoboDojo-Real benchmark, average success is thirty-three point nine percent, and just ten percent on the Piper X arm.

Second, long-horizon tasks remain a weak spot. On the RoboDojo simulation benchmark in the appendix, SLA has the best average, but it falls below most baselines on the long-horizon dimension. Memory is where it gains the most, yet four memory tasks still never succeed once.

Third, results are sensitive to settings. Moving state dropout from twenty-five to fifty percent drops the average success rate from seventy-nine point two to fifty-three point nine. And every ablation is run on RoboTwin alone.

Fourth, it isn't cheap: continual pre-training takes about a hundred and ten hours on forty-eight H100 GPUs.

### Future work

The paper doesn't have a future-work section. The authors' conclusion is that spatial understanding should be built during continual pre-training and then reinforced during downstream adaptation, as a practical path toward spatially aware robot foundation models.

My read is that two things are worth watching next: whether this recipe works as well on other VLA backbones, and whether the curve keeps rising with more data. In this chart it's still climbing at full data.

My read, for robotics teams, is that the most practical part is this: you can give an existing model a sense of space without changing the deployed architecture or adding depth inputs to the policy. Still, a real-robot success rate in the low thirties is a reminder that reliable deployment is some distance away.
