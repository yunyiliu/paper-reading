## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇清华大学牵头的论文，合作方还有佛山大学和华南理工大学，题目叫 AeroRSI，意思是，空中视觉语言导航的递归自我改进。

它要解决的问题是，一架听人话飞行的无人机，飞着飞着撞了，怎么让它从自己的失败里学会改正，而且不靠人来标注。

先说结果。在 TravelUAV 这个仿真基准上，三轮自我改进，把一个 20 亿参数策略的成功率，从 46.4% 提到 67.5%，碰撞减少了一半。

### 问题

背景是这样。现在有一类模型叫 VLA，也就是视觉语言动作模型，它看着摄像头画面，读着一句自然语言指令，直接输出动作。用在无人机上，就是空中视觉语言导航。

问题是，这些模型在闭环飞行里经常失败，最常见的就是撞上障碍物。而一个错误动作，就能让整次飞行结束。

根源是模仿学习的老问题。模型照着示范学，可它自己飞的时候，会走到示范里从来没出现过的状态，错误越积越多。

要纠正，就需要作者说的 repair，修复动作，也就是在策略自己走到的那个状态下，能避开失败的动作。难点在于，谁来判断一个动作算不算修复。

### 以前的方法

以前有几条路。一是交互式模仿学习，比如 DAgger，遇到策略自己走到的状态，就请专家给正确动作，但大规模请人标注太贵。用规则规划器代替人，规则又不理解语言指令。

二是自动纠正，从碰撞回滚、参考路径，或者辅助的老师策略里推出纠正动作。作者指出，这些方法都没有考虑，策略自己能不能真的把这些纠正执行好。

三是强化学习，靠整次飞行的奖励。它能告诉你这次飞失败了，但不告诉你当时应该换哪个动作。

还有一条相关的线，叫递归自我改进，在语言模型上很成功，模型从自己被验证过的输出里学习。但作者说，搬到飞行上有三个难点。

第一，一道题的答案可以单独验证，一个飞行动作好不好，要看之后怎么飞。第二，飞行在第一次失败就结束，后面的失败被藏起来，要等前面的修好才会暴露。第三，失败的策略，常常根本采样不出它需要的那个动作。

### 核心想法

作者的核心想法就一句话，失败的策略，应该自己判断自己的修复，办法是从这个修复动作出发，接着往下飞。

道理是，一个动作好不好，取决于之后是谁在飞。让当前策略接着飞，评估的就是这个动作在它自己手里的价值，这正是近似策略迭代需要的东西。

这样一来，两种本来不太可靠的来源，都能变成可靠的监督。一是策略自己采样出来、但没有选的动作，二是简单的规则建议，比如往上爬升，或者绕开障碍。

看这张图。左边，第 k 轮的策略在 A 点失败，从失败前的状态试几个候选，只留下它自己能飞完的。中间，学会之后，下一轮避开了 A，却在 B 点失败，这个失败原来被 A 藏住了，于是下一轮再去验证新的修复。

### 方法

方法每一轮分三步，这张总览图从左到右就是这三步。策略本身基于 Qwen3-VL-2B，看前视和下视两个摄像头，输出离散的前进、升降、转向动作，训练时只更新 LoRA 和视觉合并层。

第一步叫自适应失败锚定，简称 AFA。当前策略把所有训练路线飞一遍，对每一次失败，在失败前按指数间隔放探针，也就是前 1、2、4、8、16 步。近的抓直接原因，远的抓更早的错误决策，比如拐进了死胡同。

然后在这一轮所有探针里，均匀抽取固定数量的锚点，每轮 2400 个。这样常见的失败类型，自然分到更多锚点，已经克服的失败，就不再出现。

第二步叫延续验证修复，简称 CVR。每个锚点组一组候选，包括原来那个失败动作，策略自己采样的 4 个动作，还有 3 个规则建议，一个势场避障动作，一个保留原来前进量的变体，一个保持原来朝向的爬升。

每个候选怎么打分。仿真器恢复到锚点状态，执行这个候选，然后让当前策略接着贪心地飞，要飞过原来的失败点。分数是朝目标前进的距离，失败扣分，成功加分。

因为单次飞行有随机性，还有两道检查。复现检查，原来的动作再飞一次，要么又失败，要么明显不如最好的候选，否则这个锚点就不要。确认检查，把得分最高的两个候选按顺序重飞，第一个再次合格的，才被接受为修复。

第三步叫保持式修复学习，简称 RRL。对验证过的修复，直接最大化它的概率，但不去压低原来那个动作。作者的理由是，原动作常常只是稍差一点，在别的状态下还是对的。

同时加入一批不需要修复的状态，标签是当前策略自己的输出，防止策略被修复动作带偏。从第二轮起，再按 30% 的比例回放上一轮的修复，防止遗忘。

要注意，深度图、目标位置、存档读档，这些仿真器特权只用来生成训练数据。部署的时候，策略只看摄像头、指令和一个粗略的目标方向，结构和推理成本都和初始策略一样。

### 实验和效果

实验在 TravelUAV 上做，这是基于 AirSim 和虚幻引擎的仿真基准。训练集 7611 条路线，测试集分见过的环境、没见过的物体、没见过的地图三部分，一共 3005 条。在 200 个动作内，降落在目标 20 米以内，算成功。

最主要的对比对象是 AeroDPO。它和 AeroRSI 从同一个监督微调策略出发，都不加人工标注，所以两者的差别只来自后训练方法。

结果在表 2。总成功率，AeroDPO 是 56.9%，AeroRSI 是 67.5%，高 10.6 个百分点。逐条路线看，AeroRSI 成功而 AeroDPO 失败的有 649 条，反过来只有 330 条。

如果直接从 AeroDPO 出发，再跑三轮，总成功率到 69.9%，作者称这是新的最好结果。

表 3 是每一轮的变化。从监督策略出发，成功率依次是 46.4%、57.6%、66.0%、67.5%，碰撞率从 38.6% 降到 19.1%。每轮的增益是 11.2、8.4、1.5 个点，最后一轮已经在噪声范围之内。

这张图是失败类型的变化。碰撞和飞错方向，每一轮都在减少，但超时从 1.2% 涨到 6.8%，瓶颈从躲障碍，变成了按时飞到目标。

### 和以前方法的对比

消融实验在表 4，最能说明问题的是验证这一步。如果每个锚点都直接拿势场动作当标签，就像用规则当专家的 DAgger，成功率反而比初始策略低 6.6 个点。

同样的规则建议，只要求策略自己能飞完才保留，第一轮成功率就到 61.6%，比不验证高 12.4 个点。而对策略自己采样的动作，验证只多出 1 个点。作者的解释是，来源离策略越远，验证越有用。

只看两步之后谁离目标更近，也不行，碰撞率会高出 4.2 个点。案例图里，朝目标直冲的动作起初领先，后来撞上了棕榈树，反而一个落后大约 6 米的急转弯，飞完了全程。

为什么要一轮一轮地来。拿第一轮的修复再训一次，只到 60.3%。从初始策略一次收集两倍锚点，训练量相同，也只到 63.0%，都低于第二轮的 66.0%。作者的解释是，修复并没有过期，是失败的位置在移动。

还有一个数据挺有意思。第一轮里，只有规则建议才能修好的那些锚点，初始策略采样出合格动作的比例只有 11%，学了一轮之后，到了 62%。也就是说，规则教的东西，变成了策略自己会做的动作。

我的判断是，这篇真正有价值的地方，是把谁来判断修复这个问题，交给了策略自己的后续飞行。规则本身很粗糙，直接模仿反而有害，但经过这层验证，就成了最有效的监督，这个思路不只适用于无人机。

### 局限

再说局限。第一，所有结果都在仿真里，论文没有真实无人机的飞行实验。而且训练要靠仿真器精确地存档和读档，这在真实飞行里很难做到。

第二，在没见过的地图上，AeroRSI 比监督策略高 9.4 个点，但成功率只和 AeroDPO 持平，SPL 还更低。作者也承认，没见过的环境里，最终水平主要由初始策略决定。

第三，很多修复是爬高或者绕路，测试飞行的中位最高高度，从 10 米升到 36 米。作者把高度限制在起点以上 15 米，在 999 条路线上再测，第三轮策略相对初始策略的增益，16.2 个点里还保留了 13.2 个点。

第四是成本。每一轮大约 100 个 GPU 小时，大部分花在仿真器里飞训练路线和验证候选，真正的训练只要十几到二十几分钟。

### 未来方向

论文没有单独写未来工作，但它自己的分析指出了两个瓶颈。一是超时成了更主要的失败，策略要学会按时飞到目标。二是在没见过的地图上收益有限，因为这个循环只在训练环境里收集经验。

我的判断是，最关键的下一步是走出仿真。这套方法离不开存档读档，真实飞行里怎么拿到同样可靠的修复信号，论文没有回答。

我的判断是，这个思路也值得机械臂和地面机器人借鉴，凡是能在仿真里回到某个状态重新试的任务，都可以让策略自己验证自己的修复。不过这一点，论文没有做实验。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is led by Tsinghua University, with Foshan University and South China University of Technology. It's called AeroRSI: recursive self-improvement for aerial vision-language navigation.

The question is a practical one. A drone that follows spoken-style instructions keeps crashing. How do you get it to learn from its own failures, without paying people to label anything?

Here's the headline result. On the TravelUAV simulation benchmark, three rounds of self-improvement take a two-billion-parameter policy from forty-six point four percent success to sixty-seven point five, and cut collisions in half.

### Problem

Some background. Vision-language-action models, or VLAs, look at camera frames, read a natural-language instruction, and output actions directly. Put one on a drone and you get aerial vision-language navigation.

The trouble is that these models fail a lot in closed-loop flight, most often by hitting obstacles. And a single bad action can end the whole flight.

The root cause is an old imitation-learning problem. The model learns from demonstrations, but when it flies on its own, it reaches states the demonstrations never covered, and the errors pile up.

Fixing that takes what the authors call repairs: actions that avoid a failure at the states the policy itself actually reaches. The hard part is deciding who gets to judge whether an action counts as a repair.

### Prior work

There have been a few answers. One is interactive imitation learning, like DAgger, where an expert labels the states the learner runs into. But human labeling at scale is very expensive, and rule-based planners, the cheap substitute, ignore the language instruction.

A second is automatic correction: derive fixes from collision rollbacks, reference paths, or a helper teacher policy. The authors' point is that none of these asks whether the policy itself can actually carry the correction out.

A third is reinforcement learning with episode-level rewards. That tells you a flight failed, but not which action should have been different.

There's also a related line of work, recursive self-improvement, which has worked well for language models: the model learns from its own verified outputs. But the authors argue flight raises three problems that this leaves open.

First, an answer can be checked on its own, but a flight action is only as good as the flight that follows it. Second, a flight ends at its first failure, so later failures stay hidden until the earlier ones are fixed. Third, a failing policy often can't even sample the action it needs.

### Key idea

The key idea fits in one sentence. A failing policy should judge its own repairs, by flying on from them.

The reasoning: how good an action is depends on who flies afterwards. If the current policy continues the flight, you're measuring the action's value under that policy's own behavior, which is exactly what approximate policy iteration calls for.

Judged this way, two weak sources become reliable supervision. One is actions the policy samples but doesn't pick. The other is simple rule-based proposals, like climbing or detouring around an obstacle.

Here's the picture. On the left, in round k, the policy fails at A. From the state just before, it tries several candidates and keeps only the ones it can fly to completion itself. In the middle, after learning those, the next policy avoids A but fails at B, a failure that A had been hiding, so the next round verifies new repairs there.

### Method

Each round has three steps, left to right in this overview. The policy itself is built on Qwen3-VL-2B. It sees a front and a downward camera, outputs discretized forward, vertical and yaw actions, and only its LoRA adapters and visual merger are trained.

Step one is adaptive failure anchoring, or AFA. The current policy flies every training route. For each failure, AFA drops probes at exponentially spaced points before it: one, two, four, eight and sixteen actions back. Near probes catch the immediate cause, far ones catch earlier mistakes, like turning into a dead end.

Then it draws a fixed budget of anchors uniformly from all the round's probes, two thousand four hundred per round. Common failure types naturally get more anchors, and failures the policy has already overcome get none.

Step two is continuation-verified repair, or CVR. Each anchor gets a candidate group: the original failed action, four actions sampled from the policy, and three rule-based proposals. Those are a potential-field avoidance move, a variant that keeps the original forward motion, and a climb that keeps the original heading.

How is a candidate scored? The simulator restores the anchor state, executes the candidate, and lets the current policy keep flying greedily, past the point where it originally failed. The score is progress toward the target, minus a penalty for failure, plus a bonus for success.

Because a single flight is noisy, there are two checks. The reproduction check replays the original action, and keeps the anchor only if that action fails again or clearly trails the best candidate. The confirmation check re-flies the top two candidates in order, and accepts the first one that qualifies again.

Step three is retentive repair learning, or RRL. It maximizes the likelihood of each verified repair, but does not push down the original action. The authors' reasoning is that the original action is often only slightly worse, and still right in plenty of other states.

It also mixes in states that need no repair, labeled with the current policy's own outputs, so the repairs don't drag normal flight off course. From round two on, it replays the previous round's repairs at a thirty percent share, to avoid forgetting them.

One important detail. Depth maps, target positions, and saving and restoring simulator state are used only to generate training data. At deployment, the policy sees just its cameras, the instruction, and a coarse direction to the target, with the same architecture and inference cost as the starting policy.

### Results

The benchmark is TravelUAV, built on AirSim and Unreal Engine. There are seven thousand six hundred and eleven training routes, and three thousand and five test routes split into seen environments, unseen objects, and unseen maps. A flight succeeds if the drone lands within twenty meters of the target within two hundred actions.

The key baseline is AeroDPO. It starts from the same supervised policy as AeroRSI and also adds no human labels, so any gap between them comes from the post-training method alone.

The results are in Table 2. Overall success is fifty-six point nine percent for AeroDPO and sixty-seven point five for AeroRSI, ten point six points higher. Route by route, AeroRSI succeeds on six hundred and forty-nine routes where AeroDPO fails, and fails on only three hundred and thirty where AeroDPO succeeds.

Start AeroRSI from AeroDPO instead, run three rounds, and overall success reaches sixty-nine point nine percent, which the authors report as a new state of the art.

Table 3 goes round by round. From the supervised policy, success goes forty-six point four, fifty-seven point six, sixty-six point zero, then sixty-seven point five, while the collision rate falls from thirty-eight point six percent to nineteen point one. The gains per round are eleven point two, eight point four and one point five points, and that last one is within the noise.

This figure shows how the failures change. Collisions and flying the wrong way drop every round, but timeouts grow from one point two to six point eight percent. The bottleneck moves from dodging obstacles to reaching the target in time.

### Comparison

The ablations are in Table 4, and the most telling one is about verification. Label every anchor with the potential-field action, as DAgger would with a rule-based expert, and success actually drops six point six points below the starting policy.

Take the same rule-based proposals but keep only the ones the policy can fly to completion, and first-round success reaches sixty-one point six percent, twelve point four points above the unverified version. For the policy's own sampled actions, verification adds just one point. The authors' reading: the further a source is from the policy, the more verification matters.

Judging candidates by who's closest to the target after two actions doesn't work either; it leaves four point two points more collisions. In one case study, the moves heading straight at the target lead early, then crash into a palm tree, while a sharp turn that was about six meters behind is the one that completes the flight.

Why go round after round? Retraining on the first round's repairs again only gets to sixty point three percent. Collecting twice the anchors from the starting policy and training once, with the same number of updates, gets sixty-three point zero. Both fall short of round two's sixty-six. The authors' explanation is that repairs don't expire; the failures move.

One number stands out. At the first-round anchors that only a rule-based proposal could repair, the starting policy's own samples qualify just eleven percent of the time. After one round of learning, that's sixty-two percent. What the rules taught has become something the policy does on its own.

My read is that the real contribution here is handing the question of who judges a repair to the policy's own continued flight. The rules are crude, and imitating them directly hurts, but once filtered this way they become the most effective supervision. That idea isn't specific to drones.

### Limitations

Now the limitations. First, every result is in simulation; there are no real-drone flight experiments in the paper. And training depends on a simulator that can save and restore state exactly, which is hard to do in real flight.

Second, on unseen maps, AeroRSI beats the supervised policy by nine point four points, but only matches AeroDPO in success rate, with a lower SPL. The authors acknowledge that in unseen environments, the final level is set mainly by the starting policy.

Third, many repairs climb over or route around obstacles, and the median peak altitude of a test flight rises from ten meters to thirty-six. With altitude capped at fifteen meters above the start, on nine hundred and ninety-nine test routes, the round-three policy keeps thirteen point two of its sixteen point two point gain over the starting policy.

Fourth, cost. Each round takes about a hundred GPU hours, mostly spent flying training routes and verifying candidates in the simulator; the training itself takes only eleven to twenty-three minutes.

### Future work

The paper has no separate future-work section, but its own analysis points to two bottlenecks. Timeouts are now a bigger share of failures, so the policy needs to reach the target in time. And gains on unseen maps are limited, because the loop only gathers experience in the training environments.

My read is that the crucial next step is leaving simulation. The method relies on save and restore, and the paper doesn't say how you would get an equally reliable repair signal in real flight.

My read is also that robot arms and ground robots could borrow this. Any task where you can return to a state in simulation and try again could let a policy verify its own repairs. The paper doesn't test that, though.
