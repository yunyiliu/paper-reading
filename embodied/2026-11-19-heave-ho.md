## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇南加州大学和 VinMotion 的论文，名字叫 Heave-Ho，讲的是让好几台人形机器人一起搬东西，而且机器人之间完全不联网。

先看这张图。四台机器人抬一张桌子，两台机器人抬着箱子过窄门，还有机器人和人一起抬箱子上楼梯。每台机器人只看自己的观察，队长决定下一步做什么，然后把它说出来，其他机器人听到后一起执行。

### 问题

为什么要多台一起干。单台人形机器人越来越能干，但家里、仓库、工地上很多活，一台干不了，比如搬桌子、床架、大箱子，得几台一起分担重量。

作者说这里有三个难点，而且互相牵连。第一，物理上耦合得很紧，力通过同一个物体传过来，每台机器人的平衡和抓握，都取决于搭档在干什么。

第二，长流程任务要看着现场做规划，比如过窄门之前，得先把箱子转个方向。第三，执行必须同步，抬晚了一拍，或者一台先迈了一步，箱子和机器人都可能失去平衡。

### 以前的方法

常见的做法是集中式控制，一个策略看到全队的观察，统一给每台机器人下指令，时间也容易对齐。

问题在于，它的输入和输出会随着队伍变大而变大，加一台机器人，通常就要改模型、重新训练。而且要汇总全队的观察，执行的时候就得联网通信。

另一类是分布式方法，每台机器人各跑各的。比如 CHORUS，用一个共享的视觉语言动作模型，也就是 VLA，让不同的移动机械臂各自根据自己的观察行动。

人形机器人这边，现有工作主要研究的是机器人和人合作。多个角色一起搬东西的研究也有，但只停留在仿真里。

### 核心想法

这篇的目标，是把分布式的可扩展性，和紧密同步的全身协作结合起来，而且不联网。

思路分两层。底层，每台机器人跑同一个共享的控制策略，只看自己的身体状态和物体信息，看不到搭档的状态，搭档对它的影响，只通过手里那个共同的物体传过来。

高层，由一台队长机器人看摄像头，决定下一步的团队动作，再用喇叭说出来，其他机器人用自己的麦克风听，在同一个时间点一起开始。这张总览图画的就是这两层。

### 方法

两层之间的接口，叫团队技能。词表很小，比如抬起、放下、往前后左右各走一步，原地左转、右转。

每个技能都从两个固定姿势之一开始和结束，空手站着，或者抱着物体站着。所以任何技能后面都能接任何技能，长流程任务就变成了一串离散的选择。步长和转角也是固定的。

每台机器人还要把团队技能换算成自己的动作。比如两台面对面抬箱子，队伍往队长前方走，对队长是前进，对搭档就是后退，而抬起和转身对两边都一样。

参考动作，一是从两个人一起干活的视频里恢复出来，再迁移到机器人身上，二是用逆运动学程序化生成。它会根据物体尺寸和重量调整，比如手张多开、蹲多深。

底层控制的关键是残差训练。作者没有从零学走路和平衡，而是冻结一个现成的人形机器人基础跟踪模型，叫 SONIC，负责平衡和迈步，再在上面训练一个残差策略，专门学和搭档一起承重需要的力和接触。

训练用多智能体强化学习，所有机器人共用同一套策略参数，所以策略和队伍人数无关。训练时有一个能看到全队信息的评价网络，叫 critic，执行时每台只看自己。

奖励按每台机器人分别算，看它跟自己的参考动作跟得好不好，物体跟参考轨迹跟得好不好。失败是连坐的，只要一台失败，整队这一轮就结束，大家在同一个结果下训练。

为了能任意拼接技能，训练用的是很长的随机技能链。物体的真实尺寸在给定值上下浮动百分之十，重量浮动百分之二十，同一个网络就能适应不同的物体。

再看高层。规划器是在 Qwen3-VL-2B 上用 LoRA 微调的视觉语言模型。输入是队长头部摄像头的画面和任务指令，输出只有一个词，就是下一个团队技能，或者保持、完成。每做完一个技能问一次。

训练数据，真实世界里是操作员通过同一个接口选技能，每个任务录 24 条。仿真里用脚本规划器，每个任务录 1000 条。

同步是这篇最有意思的设计。作者把做什么和什么时候做分开。做什么，由队长决定，说出来就是一个词。什么时候做，由一个共享的时间表决定，技能只能在一个比较粗的节拍格子上开始。

这样，不同机器人听到命令稍有先后也没关系，大家都在下一个格子一起开始。格子比声音延迟的波动粗，所以一个数据包都不用发，全队也能同时开始。

用声音还有个好处，人也能加入。人可以听口令跟着抬，也可以自己当队长喊口令。

### 实验和效果

为了评测，作者做了一个基准，叫 HH-Bench，12 个协作任务，队伍从 2 台到 10 台，也包括不同型号混编。有抬、扛、拉、推、交接和精确放置，还有窄门、L 形走廊这样的空间限制。

窄门是招牌任务。门比箱子在搬运方向上的长度还窄，队伍得先原地把箱子转过来，再一台往前、一台往后走过去。只会沿固定方向走的队伍，是过不去的。

实验用的是宇树 G1 人形机器人，异构队伍里还有一台 EngineAI 的 PM01。策略在每台机器人自己的机载电脑上，以 50 赫兹运行。

先看真实世界，这一组实验里有时是人当队长喊口令。所有任务加起来，100 次试验成功 92 次。最难的是一台 G1 和一个人抬箱子上楼梯，10 次成功 7 次，因为每级台阶只够放下一只脚多一点，脚踩偏一点就不行。

这张图是真实世界的时间序列，比如两台 G1 过窄门，先抬起，转两次，再后退两步。

高层规划的对比在表 1。五个长流程任务，每个 10 次，两个在真实世界，三个在仿真里。对手一个是零样本的 GPT-6 Astra，另一个是用同样数据微调的 CHORUS，输出都改成选团队技能。人工操作员选技能，作为上限。

结果，Heave-Ho 平均成功率 86%，GPT-6 Astra 是 42%，CHORUS 是 12%，人工操作员是 98%。三个仿真任务它和操作员一样，10 次全成。真实世界的窄门 10 次成 6 次，走廊成 7 次，操作员分别是 9 次和 10 次。

速度上，它一次决策，在一张 RTX 4090 上要 52.5 毫秒，CHORUS 是 78 毫秒，GPT-6 Astra 通过 API 平均要 6.46 秒，大约是它的 120 倍。

CHORUS 为什么几乎全败。作者的解释是，它在每台机器人上各跑一份，各自异步推进，开始技能的时刻不一样，一起抬东西就失败了。只有交接箱子耦合时间短，它 10 次成了 6 次。

底层控制的对比在表 2，仿真里 12 个任务平均，每个任务三个随机种子，每个种子 512 支队伍。Heave-Ho 成功率 94.41%，其他所有控制器都不超过 28.56%。

只用冻结的 SONIC、不做后训练，成功率只有 0.22%。它能复现动作，但不知道怎么对物体用力、和搭档分担重量。

去掉 SONIC，从零学全身动作，成功率 16.78%，几乎只在两个不用抬起物体的推的任务上成功。

每台机器人用各自独立的策略、不共享参数，成功率 22.05%。把单机方法 HDMI 扩展到多机，每台的评价网络只看自己，叫 Multi-HDMI，是 28.56%，主要在扛和推的任务上成功，紧密耦合的抬举就不行。

最后是两台训练、多台部署。所有 G1 策略都只用两台一模一样的机器人训练，不重新训练直接部署。真实世界里，四台 G1，或者 G1、PM01 和人混编抬桌子，一共 40 次成功 37 次。仿真里，同一个策略能带四台、八台、十台 G1 一起抬桌子。

### 和以前方法的对比

和以前的区别在这里。集中式方法要联网，加机器人就要改模型。CHORUS 不用联网，但没有对齐时间的机制。Heave-Ho 执行完全分布式，决策只交给队长，再用声音加时间格同步。

我的判断是，这篇最值得借鉴的，是把问题拆得很干净。底层只管把力用对，高层只选下一个词，同步只靠一个粗的时间格。每一层都简单，所以能往更大的队伍扩展。

我的判断是，也要看到对比的边界。GPT-6 Astra 是零样本，没用这些任务的数据，而 Heave-Ho 的规划器每个任务都有专门的示范，差距有一部分来自训练数据。论文也没有和联网的集中式方法正面比。八台、十台机器人只在仿真里给了画面，没有给成功率。

### 局限

作者自己写了两条局限。第一，同步靠跟随者听见队长，需要比较安静的环境和可靠的收音。

第二，给新任务训练规划器，需要人工遥操作采集示范，很费时间。

论文还说明了一点，仿真里的起始位置在很大范围内随机，真实世界是从标准位置加小扰动开始，所以仿真和真实的成功率不能直接比。

### 未来方向

论文没有单独写未来方向。结论里强调的是，两台训练出来的策略，能用到最多十台机器人、不同型号的人形机器人，还有人身上。

我的判断是，下一步值得看三件事。规划器能不能少依赖每个任务的人工示范，在嘈杂的仓库或工地里声音同步还靠不靠得住，还有真实世界里更大的队伍。

我的判断是，这篇给多机器人协作提供了一个很朴素的思路，机器人之间不一定需要网络，说一个词，再加一个共同的节拍，就能一起动。不过十台的规模，目前还只在仿真里。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the University of Southern California and VinMotion. It's called Heave-Ho, and it's about getting several humanoid robots to carry things together, with no network connection between them at all.

Start with this figure. Four robots carry a table, two robots take a box through a narrow door, and a robot and a person carry a box up the stairs. Each robot acts on its own observations. A leader picks the next move and says it out loud, and the others hear it and execute together.

### Problem

Why teams? Single humanoids keep getting more capable, but plenty of work in homes, warehouses and construction sites is too much for one robot. Tables, bed frames, large boxes: you need several robots to share the load.

The authors name three challenges, and they're tangled together. First, the interaction is tightly coupled. Forces travel through the shared object, so each robot's balance and grip depend on what its partners are doing.

Second, long tasks need planning grounded in what the robots see, like turning the box before a narrow doorway. Third, execution has to be synchronized. A late lift, or one robot stepping on its own, can throw both the load and the robots off balance.

### Prior work

The usual answer is centralized control. One policy sees the whole team's observations and commands every robot, which also makes timing easy to align.

The catch is that its inputs and outputs grow with the team. Add a robot and you typically modify the model and retrain it. And collecting team-wide observations means the robots need a network while they work.

The other family is decentralized, where each robot runs its own copy. CHORUS, for example, uses one shared vision-language-action model, a VLA, so different mobile manipulators can each act from their own observations.

For humanoids specifically, existing work mostly studies robots collaborating with people. Teams of characters carrying things together have been explored too, but only in simulation.

### Key idea

The goal here is to get the scalability of decentralized control together with tightly synchronized whole-body teamwork, without a network.

The design has two levels. At the low level, every robot runs the same shared controller. It sees only its own body and the object's specification, never its partners' states. Partners affect it only through the object in its hands.

At the high level, one leader robot looks through its camera, picks the next team move, and speaks it through a speaker. The others hear it on their own microphones and start at the same moment. This overview figure shows both levels.

### Method

The interface between the two levels is a set of team skills. The vocabulary is small, things like lift, put down, one step in each direction, and turn left or right in place.

Every skill starts and ends in one of two fixed poses, standing empty-handed or standing while holding the object. So any skill can follow any other, and a long task becomes a sequence of discrete choices. Step lengths and turn angles are fixed too.

Each robot also maps a team skill into its own frame. If two robots face each other across a box, moving the team toward the leader's front is a forward step for the leader and a backward step for its partner. Lifting and turning are the same for both.

The reference motions come from two sources: human pairs recorded on video and retargeted to the robots, and procedural generation with inverse kinematics. They adapt to the object's size and mass, like how wide the hands go and how deep the squat is.

The key to the low level is residual training. Instead of learning balance and walking from scratch, the authors freeze an existing humanoid foundation tracking model called SONIC, which handles balance and stepping, and train a residual policy on top that learns the forces and contacts needed to share a load.

Training uses multi-agent reinforcement learning. All robots share one set of policy parameters, so the policy doesn't depend on team size. During training a critic sees the whole team, but at execution each robot sees only itself.

Rewards are computed per robot: how well it tracks its own reference, and how well the object follows its reference. Failure is shared. If any robot fails, the episode ends for the whole team, so they all train under one outcome.

To make skills chain freely, training runs on long random skill sequences. The object's true size varies by ten percent around the stated value and its mass by twenty percent, so one network adapts to different objects.

Now the high level. The planner is Qwen3-VL-2B, fine-tuned with LoRA. Its input is the leader's head-camera image plus the task instruction, and its output is a single token: the next team skill, or hold, or done. It's queried each time a skill finishes.

For training data, in the real world an operator picks skills through the same interface, twenty-four episodes per task. In simulation a scripted planner does it, one thousand episodes per task.

Synchronization is the most interesting design choice. The authors separate what the team does from when it does it. What is decided by the leader, as one spoken word. When is fixed by a shared schedule: a skill can only start on a coarse grid of control ticks.

So if robots hear the command at slightly different moments, it doesn't matter. Everyone starts on the next slot. Because the grid is coarser than the variation in acoustic delay, the team starts together without sending a single packet.

Speech has another upside: people can join. A person can follow the spoken commands, or take over as the leader.

### Results

To evaluate this, the authors built a benchmark called HH-Bench: twelve collaborative tasks for teams of two to ten humanoids, including mixed teams. It covers lifting, shoulder carrying, pulling, pushing, handover and precise placement, plus spatial constraints like narrow doors and L-shaped corridors.

The narrow door is the signature task. The door is narrower than the box along the carrying direction, so the team has to rotate the box in place, then walk through with one robot going forward and the other backward. A team that only walks in a fixed direction can't do it.

The hardware is Unitree G1 humanoids, plus an EngineAI PM01 for mixed teams. The policies run on each robot's own on-board computer at fifty hertz.

First, the real world, where in some experiments a person acted as the leader. Across all tasks, the teams succeeded in ninety-two of one hundred trials. The hardest was a G1 and a person carrying a box up stairs, seven of ten, because each step leaves room for little more than one foot.

This figure shows real-world sequences, for example two G1s at the narrow door: lift, turn twice, then two steps backward.

The planner comparison is in Table 1. Five long tasks, ten trials each, two in the real world and three in simulation. The opponents are zero-shot GPT-6 Astra, and CHORUS fine-tuned on the same data, both choosing team skills. A human operator picking skills by hand is the upper bound.

Heave-Ho averages eighty-six percent, GPT-6 Astra forty-two, CHORUS twelve, and the operator ninety-eight. On the three simulated tasks it matches the operator at ten out of ten. In the real world it gets six of ten at the door and seven at the corridor, against the operator's nine and ten.

It's also fast. One decision takes fifty-two point five milliseconds on a single RTX 4090, versus seventy-eight for CHORUS and six point four six seconds on average for GPT-6 Astra through its API, about a hundred and twenty times longer.

Why does CHORUS nearly always fail? The authors explain that each robot runs its own copy and advances asynchronously, so the robots start skills at different moments, which breaks joint carrying. Only the handover, where coupling is brief, gives it six of ten.

The low-level comparison is Table 2: simulation, averaged over all twelve tasks, three seeds of five hundred and twelve teams each. Heave-Ho succeeds ninety-four point four one percent of the time. No other controller exceeds twenty-eight point five six.

Frozen SONIC alone, with no post-training, succeeds zero point two two percent of the time. It reproduces the motion but doesn't know how to exert force on the object or share the load.

Remove SONIC and learn the full body action from scratch, and you get sixteen point seven eight percent, succeeding almost only on the two pushing tasks, which don't require lifting.

Give each robot its own actor instead of sharing parameters, and it's twenty-two point zero five. Multi-HDMI, a single-robot method extended to teams with critics that see only local observations, reaches twenty-eight point five six, mostly on shoulder carrying and pushing, and fails at tightly coupled lifting.

Finally, train with two, deploy with many. Every G1 policy was trained with exactly two identical robots, then deployed without retraining. In the real world, four G1s, or mixed teams of G1, PM01 and people, carried a table successfully in thirty-seven of forty trials. In simulation, the same policy drives teams of four, eight and ten G1s carrying a table.

### Comparison

Here's how it differs from what came before. Centralized methods need a network and a new model when the team changes. CHORUS needs no network, but has no way to align timing. Heave-Ho executes fully decentralized, leaves decisions to one leader, and syncs with speech plus a time grid.

My read is that the most reusable lesson is how cleanly the problem is split. The low level only gets the forces right, the high level only picks the next word, and synchronization is just a coarse clock. Each layer is simple, which is why it can extend to bigger teams.

My read is that the comparisons have limits too. GPT-6 Astra is zero-shot, with no data from these tasks, while Heave-Ho's planner gets task-specific demonstrations, so part of the gap comes from training data. There's no head-to-head with a networked centralized method. And the eight- and ten-robot teams are shown only as simulation images, with no success rates.

### Limitations

The authors list two limitations. First, synchronization depends on followers hearing the leader, so it needs a reasonably quiet environment and reliable sound pickup.

Second, training the planner for a new task needs demonstrations collected by teleoperation, which is time-consuming.

The paper also notes that simulated trials start from a wide range of positions while real trials start from a nominal setup with small perturbations, so simulated and real success rates aren't directly comparable.

### Future work

The paper has no separate future-work section. Its conclusion stresses that a policy trained with two identical humanoids carries over to teams of up to ten robots, to a different humanoid, and to people.

My read is that three things are worth watching: whether the planner can rely less on per-task demonstrations, whether speech sync holds up in a noisy warehouse or construction site, and larger teams in the real world.

My read is that this paper offers a refreshingly plain idea for robot teamwork. Robots may not need a network at all. One spoken word and a shared beat can get them moving together. But the ten-robot scale is still simulation only.
