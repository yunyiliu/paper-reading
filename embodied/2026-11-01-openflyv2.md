## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇上海人工智能实验室等单位的论文，名字叫 OpenFlyV2，是一个直接装在无人机上跑的视觉语言动作模型。

它想回答的问题是，能不能用一个大约 10 亿参数的模型，让无人机既能看懂画面、回答问题，又能听懂一句话自己飞过去，而且所有计算都在飞机上完成。

先说结论，作者在真实无人机上做了 100 次飞行测试，没有用任何真实飞行数据训练，成功率是 48%。

### 问题

先解释两个词。VLN，视觉语言导航，就是给机器人一句话，比如飞到红色钟楼附近停下，它要边看边飞，自己找到目标。VLA，视觉语言动作模型，就是一个模型同时处理图像和语言，并且直接输出动作。

作者想要的不只是会按指令飞。无人机还要能看懂场景，比如前面那栋楼是什么颜色，一句话说的是哪个目标，两个东西谁在左谁在右，谁离得近。

更难的是放在飞机上跑。把计算放到地面，导航就要看通信稳不稳、延迟大不大。放在机载芯片上，算力又很有限。

作者还提到一个很实际的点，无人机等不到下一步指令时，可以减速或者悬停，但频繁停下来，会拉低平均飞行速度，浪费它本来的机动性。

### 以前的方法

先看机器人这边。RT-1、RT-2、OpenVLA、Octo 这些模型，已经展示了越来越通用的、跨任务训练的机器人策略，这也是作者想把共享的视觉语言表示用到无人机上的出发点。

但在无人机方向，作者的判断是，现有系统往往只专做某一种能力，或者依赖机外计算，或者没法在资源受限的平台上持续闭环运行。

还有一个具体矛盾，就是历史信息。导航要记得之前看过什么、飞过哪里，以前的方法用拓扑地图或者视频历史来存，但处理密集的历史画面，计算量很大。

### 核心想法

这篇的核心想法，是一个共享的视觉语言骨干，加一个小的动作专家。要回答问题时，骨干直接输出文字。要飞的时候，动作专家读取同一份多模态上下文，输出接下来一小段航点。

为了在机载芯片上跑得动，作者加了两个省算力的设计。一个叫 MAM，运动自适应记忆，用来压缩飞行历史。另一个叫 MSP，运动结构先验，让动作生成少走几步。

### 方法

先看整体结构，就是这张架构图。骨干是 0.8B 的 Qwen3.5-VL，动作专家是 0.2B，加起来大约 10 亿参数。

每一步导航，模型拿到当前画面、指令、飞机自身的状态，还有记忆 token，输出一个动作块，里面是 12 个航点。每个航点是机体坐标系下三个方向的位移，再加一个停止信号。

先说 MAM。它不再把过去的几张图片直接塞给模型，而是把飞行历史切成一段一段的运动事件。相邻两个动作变化超过阈值，或者一段飞得太长，就切开。

每个事件保留这段动作，以及开头和结尾的两张图。模型用动作去查询这两张图里的视觉特征，压成固定数量的几个 token，再加上这个事件发生在多久以前的信息。

效果是，原来四张历史图加当前图，要 446 个输入 token，换成 MAM 之后最多 218 个，少了至少一半，而且覆盖的飞行历史更长。

再说 MSP。动作专家用的是 flow matching，可以理解成从一个随机起点出发，一步步修正成一条轨迹。每多走一步，就要多算一次。

作者观察到，无人机的短时轨迹，其实集中在少数几种常见的运动模式上。他们把训练数据里的轨迹按形状聚成 4 类，作为固定的运动原型。

每次生成动作前，一个预测头先判断现在属于哪一类，再根据当前情况调整这条原型的长度和转向，然后在它周围加上噪声，作为生成的起点。这样起点本身就带着运动结构，动作专家用更少的步数就能生成航点，最终部署时只走一步。

训练分三段。先用导航和问答数据联合训练两轮，然后打开 MAM 和 MSP，再训练三轮。最后是强化学习，只更新动作专家，在仿真里从拐弯点或停止点开始在线试飞，奖励任务进展，惩罚碰撞、错误停止和偏离路线。

数据方面，作者自己搭了一个数据集，叫 OpenFlyV2-130K。一共 13 个环境，有 AirSim 和虚幻引擎的城市场景，还有用 3D 高斯泼溅重建的 5 个大学校园和 2 处灾后废墟。3D 高斯泼溅，简单说，就是用真实照片重建出一个可以从任意角度渲染的三维场景。

里面有 72352 条导航轨迹，60006 个问答对，涵盖颜色识别、物体描述、距离估计、空间关系和视觉定位。其中 4 个环境完全不参与训练，留作没见过的测试场景。

标注由 GPT-5 自动生成，再交给 200 名标注员人工核对，按严格标准只有 60% 通过。任务相关的训练数据全部来自仿真和渲染，没有用真实的飞行示范。

### 实验和效果

先看理解能力，也就是这张图的上半部分。论文报告，在五项航拍感知任务上，这个 0.8B 的骨干整体和 GPT-5.5 大致相当，超过了 Qwen3.5-9B 和 Gemini 3 Flash。

优势最大的是视觉定位和距离估计。视觉定位，按预测框和标准框重合度超过一半算对，准确率是 67.1%，GPT-5.5 是 61.6%。距离估计的平均误差是 47.32 米，GPT-5.5 是 52.62 米。颜色识别和空间推理，是接近 GPT-5.5。

再看导航，图的下半部分。仿真里测了 800 个见过环境的任务，和 300 个没见过环境的任务，成功的标准是主动停在离目标 25 米以内。

对比的四个开源方法，AeroVLA、DualVLN、OpenUAV 的导航模型和 OmniNav，都保留原来的设计，用作者的数据训练。OpenFlyV2 的成功率，见过的环境是 62.88%，没见过的是 52.67%。DualVLN 分别是 38.88% 和 36.67%，差了 24 和 16 个百分点。

终点误差也小很多。在见过的环境里，OpenFlyV2 平均离目标 33.72 米，DualVLN 是 65.75 米。

不过要注意，论文说这四个对比方法都没法在机载的 Jetson Orin NX 上跑，所以只在仿真里比，真机上没有对照。

消融实验在表 1，这里的模型都只做了五轮监督训练，还没做强化学习。只走一步时，加上 MAM，成功率从 49.82% 提到 53.45%。再加上 MSP，提到 57.09%，还超过了什么都不加、走两步的基础版本，那个是 56.36%。

速度上，在 Jetson Orin NX 上，同样走一步，动作专家这一环的耗时，基础版本是 80 毫秒，用了 MAM 是 20 毫秒，快了四倍。

最后是真机。作者把模型装在大疆 M30T 上，机上带一块 NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX，没有用真实飞行轨迹训练，也没有在测试场地微调。视觉处理、记忆更新和动作生成都在飞机上跑，以 10 赫兹的频率生成 12 个航点的动作块。新的动作块，是在上一块还在执行的时候异步生成的。

测试在四个地点，两个是训练里重建过的校园，另外两个没见过，其中一个是上海世博文化公园。一共 100 次，单地标和双地标指令各 50 次，操作员输入指令后全程不干预，是否成功由人工判定。

总的成功率是 48%。这张图是其中三次成功的飞行，比如先飞向一栋 U 形的白楼，再向右转，飞到楼顶有水箱和卫星天线的那栋白楼。

### 和以前方法的对比

和以前的做法比，OpenFlyV2 最大的不同，是把场景理解和语言导航放进同一个模型，共享一份表示，再配一个小的动作专家，整个闭环在飞机上跑。

消融里还有两个细节。走两步的时候，只加 MAM 反而最好，成功率 59.45%，MSP 的好处集中在只走一步的情况。另外，在只走一步的组合版本里，到达过目标区域的比例是 68.36%，真正成功的是 57.09%，论文的解读是，到了目标附近，但没能正确地停下。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是证明了理解和控制可以在一块机载芯片上共存，而且两个省算力的设计，都有消融数据和耗时数据支撑，不是只给一个总分。

我的判断是，弱点也很明显。真机上没有任何对照方法，48% 是 100 次试验的汇总，论文正文没有分开给见过和没见过地点的成绩，成功也是人工判定的。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限一节，但讨论里说得很直接，真实环境里可靠的无人机视觉语言导航，仍然是很大的挑战。

具体来说，偏离任务时能不能发现，出错之后能不能恢复，画面变了之后能不能接着导航，作者说这些都还需要继续研究。

作者也说，还需要在更多环境和运行条件下，做更广的评测，才能知道这些能力怎么支撑实际应用。

### 未来方向

作者设想的下一步，是语言引导的巡检，比如先飞到描述的地标，再就看到的画面回答问题。再往后，可以扩展到多轮对话、跨多次请求追踪同一个目标，以及边飞边回答问题。代码作者说会在 GitHub 公开。

我的判断是，这个方向很实用，巡检这类任务，飞到哪里网络都不一定好，机载能跑是硬需求。但 48% 的真机成功率说明，离直接拿来用还有距离，下一步最值得看的，是出错之后的恢复能力。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory and partners, and it's called OpenFlyV2: a vision-language-action model that runs on the drone itself.

The question is whether one model of about a billion parameters can let a drone both understand what it sees and answer questions about it, and also take a plain-language instruction and fly there, with all of the computation on board.

Here's the headline result up front. Across one hundred real flights, with no real flight data used in training, it succeeded forty-eight percent of the time.

### Problem

Two terms first. VLN, vision-language navigation, means giving a robot an instruction like "fly to the red clock tower and stop nearby", and it has to look and move until it finds the goal. A VLA, a vision-language-action model, is a single model that takes in images and language and outputs actions directly.

The authors want more than instruction following. The drone should also understand the scene: what color is the building ahead, which object a sentence refers to, and whether one thing is left or right of another, or closer to the camera.

The harder part is doing it on the aircraft. Run the model on a ground server, and navigation depends on whether the link is up and how much latency it adds. Run it on board, and compute is tight.

The authors make one very practical point. A drone waiting for its next command can slow down or hover, but frequent pauses drag down its average speed and waste the agility that makes it useful in the first place.

### Prior work

On the robotics side, models like RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA and Octo have shown increasingly general robot policies trained across tasks, and that is what motivates the authors to bring shared vision-language representations to drones.

But for aerial robots, the authors argue, existing systems tend to specialize in one capability, depend on offboard compute, or can't keep a closed loop running on constrained hardware.

There's also a specific tension around history. Navigation needs to remember what it saw and where it flew. Earlier methods store that as topological maps or video histories, but processing dense visual history is expensive.

### Key idea

The core idea is one shared vision-language backbone plus a small action expert. To answer a question, the backbone simply writes text. To fly, the action expert reads the same multimodal context and outputs the next short stretch of waypoints.

To make that run on an onboard chip, the authors add two cost-saving pieces. One is MAM, Motion-Adaptive Memory, which compresses flight history. The other is MSP, a Motion-Structured Prior, which lets action generation take fewer steps.

### Method

Here's the overall architecture in this figure. The backbone is a zero point eight billion parameter Qwen3.5-VL, the action expert is zero point two billion, so about one billion in total.

At each navigation step, the model gets the current image, the instruction, the drone's own state, and memory tokens, and it outputs an action chunk of twelve waypoints. Each waypoint is a motion increment in three directions in the drone's body frame, plus a stop signal.

Start with MAM. Instead of feeding the model several past images, it cuts the flight history into motion events. A new event starts when consecutive actions change by more than a threshold, or when a segment gets too long.

Each event keeps its action segment and two images, one at the start and one at the end. The model uses the actions to query visual features from those two images, compresses them into a fixed number of tokens, and adds how long ago the event happened.

The payoff: four history images plus the current one used four hundred forty-six input tokens. With MAM, that drops to at most two hundred eighteen, a cut of at least half, while covering a longer stretch of flight.

Now MSP. The action expert uses flow matching, which you can think of as starting from a random sample and refining it step by step into a trajectory. Every extra step is another pass of compute.

The authors noticed that short drone trajectories cluster around a handful of recurring motion patterns. So they cluster training trajectories by shape into four groups and keep those as fixed motion prototypes.

Before each generation, a prediction head picks which pattern applies, adjusts that prototype's length and turning to the current situation, and adds noise around it as the starting point. Because the starting point already carries motion structure, the expert needs fewer steps to produce waypoints, and the deployed model uses just one.

Training has three stages. First, two epochs of joint training on navigation and question-answer data, then three more with MAM and MSP switched on. Last comes reinforcement learning that updates only the action expert, with online rollouts in simulation starting from turning or stopping points, rewarding progress and penalizing collisions, invalid stops and drifting off course.

For data, the authors built their own set, OpenFlyV2-130K. It spans thirteen environments: AirSim and Unreal Engine cities, plus five university campuses and two post-disaster ruins reconstructed with 3D Gaussian Splatting. That's a technique that turns real photos into a 3D scene you can render from any viewpoint.

It contains seventy-two thousand three hundred fifty-two navigation trajectories and sixty thousand and six question-answer pairs, covering color, object description, distance, spatial relations and visual grounding. Four of the environments are kept entirely out of training as unseen test scenes.

The annotations were generated by GPT-5 and checked by a pool of two hundred human annotators, and under their strict criteria only sixty percent passed. All task-specific training data was simulated or rendered, with no real flight demonstrations.

### Results

First, understanding, in the top half of this figure. The paper reports that across five aerial perception tasks, the zero point eight billion parameter backbone is broadly comparable to GPT-5.5 and ahead of Qwen3.5-9B and Gemini 3 Flash.

The biggest gains are in grounding and distance. On visual grounding, where a predicted box counts if it overlaps the reference box by at least half, it scores sixty-seven point one percent against GPT-5.5's sixty-one point six. Its mean distance error is forty-seven point three two meters, versus fifty-two point six two for GPT-5.5. On color and spatial reasoning, it comes close to GPT-5.5.

Now navigation, the bottom half. The simulation test has eight hundred episodes in seen environments and three hundred in unseen ones, and success means stopping on purpose within twenty-five meters of the goal.

Four open-source methods, AeroVLA, DualVLN, the OpenUAV navigation model and OmniNav, kept their original designs and were trained on the authors' data. OpenFlyV2's success rate is sixty-two point eight eight percent in seen environments and fifty-two point six seven in unseen ones. DualVLN gets thirty-eight point eight eight and thirty-six point six seven, so the gaps are twenty-four and sixteen percentage points.

It also ends up much closer to the goal. In seen environments, OpenFlyV2 finishes thirty-three point seven two meters away on average, against sixty-five point seven five for DualVLN.

One caveat. The paper says none of these four baselines could run on the onboard Jetson Orin NX, so they were compared in simulation only, and there's no baseline in the real flights.

The ablations are in Table 1, using models after five epochs of supervised training, before reinforcement learning. With a single step, adding MAM lifts success from forty-nine point eight two to fifty-three point four five percent. Adding MSP on top takes it to fifty-seven point zero nine, which also beats the plain two-step version at fifty-six point three six.

On speed, on the Jetson Orin NX at one step, the action expert's denoising took eighty milliseconds for the base version and twenty with MAM, a fourfold speedup for that stage.

Finally, real flights. The authors put the model on a DJI M30T carrying an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, with no training on real flight trajectories and no fine-tuning at the test sites. Vision, memory updates and action generation all ran on the aircraft, producing twelve-waypoint chunks at ten hertz. Each new chunk was generated asynchronously while the previous one was still executing.

Testing covered four sites: two campuses that were among the reconstructed training scenes, and two unseen locations, one of them Shanghai Expo Culture Park. There were one hundred trials, fifty single-landmark and fifty two-landmark instructions, with no manual intervention after the operator entered the instruction, and success judged by a person.

The overall success rate was forty-eight percent. This figure shows three successful flights, for example heading to a U-shaped white building, then turning right toward a white building with rooftop tanks and satellite dishes.

### Comparison

Compared with earlier work, the key difference is that OpenFlyV2 puts scene understanding and language-guided navigation into one aerial model with a shared representation and a compact action expert, with the whole loop on the drone.

Two more details from the ablations. At two steps, MAM alone is actually the best configuration, at fifty-nine point four five percent, so MSP's benefit is concentrated in the one-step setting. And in the one-step combined version, the drone entered the goal region sixty-eight point three six percent of the time but succeeded only fifty-seven point zero nine percent, which the paper reads as reaching the goal area without terminating correctly.

My read is that the paper's main contribution is showing that understanding and control can share one onboard chip, and that both cost-saving pieces are backed by ablations and timing numbers, not just one headline score.

My read is that the weaknesses are just as clear. There's no baseline at all in the real flights, the forty-eight percent pools one hundred trials, the text doesn't separate seen from unseen sites, and success was judged by hand.

### Limitations

The paper has no dedicated limitations section, but the discussion is direct about it: reliable aerial vision-language navigation in the real world remains a substantial challenge.

Specifically, detecting when the drone has drifted from the task, recovering from errors, and resuming navigation as the view changes are all, in the authors' words, important directions still to explore.

They also say broader evaluation across environments and operating conditions will be needed to understand how these capabilities can support practical applications.

### Future work

Next, the authors suggest language-guided inspection: fly to a described landmark, then answer questions about what's in view. Beyond that, they point to dialogue, tracking references across requests, and answering questions while flying. The code is promised on GitHub.

My read is that this is a practical direction, since inspection jobs often happen where the network is unreliable, so running on board matters. But a forty-eight percent real-world success rate says there's still a way to go, and error recovery is the thing I'd watch next.
