## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇南洋理工大学和 Ropedia 团队的论文，名字叫 Ropedia W0，是一个给机器人用的世界模型。

它想回答的问题是，能不能用大量人类第一视角的视频，训练出一个人和各种机器人都能用的世界模型。

### 问题

先说什么是世界模型。简单说，给它当前的画面和接下来要做的动作，它预测接下来会看到什么，相当于机器人脑子里的一个模拟器。

难点有两个。第一，已有的模型大多只做固定底座、带夹爪的机械臂。机器人一边走一边动手，也就是 loco-manipulation，视角会不停变化，再加上灵巧手和物体的接触，这些都研究得不多。

第二，不同机器人的动作空间完全不一样，关节数量和含义都不同，很难用一个模型通吃。

### 以前的方法

论文点名了两个代表。DreamDojo 在后训练时，要把潜在动作换成机器人专用的动作。Cosmos3 虽然共用一个主干网络，但每个领域要有自己的动作投影。

作者的意思是，这两种做法遇到一个新机器人，都需要专门的控制接口，或者额外的适配。

数据上也有问题。人类第一视角视频很多，但公开数据集经常缺少真实尺度的相机轨迹，手部姿态的标注有噪声甚至缺失，格式也各不相同，很难放在一起训练。

### 核心想法

这篇的核心想法是，不去统一各家机器人的关节动作，而是把动作统一画到图像上。

走路带来的视角变化，用相机位姿来表示。手上的操作，用投影到画面里的骨架来表示。机器人的骨架，用正向运动学算出来，也就是根据关节角度算出手在空间里的位置，再投影到画面上。

关节空间的动作，不同机器人的维度和含义都不一样，但骨架都能画成同一种图像格式。这样人类视频和机器人视频，就能用同一个接口来训练。

### 方法

第一步是数据，作者叫它 Xp-Data。人类数据来自 14 个公开的第一视角数据集，加上 6000 多小时自己用四个鱼眼相机采集的视频。机器人数据来自 13 个公开数据集，覆盖夹爪和灵巧手。

最终整理出大约一万小时人类视频，和三千小时机器人视频。

这张图是数据流程。先用视觉语言模型筛掉模糊、过暗、抖动的片段，再标注相机轨迹、手部骨架和分层的文字描述，最后做质量检查。

有一个细节。公开的机器人数据里，直接用正向运动学投影出来的骨架，经常和画面里的手对不上，很多数据还缺可靠的相机标定。作者投入了大量人工，去标注和修正机器人的关键点。

文字描述也有三道关，Gemini 先写，Qwen 核对，有分歧的再由人来判断。最后所有数据切成 81 帧、640 乘 480、每秒 16 帧的标准片段。

模型部分，看这张结构图。主干是视频扩散 Transformer，基于 Wan2.2，有两个规模，一个是 5B 的稠密模型，一个是 A14B 的混合专家模型，总参数大约 270 亿，每次激活大约 140 亿。

输入是第一帧画面、一段文字，再加上每一帧的相机和骨架条件。相机轨迹编码成 Plücker 射线图，就是给画面里每个位置标上对应的相机几何，骨架则渲染成骨架图。这两路信号直接加到视频 token 上，新加的投影层初始化为零，所以一开始不会改变原模型的行为。

训练分两个阶段。先只用人类视频做预训练，学习相机和手动起来以后，画面会怎么变。再用人和机器人各占一半概率的混合数据做中期训练，让模型学到机器人的外观和结构。

最后还做了蒸馏，把 50 步生成压到 4 步，并且可以一段接一段地流式生成。论文报告，蒸馏后的 5B 模型在单张 GPU 上能跑到每秒 21.3 帧，是原来的 7 倍，能生成一分钟长的视频。

作者还做了一个新的评测集，叫 Xp-Teleop。一台宇树 G1 人形机器人装两只灵巧手，两个操作员一个控制走路，一个控制双手，一共 27 段长任务，大约一小时，失败的抓取和重试也保留了下来。

### 实验和效果

评测覆盖五个跨形态的机器人基准，加上 200 段训练里没见过的人类第一视角视频。指标主要是 PSNR、SSIM 和 LPIPS，衡量生成画面和真实画面有多接近，再加上从生成视频里反推的相机轨迹误差，和手部跟随的精度。

先看人类视频，这是零样本测试。A14B 预训练版本的 PSNR 是 19.77，对比模型里的 Cosmos3-Nano 是 18.66。相机轨迹误差是 1.54 厘米，Cosmos3-Nano 是 1.93 厘米。

再看 DreamDojo 基准，用的是傅利叶 GR-1 机器人。只用百分之一的后训练数据，Ropedia W0 的 5B 版本在 In-lab 测试集上 PSNR 是 20.85，DreamDojo 14B 是 18.98。论文报告，从百分之一到百分之百的数据量，它都超过 DreamDojo，数据越少，优势越大。

灵巧人形的三个基准在表 4。以 Xp-Teleop 为例，A14B 中期训练版本不做任何后训练，PSNR 是 16.42，已经高于所有对比模型用全量数据后训练的结果，其中最好的是 Cosmos3-Nano 的 14.98。

一个检查点也能直接预测八种形态。在跨形态测试集上，不做后训练，A14B 的 PSNR 是 21.98，用同样机器人数据微调过的 Cosmos3-Nano 是 19.02。

标题里说的 scaling，看这张图。保持数据配比不变，人类预训练数据越多，在没见过的人类视频上，论文报告各项指标单调变好，5B 模型的 PSNR 从 18.27 升到 18.9，LPIPS 从 0.267 降到 0.244。迁移到机器人基准上，作者报告的趋势也一样。

消融实验说明两路条件都有用。去掉骨架条件，A14B 的 PSNR 掉了 3.8 dB。去掉相机条件，平均只掉 0.3 dB，因为八种形态里有六种相机基本不动，但在视角会动的形态上，掉了 1.3 dB。

预训练本身的作用也很明显。同样的架构，从公开的视频生成权重出发，和从 Ropedia W0 的预训练权重出发，都只用百分之一的 GR-1 数据后训练，In-lab 上的 PSNR 分别是 16.04 和 20.85。

最后是下游应用。把它当模拟器给策略打分，在 RoboTwin 2.0 仿真里，对五个 Pi0.5 检查点的评分，和仿真里实际评测结果的相关系数是 0.984，排名完全一致。

拿它做规划，让模型想象多个候选动作的结果，由一个外部的价值模型打分，再挑最好的执行。在真实的 G1 机器人上，任务进度从随机挑选的百分之二十六，提高到百分之五十五，单个最好的策略检查点是百分之四十五。

还有一个用法，用它的 5B 预训练模型，去初始化世界动作模型 FastWAM 的视频主干。在没参与训练的 LIBERO-Plus 上，成功率提高了 6.76 个百分点，从 50.06 到 56.82。

### 和以前方法的对比

总结一下和以前的区别。DreamDojo 用从视频里推出来的潜在动作，Cosmos3 每个领域一套动作编码器，Ropedia W0 用的是相机加骨架这种显式的、画在图上的接口，从预训练到后训练都不变。

对比的基线都按各自的官方方案后训练。不过在 FastWAM 的少样本设置里，比如每个任务只有 10 条演示时，LIBERO-Plus 的相机扰动和噪声扰动两项，成功率反而下降了。

我的判断是，这篇最大的贡献可能不在模型结构，而在数据工程。一万小时人类视频、三千小时机器人视频，再加上大量人工修正的机器人骨架，这种投入很难复现，而结构本身是在 Wan2.2 上加条件分支。

我的判断是，也要看清绝对水平。在灵巧人形基准上，最好的 PSNR 大多在 16 到 22 dB 之间，在 Xp-Teleop 上做大约 30 秒的长时间生成，PSNR 降到 13.47。而且 PSNR 这类像素指标，并不能直接说明物理上对不对。

### 局限

作者在附录里列了三条局限。

第一，蒸馏后的模型能做一分钟左右的实时生成，更长时间的稳定交互，还需要更强的记忆机制，或者定期重新对齐。

第二，他们最大只做到 140 亿激活参数，数据扩展实验也是在固定数据配比下做的。

第三，也是最根本的一条。相机加骨架这个接口，只描述相机和手怎么动，不包括物理接触和物体动力学。模型学到的是动作在画面上的后果，而不是背后的物理。

### 未来方向

作者提到的方向，一是继续扩大数据和模型规模，覆盖更多形态和任务，二是和可以直接用于仿真、有物理属性的 3D 资产和场景结合，去建模真正的动力学。

我的判断是，这篇最值得关注的信号是，人类视频的量，确实能转化成机器人世界模型的能力。如果这个趋势在更大规模上成立，具身智能的数据瓶颈，可能会部分转移到人类视频的采集和标注上。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Nanyang Technological University and the team at Ropedia. It's called Ropedia W0, and it's a world model for robots.

The question behind it: can a huge pile of first-person human video train one world model that works for people and for many different robots?

### Problem

First, what's a world model? Give it the current view and the actions you're about to take, and it predicts what you'll see next. Think of it as a simulator inside the robot's head.

There are two hard parts. First, most existing models only cover fixed-base arms with grippers. When a robot walks and manipulates at the same time, what the field calls loco-manipulation, the viewpoint keeps moving, and dexterous hands touching objects add more complexity. Both are underexplored.

Second, robots have completely different action spaces. The number of joints differs, and so does what each joint means. That makes one model for all of them hard.

### Prior work

The paper singles out two examples. DreamDojo swaps its latent actions for robot-specific actions during post-training. Cosmos3 shares one backbone but needs a separate action projection for each domain.

The authors' point is that both approaches need a dedicated control interface, or extra adaptation, every time a new robot shows up.

Data is the other problem. There's plenty of first-person human video, but public datasets often lack camera trajectories at real-world scale, hand poses are noisy or missing, and every dataset uses its own format. That makes them hard to train on together.

### Key idea

The core idea is to stop trying to unify each robot's joint actions, and instead draw every action into the image.

Viewpoint changes from walking are described by camera poses. Manipulation is described by a skeleton projected into the frame. For robots, that skeleton comes from forward kinematics, which means computing where the hand is in space from the joint angles, and then projecting it onto the image.

Joint-space actions differ in size and meaning from robot to robot, but every skeleton can be rendered in the same image format. So human video and robot video can be trained through one shared interface.

### Method

Step one is data, which the authors call Xp-Data. The human side combines fourteen public first-person datasets with more than six thousand hours of in-house video recorded on a four-fisheye-camera rig. The robot side draws on thirteen public datasets, covering both grippers and dexterous hands.

The result is roughly ten thousand hours of human video and three thousand hours of robot video.

This figure shows the pipeline. A vision-language model first filters out blurry, dark or shaky clips. Then come camera trajectories, hand skeletons and layered text descriptions, and finally quality checks.

One detail stands out. In public robot data, skeletons projected straight from forward kinematics often don't line up with the hands in the image, and many releases lack reliable camera calibration. So the team put substantial manual effort into annotating and correcting robot keypoints.

The captions go through three gates too. Gemini writes them, Qwen checks them, and humans settle any disagreements. Everything is then cut into standard clips of eighty-one frames, at six forty by four eighty, sixteen frames per second.

Now the model, in this architecture figure. The backbone is a video diffusion transformer built on Wan2.2, at two sizes: a dense five-billion-parameter model, and a mixture-of-experts model called A14B, with about twenty-seven billion parameters in total and about fourteen billion active.

The inputs are the first frame, a text prompt, and per-frame camera and skeleton conditions. The camera trajectory becomes a Plücker ray map, which attaches camera geometry to every location in the image, and the skeleton is rendered as a skeleton map. Both signals are added directly to the video tokens, and the new projection layers start at zero, so at the start, the original model behaves exactly as before.

Training has two stages. Pre-training uses human video only, learning how the picture changes when the camera and hands move. Mid-training then samples human and robot data with equal probability, so the model picks up what robots look like and how they're built.

Finally, the model is distilled from fifty sampling steps down to four, and made to stream one chunk after another. The paper reports that the distilled five-billion model runs at twenty-one point three frames per second on a single GPU, seven times the original, with minute-long rollouts.

The authors also built a new benchmark, Xp-Teleop. A Unitree G1 humanoid with two dexterous hands is driven by two operators, one for walking and one for the hands. There are twenty-seven long takes, about an hour in total, and failed grasps and recoveries are kept in.

### Results

Evaluation covers five cross-embodiment robot benchmarks, plus two hundred first-person human clips never seen in training. The main metrics are PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, which measure how close the generated frames are to the real ones, plus camera trajectory error recovered from the generated video, and how accurately the hands follow.

Start with human video, which is zero-shot. The pre-trained A14B model scores a PSNR of nineteen point seven seven, against eighteen point six six for Cosmos3-Nano. Camera trajectory error is one point five four centimeters, versus one point nine three for Cosmos3-Nano.

Next, the DreamDojo benchmark, on the Fourier GR-1 robot. With just one percent of the post-training data, the five-billion Ropedia W0 reaches twenty point eight five PSNR on the In-lab test set, versus eighteen point nine eight for the fourteen-billion DreamDojo. The paper reports that it beats DreamDojo at every budget from one to one hundred percent, with the biggest gains when data is scarce.

The three dexterous-humanoid benchmarks are in Table 4. Take Xp-Teleop. The A14B mid-trained model, with no post-training at all, scores sixteen point four two, already above every baseline's full-data result, the best of which is Cosmos3-Nano at fourteen point nine eight.

A single checkpoint can also predict eight different embodiments directly. On the cross-embodiment set, with no post-training, A14B scores twenty-one point nine eight, while Cosmos3-Nano, fine-tuned on the same robot data, scores nineteen point zero two.

The scaling in the title is this figure. Holding the data mix fixed, the paper reports that more human pre-training data improves every metric monotonically on unseen human video. The five-billion model's PSNR rises from eighteen point two seven to eighteen point nine, and LPIPS falls from zero point two six seven to zero point two four four. The authors report the same trend when transferring to robot benchmarks.

The ablations show both conditions matter. Remove the skeleton, and A14B loses three point eight decibels of PSNR. Remove the camera, and the average drop is only zero point three, because six of the eight embodiments barely move the camera. But on the embodiments with moving viewpoints, the drop is one point three.

Pre-training itself clearly pays off. Take the same architecture, start it from public video-generation weights or from Ropedia W0's pre-trained weights, and post-train both on one percent of the GR-1 data. In-lab PSNR is sixteen point zero four versus twenty point eight five.

Last, downstream uses. As a simulator for scoring policies, in RoboTwin 2.0 simulation, its scores for five Pi zero point five checkpoints correlate with the ground-truth simulator evaluations at zero point nine eight four, with the ranking fully preserved.

For planning, the model imagines the outcomes of several candidate actions, an external value model scores them, and the best one is executed. On a real G1 robot, task progress rises from twenty-six percent with random selection to fifty-five percent, against forty-five percent for the best single policy checkpoint.

And one more use. The five-billion pre-trained model initializes the video backbone of a world-action model called FastWAM. On LIBERO-Plus, held out from training, success rises by six point seven six percentage points, from fifty point zero six to fifty-six point eight two.

### Comparison

To sum up the difference. DreamDojo uses latent actions inferred from video, Cosmos3 uses a separate action encoder per domain, and Ropedia W0 uses an explicit interface, camera plus skeleton drawn in the image, that stays the same from pre-training to post-training.

The baselines were post-trained with their official recipes. But in FastWAM's few-shot settings, for example with ten demos per task, success on LIBERO-Plus actually dropped under two perturbations, camera and noise.

My read is that the biggest contribution here may be data engineering rather than architecture. Ten thousand hours of human video, three thousand hours of robot video, and lots of hand-corrected robot skeletons is hard to reproduce, while the architecture itself adds conditioning branches to Wan2.2.

My read is also that the absolute numbers deserve a look. On the dexterous-humanoid benchmarks, the best PSNR mostly sits between sixteen and twenty-two decibels, and on a roughly thirty-second rollout on Xp-Teleop, it falls to thirteen point four seven. And pixel metrics like PSNR don't directly tell you whether the physics is right.

### Limitations

The authors list three limitations in the appendix.

First, the distilled model handles real-time rollouts of about a minute. Stable interaction over longer horizons will need stronger memory, or periodic re-grounding.

Second, they only go up to fourteen billion active parameters, and the data-scaling experiments hold the data mix fixed.

Third, and most fundamental. The camera-plus-skeleton interface describes how the camera and hands move, but not physical contact or object dynamics. The model learns what actions look like on screen, not the physics behind them.

### Future work

The authors point in two directions: scale data and model size further, across more embodiments and tasks, and couple the model with simulation-ready, physically grounded 3D assets and scenes, to model the real dynamics.

My read is that the signal worth watching is this: the volume of human video really does turn into capability in a robot world model. If that trend holds at larger scale, part of embodied AI's data bottleneck could shift to collecting and annotating human video.
