## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇伊利诺伊大学厄巴纳-香槟分校的论文，合作方还有香港中文大学深圳和亚马逊，名字叫 CoDA，意思是数据引擎和动作策略的共同进化。

一句话概括，它让负责生成仿真环境的那一方，盯着机器人策略哪里学不会，专门造针对性的练习，再配上对应的训练信号。

### 问题

先说背景。现在训练机器人做操作，大量数据来自仿真。以前搭一个仿真环境很费劲，要设计任务、找物体模型、摆布局、写训练信号，很多靠人手工。

生成式仿真把这件事自动化了，coding agent 能直接写出场景和任务，大模型还能写奖励函数。论文的说法是，环境已经不稀缺了。

真正的问题变成，下一个该造什么环境，以及用什么监督信号，帮策略从这些环境里学到东西。物理上合理的环境，不一定对训练有用。

### 以前的方法

已有的数据引擎大致有三种做法。一种在人工设定的范围里随机采样变化，一种反复修改场景直到它物理上合理，还有一种按每个任务的成功率来分配生成预算。

作者指出，这些信号只说明环境合不合法、策略现在做得怎样，不衡量训练之后策略进步了多少。于是生成器可能把预算花在策略早就会了，或者暂时学不会的环境上。

训练信号这边，以前有让大模型写稠密奖励的工作，比如 Text2Reward。论文说，它的迭代改进要靠人描述策略怎么失败，写出的奖励也可能鼓励完成任务以外的行为。

### 核心想法

CoDA 的核心想法是，给数据引擎和动作策略一份共享的记忆。

策略每次失败，都由一个视觉语言模型来诊断，到底是哪个场景因素造成的，发生在动作的哪个阶段。同一条诊断，会被编译成两份程序，一份是生成程序，负责把这种难点再造出来，一份是训练程序，负责帮策略克服它。

然后用实际测出来的学习进步，决定哪些难点值得继续练。这张图就是整个循环。

### 方法

下面讲具体怎么做。数据引擎是一个带工具的 coding agent，它针对仿真器的开发接口写可执行的场景程序，每个程序还要附带一个机器可以检查的成功判定。

关键设计是把场景拆成三个独立因素，任务、物体间的空间关系，和物体自身属性，比如形状、尺寸、质量、摩擦和外观。这样引擎可以只改一个因素，另外两个不动，来检验诊断对不对。

场景在交给策略之前，要通过三项检查，摆放没有碰撞，短时间被动仿真里保持静止，尺寸合理。

诊断由视觉语言模型完成，它看执行视频、接触信息和关节状态，把失败归到任务、空间、属性或者动作执行这四类之一，再定位到接近、抓取、搬运、放置或插入、松开这五个阶段之一。

共享记忆分三层。第一层逐条记录场景怎么搭的、策略怎么失败的。第二层把反复出现的失败聚成难点模式，论文举的例子是，目标物体旁边，在机械臂接近的那一侧，有一个遮挡物。

每个难点模式有两个指标，难度看策略的成功率，重要性看在它上面训练之后，成功率涨了多少。第三层才是可以复用的生成程序和训练程序。

诊断不能直接信。对每个候选难点，引擎生成两批场景，一批改动诊断指出的那个因素，一批改动另一个因素，作为对照。只有改动诊断因素的那批更难，这个难点才算通过验证。

生成预算用置信上界规则来分，同时看难度和学习进步，另留百分之十五造全新环境，百分之十复查老难点，防止退步。一个生成程序，只有策略成功率在百分之二十到八十之间，才留在练习池里。

训练信号这边，作者提出了 Text2potential。成功判定只告诉你最后成没成，中间进展一概不知，任务一长，价值学习就很难。

Text2potential 让大模型在诊断出的失败阶段把任务切成几个子目标，每个子目标有完成判定和衡量进展的势函数，都写成仿真器状态上的程序，叠起来就是一个分阶段的势函数。

它用的是经典的基于势函数的奖励塑形。作者引用的理论结论是，在一定条件下，这种塑形不改变原任务的最优策略。而且对一整个动作块来说，只需要在块的起点和终点各算一次势函数。

策略本身是 π0.5，一个 30 亿参数、用 flow matching 输出动作的 VLA 模型，每次输出 8 步的动作块。另训一个价值网络给动作块打分，再用优势加权回归更新策略，价值越高的动作块，模仿得越用力。

这种加权不需要计算动作的概率密度，所以 flow matching 和扩散这类策略也能直接用。

场景生成、失败诊断和势函数编译，用的都是 GPT-5.5。仿真器是 MuJoCo，机器人是 7 自由度的 Franka 机械臂。

### 实验和效果

实验分两部分。第一部分是四类操作任务，抓取放置、开合铰接物体、放进容器，以及堆叠和插入。每类有四组训练没见过的测试，分别换新物体实例、新布局、新任务和新类别，每组 100 个环境。

默认跑 5 轮，每类任务的预算是 320 个通过检查的环境，所有结果是三个随机种子的平均。

结果在表 1。分布外的平均成功率，直接用发布的 π0.5 是 2.3%，用种子示范做监督微调是 5.3%，域随机化是 7.9%，均匀生成变体是 8.5%。

按成功率引导生成的闭环方法是 18.1%，用 CoDA 的诊断但不要记忆是 19.2%，完整的 CoDA 是 27.6%。在同样的生成预算下，比最强的闭环基线高 9.5 个百分点，四类任务上都是第一。

这张图看更长的训练。从 5 轮延长到 15 轮，CoDA 的分布外成功率从 27.6% 涨到 42.1%，均匀生成、域随机化和按成功率引导的方法，额外提升要小得多。

第二部分是 LIBERO-Pro，它在标准 LIBERO 的基础上，扰动物体的位置，或者改动指令。

原版 π0.5 在标准 LIBERO 上是 97.1%，但在这两种扰动下，总体成功率只有 25% 和 1%，OpenVLA 和 π0 都是零。

用 CoDA 在 LIBERO 源任务的变体上训练 15 轮后，这两个数字变成 84% 和 76%，比代码即策略的方法 ASPIRE 分别高 7 和 9 个百分点，不过 Object 这一组上 ASPIRE 仍然最强。标准 LIBERO 没掉，是 97.4%。

再看迁移。用 π0.5 积累的记忆，直接训练 70 亿参数的 OpenVLA-OFT，不生成新环境，它的分布外成功率从 3.8% 涨到 21.1%，离它自己完整跑 CoDA 的 22.7% 不到 2 个点。

生成程序也能跨任务类别复用。平均来看，迁移过来的程序，能带来同类别自己学的程序百分之六十点三的学习进步，而用同样的大模型 token 预算从头学，只有百分之三十五点八。

### 和以前方法的对比

消融实验能看出哪些部件真起作用。先看表 4 的 Text2potential。同样训练预算，只用最终成功信号是 22.3%，用 Text2potential 是 27.6%。达到 25% 分布外成功率，策略更新从 3.1 万步降到 2.1 万步，作者称约少百分之三十二。

如果势函数不是从失败诊断来，而是直接从任务描述生成，成绩掉到 24.6%。如果把策略冻结，只用价值网络挑动作块，只有 21.4%，说明用学到的价值去更新策略是有好处的。

再看记忆，在表 5。只保留生成程序是 22.8%，只保留训练程序是 22.1%，两样都留是 27.6%。相对于没有记忆的 19.2%，合起来 8.4 个点的提升，超过了两者单独提升之和。

有意思的是，用同一种记忆同时服务生成和训练的几种做法，包括 Voyager 那样的技能库，都不如只留任一种专门程序。这支持了作者把两类程序分开存的设计。

诊断的验证也有用。对照实验否掉了 18.2% 的诊断，经过这一步，诊断和人工标注的一致率从 73.6% 提到 82.4%。换成另外三个视觉语言模型来做诊断，成绩降到 24.9% 到 26.1% 之间，但都高于按成功率引导的基线。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把大模型的诊断当成待检验的假设，而不是直接相信。只改一个因素、留一组对照，做法很朴素，但让整个闭环可信了很多。

我的判断是，也要看清代价和差距。每一千个通过检查的环境，CoDA 要用 740 万大模型 token 和 66 个 GPU 小时，按成功率引导的基线是 460 万和 51 个小时，同样多的环境它更贵。而且 27.6% 的分布外成功率，绝对值还不高。

### 局限

作者自己列了几条局限。第一，整个系统靠基础模型来生成场景、诊断失败和编译势函数，模型对物体、物理交互或任务理解错了，场景和训练信号都会跟着错。

物理检查和对照实验能发现一部分错误，但覆盖面取决于用了哪些检查。换了底层模型，结果也可能变，所以模型版本对复现很重要。

第二，生成环境、评估策略执行、验证诊断，都要额外的仿真和模型推理，这限制了在固定预算下能跑多少轮、覆盖多少任务类别。

第三，所有评估都在仿真里，迁移到真实机器人还没有验证，尤其是对接触动力学、传感和执行器差异敏感的任务。

### 未来方向

论文没有单独写未来工作。从局限看，最直接的下一步是上真机，验证仿真里练出的能力能不能带到现实。

作者在结论里强调了两种复用，生成程序跨任务类别迁移，记忆跨策略模型复用。

我的判断是，这篇指向的方向，是数据引擎不只造更多数据，而是围绕策略的弱点造对的数据。如果诊断和程序真能跨任务、跨模型积累下来，它会更像一个越来越厚的题库，而不是一次性的数据集。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, with co-authors at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen, and Amazon. It's called CoDA, short for co-evolution of data engines and action policies.

In one line: the system that generates simulated environments watches where the robot policy keeps failing, builds practice targeted at exactly that, and pairs it with a matching training signal.

### Problem

Some background. A lot of robot manipulation data now comes from simulation. Building a simulated environment used to be slow work: you need a task design, the right object models, a sensible layout, and a training signal, much of it written by hand.

Generative simulation automated that. Coding agents write the scenes and tasks directly, and language models write the reward functions. As the paper puts it, environments are no longer scarce.

So the real question becomes which environment to build next, and what supervision will help the policy actually learn from it. A physically valid environment isn't necessarily a useful one.

### Prior work

Existing data engines do roughly one of three things. They sample variations from hand-set ranges, they refine a scene until it's physically valid, or they split the generation budget by per-task success rate.

The authors' point is that these signals tell you whether an environment is valid and how the policy does right now. They don't measure how much the policy improves from training. So a generator can burn its budget on environments the policy has already mastered, or can't learn yet.

On the training-signal side, earlier work like Text2Reward has language models write dense rewards. But the paper notes that its refinement loop relies on humans describing how the policy failed, and the rewards can end up favoring behavior other than finishing the task.

### Key idea

CoDA's core idea is to give the data engine and the action policy one shared memory.

Every time the policy fails, a vision-language model diagnoses which scene factor caused it and in which phase of the motion. That one diagnosis is compiled into two programs: a generation program that recreates the difficulty, and a training program that helps the policy overcome it.

Then measured learning progress decides which difficulties are worth revisiting. This figure shows the whole loop.

### Method

Here's how it works. The data engine is a tool-using coding agent that writes executable scene programs against the simulator's SDK, and every program has to come with a machine-checkable success test.

The key design choice is splitting each scene into three independent factors: the task, the spatial relations between objects, and the objects' own attributes, like shape, scale, mass, friction and appearance. That lets the engine change one factor and hold the other two fixed, which is how it tests a diagnosis.

Before any scene reaches the policy, it has to pass three checks: no collisions in the placement, staying still during a short passive simulation, and a plausible scale.

The diagnosis comes from a vision-language model that sees the rollout video, contact information and joint states. It assigns each failure to one of four factors, task, spatial, attribute, or plain motor execution, and to one of five phases: approach, grasp, transport, place or insert, and release.

The shared memory has three tiers. The first records each scene build and each failure. The second clusters recurring failures into difficulty modes. The paper's example is an occluder next to the target, on the side the arm approaches from.

Each mode carries two numbers. Hardness is the policy's success rate on it, and importance is how much that success rate rises after training on it. The third tier holds the reusable generation and training programs.

And the diagnoses aren't taken on trust. For each candidate difficulty, the engine generates two batches: one that varies the factor the diagnosis blamed, and a control batch that varies a different factor. Only if the targeted batch is harder does the difficulty count as validated.

The generation budget is split with an upper-confidence rule that weighs hardness and learning progress. Fifteen percent is reserved for brand-new environments, and ten percent for replaying old difficulties to catch regressions. A generation program stays in the pool only while the policy's success rate on it is between twenty and eighty percent.

For the training signal, the authors propose Text2potential. A success test only tells you whether the task finished, nothing about progress along the way, and on long tasks that makes value learning hard.

Text2potential has a language model split the task at the diagnosed failure phase into subgoals. Each subgoal gets a completion test and a potential function that measures progress, all written as code over the simulator state. Stack the subgoals and you get a staged potential.

This is classic potential-based reward shaping. The authors cite the theory that, under certain conditions, this kind of shaping doesn't change the task's optimal policy. And for a whole action chunk, you only need to evaluate the potential at the chunk's start and end.

The policy itself is pi zero point five, a three-billion-parameter VLA model with a flow-matching action head, emitting chunks of eight actions. A separate value network scores action chunks, and the policy is updated by advantage-weighted regression, meaning it imitates higher-value chunks more strongly.

That weighting doesn't need the action's probability density, so it works directly with flow-matching and diffusion policies.

Scene generation, failure diagnosis and potential compilation all use GPT-5.5. The simulator is MuJoCo, and the robot is a seven-degree-of-freedom Franka arm.

### Results

The experiments come in two parts. First, four task families: pick-and-place, opening and closing articulated objects, putting things inside containers, and stacking or insertion. Each family has four held-out test suites the policy never trained on, with new object instances, new layouts, new tasks and new categories, one hundred environments each.

The default run is five rounds, with a budget of three hundred and twenty accepted environments per family, and every result is averaged over three random seeds.

The results are in Table 1. Average out-of-distribution success is two point three percent for the released pi zero point five checkpoint, five point three with supervised fine-tuning on seed demonstrations, seven point nine with domain randomization, and eight point five with uniformly generated variants.

The closed-loop method guided by success rates reaches eighteen point one. CoDA's diagnoses without the memory reach nineteen point two. Full CoDA reaches twenty-seven point six. That's nine and a half points above the strongest closed-loop baseline at the same generation budget, and it's best in all four families.

This figure shows longer training. Going from five to fifteen rounds lifts CoDA's out-of-distribution success from twenty-seven point six to forty-two point one percent, while uniform generation, domain randomization and success-rate-guided generation gain much less.

The second part is LIBERO-Pro, which takes the standard LIBERO benchmark and perturbs object positions or changes the instructions.

The original pi zero point five scores ninety-seven point one percent on standard LIBERO. Under these two perturbations, its overall success is just twenty-five percent and one percent. OpenVLA and pi zero score zero.

After fifteen rounds of CoDA training on variants of the LIBERO source tasks, those numbers become eighty-four and seventy-six percent, seven and nine points above ASPIRE, a code-as-policy method. ASPIRE is still strongest on the Object suite, though. And standard LIBERO doesn't drop: it's ninety-seven point four percent.

There are transfer results too. Take the memory accumulated with pi zero point five and use it to train the seven-billion-parameter OpenVLA-OFT, with no new environments generated. Its out-of-distribution success goes from three point eight to twenty-one point one percent, less than two points short of the twenty-two point seven it gets from running full CoDA itself.

Generation programs also carry over between task families. On average, transferred programs deliver sixty point three percent of the learning progress of programs learned in the target family, versus thirty-five point eight percent when programs are learned from scratch on the same language-model token budget.

### Comparison

The ablations show which parts matter. Start with Text2potential, in Table 4. At the same training budget, the sparse success signal alone gives twenty-two point three percent, and Text2potential gives twenty-seven point six. Reaching twenty-five percent out-of-distribution success takes twenty-one thousand policy updates instead of thirty-one thousand, which the authors put at roughly thirty-two percent fewer.

If the potential is generated from the task description instead of the failure diagnosis, success drops to twenty-four point six. If the policy is frozen and the value network only picks among action chunks, it's twenty-one point four, so using the learned values to update the policy helps.

Next, memory, in Table 5. Keeping only generation programs gives twenty-two point eight. Keeping only training programs gives twenty-two point one. Keeping both gives twenty-seven point six. Against nineteen point two with no memory, the combined gain of eight point four points is larger than the two individual gains added up.

Interestingly, the variants that use a single memory for both generation and training, including a Voyager-style skill library, all do worse than keeping either specialized program type alone. That backs the authors' choice to store the two kinds of programs separately.

Validating the diagnoses pays off too. The control test rejects eighteen point two percent of diagnoses, and with that step, agreement with human annotators rises from seventy-three point six to eighty-two point four percent. Swapping in three other vision-language models as the diagnoser lowers success to between twenty-four point nine and twenty-six point one, still above the success-rate-guided baseline.

My read is that the most valuable thing here is treating the model's diagnosis as a hypothesis to test rather than an answer to trust. Change one factor, keep a control: it's a simple move, but it makes the whole loop far more credible.

My read is also that the costs and the gap deserve attention. Per thousand accepted environments, CoDA uses seven point four million language-model tokens and sixty-six GPU-hours, against four point six million and fifty-one for the success-rate-guided baseline, so the same number of environments costs more. And twenty-seven point six percent out-of-distribution success is still low in absolute terms.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, the system depends on foundation models to generate scenes, diagnose failures and compile potentials. If those models misunderstand objects, physical interactions or task requirements, the scenes and training signals inherit the errors.

Physical validity checks and control experiments catch some of those errors, but their coverage depends on which checks are used. Results can also shift when the underlying models change, which makes model versions important for reproducibility.

Second, generating environments, evaluating rollouts and validating diagnoses all need extra simulation and model inference, which limits how many rounds and task families fit in a fixed budget.

Third, everything is evaluated in simulation. Transfer to physical robots hasn't been shown, especially for tasks sensitive to differences in contact dynamics, sensing and actuation.

### Future work

The paper has no separate future-work section. Reading from its limitations, the obvious next step is real robots: checking whether what's learned in simulation carries over to the physical world.

In their conclusion, the authors stress two kinds of reuse: generation programs transferring across task families, and memory reused across policy backbones.

My read is that this points toward data engines that don't just make more data, but make the right data around a policy's weaknesses. If diagnoses and programs really do accumulate across tasks and models, this starts to look like a growing bank of practice problems rather than a one-off dataset.
