## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇香港理工大学的论文，题目叫 Non-penetrating World Models，意思是，不穿墙的世界模型。

它研究的问题听起来很简单，在视频世界模型里，让镜头朝一堵墙走过去，它会不会直接穿过去。答案是，作者测的六个模型里，五个会。

### 问题

先说什么是交互式视频世界模型。它有点像一个用 AI 生成画面的游戏引擎，你给它按键，或者镜头怎么移动的指令，它就一段一段地生成接下来的视频，论文里一段叫一个 chunk。

问题在于，它只管听指令，不检查这个动作在物理上能不能做到。训练视频里几乎没有被墙挡住的镜头，所以撞墙这种指令，对模型来说很可能是没见过的情况。

结果就是这张图。作者测了六个开源世界模型，命令镜头往墙里走，五个直接穿了过去，剩下一个 WorldPlay2，干脆往后退，也没有按用户的指令走。

论文开头用了一个比喻，哈利波特里的九又四分之三站台，可以穿过墙进入另一个世界。现在的世界模型，好像把每一堵墙都当成了这个站台。

### 以前的方法

在计算机图形学里，这个问题早就有解法。游戏引擎和物理模拟器靠碰撞检测和碰撞响应，发现要撞上，就调整运动，同时尽量保留原本想走的方向。

但这些方法都需要明确的三维几何，而视频生成模型里，根本没有存几何信息。

那么直接的办法有两个。第一个，在提示词里告诉模型，前面有墙，碰到了要停下，或者沿着墙滑过去。作者发现，这样做几乎没有效果。

第二个，接一个外部的深度估计模型，看出前面有障碍物，就把运动停下。这确实有效，但每一段都要先把画面解码出来，再多跑一个网络，而且它只会停，不会滑，用户想走的方向也就没了。

### 核心想法

作者的关键发现是，生成模型自己，其实已经知道墙在哪。

具体说，扩散 Transformer，也就是 DiT，它的中间隐藏状态，就能预测一个指令会不会导致穿墙，而且是在下一段视频生成出来之前。

用作者的话说，生成器已经知道它的墙在哪里，只是输出没有按这个知识去做。所以 NoPeWorld 不靠外部工具，而是直接读生成器的隐藏状态，来判断动作安不安全。

### 方法

方法借鉴了图形学的两条原则。第一，实心物体不能互相穿透。第二，撞上之后，正对着墙就停下，斜着撞上就沿着墙滑过去，这叫 collide and slide。

落到世界模型上，每一步作者准备了 34 个候选动作，包括用户原本的指令、停下、缩短的步子，以及最多 90 度的转向。目标是，在安全的候选里，选一个最接近用户意图的。

但生成模型里没有几何信息，安全不安全、离意图多远，都没法直接算。所以作者训练了一个小的预测头，接在冻结的 DiT 上，参数不到 200 万，原模型一个参数都不改。

这个头读 DiT 中间偏深的几层，对每个候选动作输出三样东西，安全的概率，离障碍物还有多远，以及和用户意图有多接近。

训练数据来自 Unreal 引擎。作者在 UnrealZoo 的 44 个场景里录了 729 段视频，喂给冻结的 DiT，再用引擎里的真实地图，给每个候选动作打标签，看它会不会撞上、离墙多远。这些训练场景，和测试场景完全不重叠。

推理的时候，规则很简单。用户的指令如果安全，就原样执行。如果不安全，就换成安全候选里最贴近用户意图的那个，一个安全的都没有，就停下。

读隐藏状态有两种时机，所以有两种模式。预测模式，读的是上一段视频生成完之后缓存的状态，在下一段开始之前就决定动作，不需要多跑一次 DiT。

碰撞模式，读的是当前这一段去噪的第一步。如果指令被换掉了，只把第一步重跑一遍，后面照常进行。

这张图把训练和两种推理模式画在了一起。

### 实验和效果

为了评测，作者还做了一个基准，叫 NoPeBench。以前的基准大多绕着障碍物走，作者数了一下，WBench 的 158 个导航案例里，只有 13 个真正开向可见的障碍物。而 NoPeBench 里，每一条测试指令都开向一个实心障碍物。

它有三个任务。任务一，在 32 个没见过的场景里取 100 条路线，用真实的历史画面，只看每一步的决策对不对。任务二，同样的路线，让模型闭环生成 5 秒视频，再做三维重建，把镜头轨迹放回引擎的真实地图上，判断有没有穿墙。

任务三，换成 131 张开放域的图片，有静止的障碍，也有移动的障碍，有第一人称，也有第三人称。这里没有三维真值，就用两个闭源多模态大模型当裁判，作者先拿人工标注检验过它们。另外还有 50 条前方没有障碍的路线，用来检查方法会不会乱刹车。

先看任务一，结果在表 3。不加干预的原始模型，危险指令全部照单全收。加上 NoPeWorld，两种模式都把漏掉的危险指令降到百分之 0.6 到百分之 11.5，决策分提高了 17 到 33 分。

再看任务二，主结果在表 4。以 LingBot-World 2.0 为例，原始模型在 89% 的路线上穿了墙，预测模式降到 4%。安全意图分，也就是既安全、又照着用户方向走才给分的指标，从 5 提到了 56。

六个模型平均下来，碰撞模式把违规率从 74% 降到 16%。而在提示词里写明障碍物的名字、方位和距离，几乎没有帮助，LingBot 的违规率反而从 89% 变成了 94%。

外部深度估计的方法，在其中四个模型上违规最少，但它是靠停下来做到的，镜头卡在墙前，不会沿着墙滑。NoPeWorld 在五个靠镜头位姿驱动的模型上，安全意图分和总分都是最好的，最高是外部深度方法的 3.1 倍。

在前方没有障碍的 50 条路线上，两种模式在百分之 92 到 100 的视频里，既没有提前停下，也没有后退，说明它很少乱刹车。

任务三最有意思。预测头只在 Unreal 的静态场景、第一人称视角下训练过，但换到不是引擎渲染的图片、第三人称，还有移动的障碍物，两种模式在每个模型上都降低了违规率。比如 LingBot 在静态障碍上，从 67% 降到 19%。这张图是一些例子。

在 12 组模型和场景的组合里，碰撞模式有 10 组追平或者超过外部深度方法，带移动障碍物的 6 组全部在内。

### 和以前方法的对比

为什么读 DiT 有用，作者做了分析。他们用后悔值来衡量，就是选的动作比最好的动作差多少。在 LingBot 上，同样一个小预测头，读 DiT 的隐藏状态，后悔值大约是一直照原指令走的一半，而读 VAE 潜变量或者像素，几乎没有改善。

而且在 LingBot 上，这个信号分布在中间和较深的很多层里，读当前这一段时，从第十层往后读，效果都差不多。作者认为，这可能是同一种预测头设计能用在六个模型上的原因。

成本看表 5，测的是 LingBot。原始模型生成一条路线要 53.7 秒，预测模式只多 0.7 秒，大约百分之一，碰撞模式多 9.9 秒，外部深度方法多 14.7 秒，大约百分之二十七。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的不是那个小预测头，而是那个发现，模型知道，却不去做。它说明世界模型的物理问题，不一定要重新训练整个模型，读出它已有的知识，在外面加一层约束，就能补上一部分。

我的判断是，和外部深度方法相比，它的优势是能滑，不只是停，而且预测模式几乎不加成本。但如果只看违规率，外部深度方法在不少模型上更低，两者是不同的取舍。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限一节，但从结果里能看出几点。第一，效果不均匀。在 Hy-Nomas 上，碰撞模式的违规率还有 40%，任务一里安全指令被误改的比例，也在百分之 31 到 49 之间。

第二，WorldPlay2 是唯一用按键驱动的模型，它本来就会后退，而不是穿墙。加上 NoPeWorld 后，违规从 11% 降到 1%，但安全意图分反而低于原始模型，因为它的停下吃掉了一部分原本的滑动。

第三，即使在效果最好的模型上，安全意图分也只有一半左右，离引擎里真实的停和滑还有不小的距离。任务一和任务二的路线还排除了玻璃、细杆和移动的物体，任务三则依赖大模型裁判。

还有一点，方法假设生成器会老老实实画出被选中的动作，这一点要靠任务二来检验，而且每个生成模型都要单独训练一个预测头。

### 未来方向

作者在结论里说，实心的边界只是第一步，接下来要处理的，是会推动、搬运、打碎物体的接触。代码、基准和模型权重，论文说都已经公开。

我的判断是，这个思路可能推广到别的物理问题上。只要模型内部已经有某种知识，就可以用一个便宜的读出头，加一层规则，把它变成约束，不过这需要新的实验来证明。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, and it's called Non-penetrating World Models.

The question sounds almost too simple. In a video world model, if you steer the camera straight into a wall, does it walk right through? Of the six models the authors tested, five do.

### Problem

First, what's an interactive video world model? Think of it as a game engine where every frame is generated by AI. You send it key presses or camera moves, and it generates the next piece of video, one chunk at a time.

The trouble is that it just follows the command. It never checks whether the motion is physically possible. And because training videos almost never show a camera blocked by a wall, a command that drives into one is likely out of distribution.

Here's what that looks like. The authors tested six open-source world models and told each camera to walk into a wall. Five went straight through. The sixth, WorldPlay2, backed away instead, which also ignores what the user asked for.

The paper opens with Harry Potter's Platform Nine and Three Quarters, where you can walk through a wall into another world. Today's world models act as if every wall were that platform.

### Prior work

Computer graphics solved this long ago. Game engines and physics simulators use collision detection and collision response: they spot an impending hit and adjust the motion, while keeping as much of the intended movement as they can.

But all of those methods need explicit 3D geometry, and a video generator doesn't store any.

So there are two obvious fixes. The first is to put it in the prompt: there's a wall ahead, stop or slide along it when you reach it. The authors found this has little effect.

The second is to bolt on an external depth estimator and stop the motion when an obstacle shows up. That works, but every chunk has to be decoded into pixels and run through an extra network, and it can only stop, not slide, so the user's intended motion is lost.

### Key idea

The key finding is that the generator already knows where the wall is.

Specifically, the hidden states of the diffusion transformer, the DiT, can predict whether a command will cause penetration, before the next chunk of video is even generated.

In the authors' words, the generator already knows where its walls are, but its output fails to act on that knowledge. So instead of external tools, NoPeWorld reads the generator's own hidden states to decide whether an action is safe.

### Method

The method borrows two rules from graphics. First, solid objects can't overlap. Second, on contact, you stop if you hit a wall head-on, and slide along it if you hit it at an angle. That's called collide and slide.

In the world model, each step offers thirty-four candidate motions: the user's original command, a stop, shorter steps, and turns of up to ninety degrees. The goal is to pick the safe candidate that stays closest to what the user intended.

But without geometry inside the generator, neither safety nor intent can be computed directly. So the authors train a small prediction head on top of the frozen DiT. It has under two million parameters, and the original model isn't changed at all.

The head reads middle-to-deep DiT blocks and, for each candidate, outputs three things: the probability it's safe, how much clearance it leaves, and how well it matches the user's intent.

Training data comes from Unreal Engine. The authors replay seven hundred twenty-nine videos from forty-four UnrealZoo scenes through the frozen DiT, and label every candidate using the engine's true map: does it collide, and how far is the wall. None of these training scenes appear in the benchmark.

At inference, the rule is simple. If the user's command is safe, it runs as is. If not, it's replaced by the safe candidate closest to the intent, and if nothing is safe, the camera stops.

There are two moments to read the hidden states, so there are two modes. Prediction mode reads the cached state from the previous chunk and picks the action before the next chunk starts, with no extra DiT pass.

Collision mode reads the first denoising step of the current chunk. If the command gets changed, only that first step is rerun, and the rest proceeds as normal.

### Results

To measure all this, the authors built a benchmark called NoPeBench. Existing benchmarks mostly steer around obstacles; by the authors' count, only thirteen of WBench's one hundred fifty-eight navigation cases actually drive into a visible obstacle. In NoPeBench, every test command drives into a solid one.

It has three tasks. Task one takes one hundred routes in thirty-two unseen scenes, replays the real history, and only checks whether each decision is right. Task two runs the same routes closed-loop for five seconds of generated video, then reconstructs the camera path in 3D on the engine's true map to check for penetration.

Task three switches to one hundred thirty-one open-domain images, with static and moving obstacles, in first and third person. There's no 3D ground truth here, so two closed multimodal models act as judges, after the authors checked them against human labels. There are also fifty routes with nothing in the way, to catch a method that brakes for no reason.

Task one first, in Table three. The raw model keeps every unsafe command. With NoPeWorld, both modes cut missed unsafe commands to between zero point six and eleven point five percent, and raise the decision score by seventeen to thirty-three points.

Now task two, the main results in Table four. Take LingBot-World two point oh. The raw model goes through the wall on eighty-nine percent of routes, and prediction mode brings that down to four percent. The Safe-Intent Score, which only gives credit when a video is both safe and follows the user's direction, goes from five to fifty-six.

Averaged over all six models, collision mode cuts the violation rate from seventy-four percent to sixteen. Spelling out the obstacle's name, side and distance in the prompt barely helps; on LingBot, violations actually went from eighty-nine to ninety-four percent.

The external depth method has the fewest violations on four of the models, but it gets there by halting: the camera sticks at the wall and never slides. On the five pose-driven models, NoPeWorld has the best Safe-Intent Score and the best overall score, up to three point one times the Safe-Intent Score of external depth.

On the fifty routes with a clear path, both modes neither stop early nor back up in ninety-two to one hundred percent of videos, so it rarely brakes for nothing.

Task three is the interesting one. The head was trained only on static Unreal scenes from a first-person view, yet on images not rendered by the engine, from third person, and with moving obstacles, both modes lower the violation rate on every model. On LingBot with static obstacles, it drops from sixty-seven percent to nineteen. Here are some examples.

Across twelve combinations of model and setting, collision mode matches or beats external depth in ten, including all six with moving obstacles.

### Comparison

Why does reading the DiT work? The authors measure regret, how much worse the chosen motion is than the best one. On LingBot, the same small head, reading DiT states, roughly halves the regret of always keeping the user's command. Reading VAE latents or raw pixels barely helps at all.

On LingBot, the signal is also spread across many middle and deep blocks; for the current chunk, reading from the tenth block onward works about equally well. The authors suggest that's why one head design can serve all six models.

Cost is in Table five, measured on LingBot. A raw route takes fifty-three point seven seconds. Prediction mode adds zero point seven seconds, about one percent. Collision mode adds nine point nine seconds, and external depth adds fourteen point seven, about twenty-seven percent.

My read is that the real contribution isn't the small head, it's the finding: the model knows, but doesn't act. It suggests some physical failures in world models don't require retraining the whole model. You read out what it already knows and add a constraint on top.

My read is that, against external depth, the win is sliding instead of only stopping, and prediction mode costs almost nothing. But on raw violation rate, external depth is lower on several models, so it's a different trade-off, not a clean sweep.

### Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations section, but the results show a few. First, gains are uneven. On Hy-Nomas, collision mode still violates on forty percent of routes, and in task one it changes thirty-one to forty-nine percent of safe commands.

Second, WorldPlay2 is the only keystroke-driven model, and it natively backs away rather than going through. NoPeWorld cuts its violations from eleven percent to one, but its Safe-Intent Score ends up below the raw model's, because its stops eat into the model's own sliding.

Third, even on the best model the Safe-Intent Score is only about half, still well short of the engine's real stops and slides. The task one and two routes also leave out glass, thin poles and moving bodies, and task three relies on model judges.

And the method assumes the generator faithfully renders whatever motion is chosen, which task two has to check, and each generator needs its own trained head.

### Future work

In the conclusion, the authors say solid boundaries are only the first step; next comes contact that pushes, carries and shatters objects. The code, the benchmark and the model weights are released, according to the paper.

My read is that the recipe could extend to other physical failures. If a model already encodes something internally, a cheap readout head plus a rule can turn it into a constraint. That still has to be shown in new experiments.
