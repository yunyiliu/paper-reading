## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇韩国汉阳大学的论文，名字叫 V-JEPA-LM，讲的是怎么让视频语言模型，真正去读画面里的运动。

它的出发点是一个有点反直觉的发现，视频编码器明明分得出物体动得快还是慢，接在后面的语言模型，回答这类问题却和瞎猜差不多。

### 问题

先说背景。V-JEPA 是一类视频编码器，它不看任何文字，只靠预测视频里被遮住的部分来训练。论文把它叫做世界模型编码器，它学到的是物理世界怎么运动。

要让它用语言回答问题，就得接一个语言模型，再用视频描述和问答数据去训练。问题在于，每条描述或问题，只提到画面里的少数几个属性，模型学会的是按文字去找对应的东西，而不是把视频读一遍，这类题大多看一帧就能答。

作者做了个测量。同一批判断速度的题，在冻结的 V-JEPA 特征上接一个线性探针，也就是一个最简单的线性分类器，准确率是 86.7。可是整个模型自己作答，只有 33.7，这是三选一的题，等于随机。

### 以前的方法

以前是怎么做的。开源的视频大模型，一般用和文字对齐过的图像编码器，再配一个语言模型，在大量的描述和问答数据上训练。

Meta 的 V-JEPA 2 和 2.1 做成视频大模型时，走的也是这套流程，对齐、预训练、指令微调几个阶段，用了 8850 万和 7250 万条样本，而且只放出了编码器，没有放出视频大模型。

作者认为，堆规模解决不了这个问题，换更强的编码器也不行。他们引用 Meta 的对照实验，同样的流程下换成 V-JEPA 2.1，在 TemporalBench 和 TOMATO 这两个基准上，分数反而比 V-JEPA 2 低。缺的是一种非看变化不可的监督信号。

### 核心想法

核心想法分两步。第一步，先搞清楚运动信息在编码器里是什么样子。

把一段视频倒放，对 SigLIP 这种一帧一帧编码、又和文字对齐过的编码器来说，只是同一批 token 换了个顺序，时间的方向，不在任何一个 token 的内容里。

V-JEPA 不一样。倒放、镜像或者变速之后，它的 token 会沿着一个变化分量移动，而且不同视频移动的方向是一致的。按作者的度量，这种一致性比 SigLIP 高 20 到 100 倍。

第二步，就按这个结构来设计监督。同一段视频和它变换之后的版本，问同一个问题，两个答案被设计成不一样，那么能区分它们的，只有变化本身。

看论文这张图。同一段视频，正着放，答案是打开电饭煲，倒着放，答案就成了关上电饭煲。画面相同，问题相同，决定答案的只有变化。作者把这叫做反事实监督。

### 方法

具体怎么造数据。一个生成器从公开数据集里取一段视频，施加一个像素层面的变换，再写出问题、选项和答案。答案由变换本身决定，作者称不需要新的人工标注，也不需要语言模型参与。

一共 14 种构造，分成 6 类。有倒放、镜像这类让答案翻转的，有事件的先后顺序，有动作出现在开头、中间还是结尾，有速度的快慢，有重复的次数，还有动作有没有做完、画面是否违反物理规律。

拿速度举例。同一段视频上下各放一份，两份截取的时间跨度不一样，跨度长的那份动得更快。把两个跨度对调，答案就跟着反过来。

生成器还做了平衡。每个场景配每种答案的机会，在期望上是均等的，正确选项的长短排名，还有纯文本语言模型对各个选项的偏好，也都做了平衡。

这些反事实数据一共 50.9 万行，来自 33 个数据源，只占训练采样的百分之六左右。

原来的描述和问答数据保持不变，继续负责教语言和知识。

模型本身很简单。V-JEPA 2.1 编码器全程冻结，读 32 帧，中间一个作者叫做 spectral bridge 的连接模块，把每段视频变成 1024 个 token，再交给语言模型。

训练只有一个阶段，用大约 450 万条公开样本，来自 175 个公开数据源。语言模型用 Qwen3 和 Llama，从 1B 到 8B，一共 6 个模型。

### 实验和效果

实验设置。作者在 11 个视频基准上算 12 项分数，取平均。所有开源模型，都由作者用同一套设置重新评测，统一 32 帧。

主结果在表 4。4B 档，V-JEPA-LM 平均 72.14，同档最好的开源模型 Molmo2-4B 是 61.08，高出 11.1 分。它也比 8B 档最好的开源模型 PLM-8B 的 64.79，高 7.4 分。

2B 档平均 66.02，比同档最好的 Qwen3-VL-2B 高 13.6 分。

差距最大的是考运动的基准。4B 档里，TOMATO 上它是 53.5，其他开源模型最高 36.5，Vinoground 的视频项它是 66.8，其他最高 33.8。

数据量上，作者统计，和报告了样本数的开源模型相比，它用的训练样本少 2 到 38 倍，视觉 token 少 2 到 8 倍。4B 模型的训练用了 499 个 H100 小时。

再和 Meta 没有公开的 V-JEPA 视频大模型比。在双方都报告的 6 个基准上，两个 8B 模型的平均分领先 6.7 和 4.9 分。不过 Meta 的数字是从论文里引用的，评测设置并不完全相同。

作者还造了 6 组训练里没见过的测试，比如没出现过的速度比例、没用过的视频来源。V-JEPA-LM 平均 94.5 到 96.3，开源模型是 43.5 到 55.5。把视频换成同一帧重复，分数掉到 37.9 和 37.6，作者据此说明，单帧解不了这些题。

### 和以前方法的对比

那么提升来自编码器，还是来自监督数据。作者做了一个二乘二的对照，4 个 2B 模型，编码器用 V-JEPA 或者 SigLIP，加或者不加反事实数据，其余的数据和训练安排都相同。

结果在表 6。SigLIP 不加是 58.52，加了是 62.13。V-JEPA 不加是 61.53，加了是 66.02。两种编码器都受益，V-JEPA 多 4.5 分，SigLIP 多 3.6 分。

但具体到能力，差别很大。看速度类的题，训练到 8000 步时，不加反事实数据，SigLIP 是 34.0，V-JEPA 是 32.4。加了之后，SigLIP 到 45.4，V-JEPA 到 65.3。

作者的总结是，编码器决定能读出什么，监督决定实际读了什么。方向、计数、精细动作来自编码器，速度、镜头运动、状态变化，要靠监督。

还有一个对照。把反事实数据换成同样权重的一种题，只问视频是正放还是倒放，这是之前一篇工作的做法。模型学会了这道题，在没见过的视频上准确率 95.9，但最后的 12 项平均是 61.17，并不比只用描述和问答的 61.53 高。

作者的结论是，提升来自反事实这种构造方式，而不是时间类的题目变多了。

回到开头那个测量。加了反事实数据之后，同一批速度题，模型自己的回答从 33.7 升到 92.8。而 SigLIP 这一边，不管加不加，没有哪一层超过 40。

作者还做了干预实验。推理的时候，从每个视觉 token 里去掉 8 个速度方向，一组没见过的速度组合题，准确率从 98.9 掉到 65.7，而去掉同样能量的随机子空间，还有 97.2。

我的判断是，这篇最扎实的是那个二乘二对照，它把功劳拆开了，只换编码器不够，只加数据也不够，两样要配在一起。

我的判断是，对比里有两处要留意。开源模型的分数是作者统一用 32 帧重测的，而论文附录列出，其中几个模型自己报告成绩时用的帧数更多。那 6 组没见过的测试，题目措辞和选项也沿用了训练数据生成器的写法，九十多分对四五十分的差距，不能直接当成真实场景里的差距。

### 局限

局限，作者自己列了三条。第一，只处理短视频。所有模型每段读 32 帧，基准里的题目针对的是几秒到几分钟的片段，小时级的长视频留给以后。

第二，不做图片问答，文档、图表、文字识别都不在目标之内，因为 V-JEPA 的训练从来不涉及读文字。作者也承认，在 NExT-QA 和 PerceptionTest 这类偏知识的视频基准上，数据多 2 到 38 倍的开源模型仍然占优。

比如 4B 档的 NExT-QA，V-JEPA-LM 是 79.3，Molmo2-4B 是 85.1。

第三，每个模型只训练了一次，用 450 万公开样本。几亿样本那种通用大模型的规模，不在这项研究的范围之内。

### 未来方向

未来方向，作者提到长视频。他们认为，每段视频 1024 个 token 的紧凑表示，适合作为长视频理解的基础模块，这部分留给以后的工作。

开源方面，论文说模型权重和推理代码已经放出，训练代码、数据生成器和数据清单之后会公开。

我的判断是，这篇给做视频理解的人提了个醒，模型答不对运动类的问题，未必是编码器看不见，也可能是训练数据从来没要求它去看。先用探针量一量编码器里有什么，再决定造什么数据，这个思路比分数本身更值得借鉴。

我的判断是，也要看到边界。它领先的是平均分和运动类的题，偏知识的基准上还落后，适用范围也只是短视频，离通用的视频模型还有距离。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Hanyang University in Seoul. It's called V-JEPA-LM, and it's about getting a video-language model to actually read the motion in a clip.

It starts from a finding that sounds wrong at first. The video encoder can clearly tell whether something is moving fast or slow, and yet the language model sitting on top of it answers that kind of question no better than guessing.

### Problem

Some background. V-JEPA is a family of video encoders that never see text. They're trained only to predict masked parts of a video, and the paper calls them world-model encoders, because what they learn is how the physical world moves.

To answer questions in language, you attach a language model and train on video captions and question-answer pairs. The trouble is that each caption or question mentions only a few attributes of the clip. So the model learns to fetch what the text asks about, not to read the clip, and most of these items can be answered from a single frame.

The authors measured it. On one set of speed questions, a linear probe, the simplest possible classifier, reads the answer from frozen V-JEPA features at eighty-six point seven percent. The full model, answering the same items itself, gets thirty-three point seven. With three options, that's chance.

### Prior work

So what's the usual recipe? Open video models typically pair an image encoder that was aligned to text with a language model, then train on large caption and question-answering corpora.

When Meta's V-JEPA 2 and 2.1 were turned into video language models, they went through that same recipe: alignment, pre-training and instruction stages, on eighty-eight point five million and seventy-two point five million samples. And only the encoders were released, not the video language models.

The authors argue that scale doesn't fix this, and neither does a stronger encoder. They cite Meta's own controlled comparison: under the same recipe, V-JEPA 2.1 scores lower than V-JEPA 2 on TemporalBench and TOMATO. What's missing is supervision that can't be satisfied without looking at the change.

### Key idea

The idea comes in two steps. Step one: find out what motion information looks like inside the encoder.

Play a clip backwards. For an encoder like SigLIP, which works frame by frame and was aligned to text, that just reorders the same tokens. The direction of time isn't in the content of any single token.

V-JEPA behaves differently. Reverse, mirror or re-time a clip, and its tokens move along a change component that points the same way across different videos. By the authors' measure, that's twenty to one hundred times more consistent than in SigLIP.

Step two: design the supervision around that structure. Take a clip and a transformed copy, ask both the same question, and build them so the answers differ. Then the only thing that can tell them apart is the change.

Look at this figure. The same clip played forward, and the answer is opening the rice cooker. Played backward, the answer becomes closing it. Same frames, same question, and only the change decides. The authors call this counterfactual supervision.

### Method

Here's how the data gets made. A generator takes a clip from a public dataset, applies a pixel-level transformation, and writes the question, the options and the answer. The answer follows from the transformation, so the authors say no new manual annotation and no language model are involved.

There are fourteen constructions in six families. Sign flips like reversal and mirroring, where the answer inverts. The order of events. Whether an action sits at the beginning, middle or end. Speed. How many times something repeats. And whether an action completes, or a scene breaks the laws of physics.

Take speed. One clip is shown twice, one above the other, each copy covering a different span of the original, and the copy with the longer span moves faster. Swap the two spans and the answer flips.

The generator also balances things. Every scene appears with each answer equally often in expectation, and it balances the length rank of the correct option and how likely a text-only language model finds each option.

All of that adds up to five hundred and nine thousand counterfactual rows from thirty-three sources, about six percent of what's sampled in training.

The original caption and question-answering mixture stays as it is, and keeps teaching language and knowledge.

The model itself is simple. A V-JEPA 2.1 encoder, frozen throughout, reads thirty-two frames. A connector the authors call a spectral bridge turns each clip into one thousand and twenty-four tokens, and those go to the language model.

Training is a single stage, on roughly four and a half million public samples from one hundred and seventy-five public sources. The language models are Qwen3 and Llama, from 1B to 8B, six models in all.

### Results

The setup. The authors report twelve scores across eleven video benchmarks and take the plain average. Every open model was re-evaluated by the authors under one protocol, with thirty-two frames.

The main results are in Table 4. In the 4B class, V-JEPA-LM averages seventy-two point one four. The best open 4B model, Molmo2-4B, is at sixty-one point zero eight, so that's eleven point one points. It's also seven point four points above the best open 8B model, PLM-8B, at sixty-four point seven nine.

The 2B model averages sixty-six point zero two, thirteen point six points above the best open model in its class, Qwen3-VL-2B.

The widest margins are on the motion-heavy benchmarks. In the 4B class it scores fifty-three point five on TOMATO, where no other open model passes thirty-six point five, and sixty-six point eight on Vinoground's video score, where the others top out at thirty-three point eight.

On data, the authors count two to thirty-eight times fewer training samples than the open models that report their counts, and two to eight times fewer visual tokens. Training the 4B model took four hundred and ninety-nine H100-hours.

Then there's Meta's unreleased V-JEPA video language models. On the six benchmarks both sides report, the two 8B models lead on average by six point seven and four point nine points. But Meta's numbers are quoted from its papers, and the evaluation settings aren't identical.

The authors also built six held-out test sets, things like speed ratios that never appear in training, or a video corpus never used for that kind of question. V-JEPA-LM averages ninety-four point five to ninety-six point three, and open models forty-three point five to fifty-five point five. Replace the video with one repeated frame, and the scores fall to thirty-seven point nine and thirty-seven point six, which the authors take to show that single frames don't solve these sets.

### Comparison

So does the gain come from the encoder or from the supervision? The authors ran a two-by-two study. Four 2B models, with either V-JEPA or SigLIP as the encoder, with or without the counterfactual rows, on the same mixture and schedule.

The results are in Table 6. SigLIP without the rows scores fifty-eight point five two, and with them sixty-two point one three. V-JEPA without scores sixty-one point five three, and with them sixty-six point zero two. Both encoders benefit: four and a half points for V-JEPA, three point six for SigLIP.

But ability by ability, the picture is very different. Take speed questions at eight thousand training steps. Without the counterfactual rows, SigLIP is at thirty-four point zero and V-JEPA at thirty-two point four. With them, SigLIP reaches forty-five point four and V-JEPA sixty-five point three.

The authors sum it up this way: the encoder decides what can be read, and the supervision decides what is read. Direction, counting and fine actions come from the encoder. Speed, camera motion and state change need the supervision.

There's one more control. Swap the counterfactual rows, at the same weight, for a single question type that only asks whether the video is playing forward or in reverse, which is what an earlier paper did. The model learns that question, ninety-five point nine percent on unseen clips, but its final twelve-score average is sixty-one point one seven, no better than the sixty-one point five three from captions and question-answering alone.

The authors' conclusion is that the gain comes from the counterfactual construction, not from having more temporal questions.

Back to the measurement from the start. With the counterfactual rows, on those same speed items, the model's own answer goes from thirty-three point seven to ninety-two point eight. On the SigLIP side, with or without the rows, no stage gets above forty.

The authors also ran an intervention. At inference, they remove eight speed directions from every visual token. On a held-out set of unseen speed combinations, accuracy drops from ninety-eight point nine to sixty-five point seven. Remove a random subspace of the same energy instead, and it stays at ninety-seven point two.

My read is that the two-by-two study is the most solid thing in this paper. It separates the credit: swapping the encoder alone isn't enough, adding the data alone isn't enough, and you need the two together.

My read is that two things in the comparison deserve caution. The open models' scores were re-measured by the authors at thirty-two frames, and the paper's appendix shows that several of those models report their own results with more frames. And the six held-out sets reuse the question wording and options of the training generator. So a gap of ninety-something against forty or fifty shouldn't be read as the gap you'd see in the wild.

### Limitations

The authors list three limitations. First, short clips only. Every model reads thirty-two frames per clip, the benchmarks ask about clips of seconds to a few minutes, and hour-long video is left for later.

Second, no image question answering. Documents, charts and text recognition are out of scope, because V-JEPA's training never involves reading text. The authors also acknowledge that on knowledge-heavy video benchmarks like NExT-QA and PerceptionTest, open models trained on two to thirty-eight times more data keep an edge.

For example, on NExT-QA in the 4B class, V-JEPA-LM scores seventy-nine point three, and Molmo2-4B scores eighty-five point one.

Third, each model was trained once, on four and a half million public samples. The scale of general-purpose models, hundreds of millions of samples, is outside the scope of this study.

### Future work

For future work, the authors point to long video. They see the compact representation, one thousand and twenty-four tokens per clip, as a natural building block for hour-long understanding, and leave that for later.

On openness, the paper says the weights and inference code are released, and that the training code, the data generator and the data manifests will follow.

My read is that this paper is a useful warning for anyone building video understanding. When a model can't answer motion questions, the encoder may see the motion just fine; the training data may simply never have required the model to look. Probe what the encoder holds first, then decide what data to build. That approach is worth borrowing more than the scores are.

My read is that the boundaries matter too. It leads on the average and on motion questions, it still trails on knowledge-heavy benchmarks, and it only covers short clips. It's some distance from a general-purpose video model.
