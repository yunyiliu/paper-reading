## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇 Alaya Lab 和东京大学等机构的论文，叫 DirCast，它让一个模型，同时当电竞直播的导播和解说。

看比赛直播，屏幕切到哪个选手，是导播决定的，嘴上讲什么，是解说决定的。这篇论文想让一个模型，从原始画面出发，实时把两件事一起做了。

这张图就是它在一场 Dota 2 里的样子。上面是十个英雄各自的镜头，框出来的是它选上直播的那一路，下面是播出的画面和它说的话。

### 问题

一场 Dota 2 有十个英雄，每个英雄一路镜头。导播每秒都要决定播哪一路，解说对着播出的画面讲，两个人都不能等未来，团战在地图另一边打起来，导播只有几秒钟切过去。

论文说，所以职业比赛要专业转播团队，而大量地区联赛和业余比赛，只能一个固定镜头，没有解说。

难点在于两件事绑在一起。十路画面每秒一帧，视觉 token 太多，流式模型很难长时间留在上下文里。导播错过一场团战，解说看到的就是错的画面，后面每一秒都受影响。

训练上还有两个坑。人类导播大部分时间不切镜头，模型照着学，容易学成永远不切。而允许沉默的流式模型，一旦安静下来，就很难再开口。

### 以前的方法

以前的工作各做了一半。一半是流式解说模型，比如 LiveCC、StreamingVLM 和 Proact-VL，一帧一帧看视频，自己决定什么时候说、说什么。

问题是，它们的输入都是人类导播剪好的画面。看什么，这个最难的判断，人已经替它做了。只给原始的十路画面，这些模型就用不上。

另一半是体育转播里的自动导播和视角选择，但大多是离线挑镜头，不是逐秒做决定，也不和解说配合。

### 核心想法

作者的出发点是，导播和解说不是两个碰巧同时运行的系统。导播决定观众看到什么，解说讲观众看到的东西，两个人都依赖之前播了什么、说了什么。

所以他们用一个因果的视频语言模型扮演两个角色，解说在导播决定之后生成，并以它为条件。这样解说讲的，永远是导播选中的画面。

### 方法

这张图是整体框架。输入是一条每秒增长一段的 token 流。每一段先是比赛时钟和这一秒的事件，然后是十路低分辨率画面，每一路前面带一个状态 token，记录英雄的血量、附近敌人和最近受到的伤害。

接着是导播 token，模型在这里做决定。选中的那一路，再以高分辨率加进来，后面是解说 token 和要说的话。

为什么分两种分辨率。十路全用高清，token 太多，大多数画面也没什么可讲。低分辨率足够看出哪里在打，解说要认出英雄、物品和技能，才需要高清。实现上，十路是 128 像素，播出的那一路是 288 像素。

第一个设计叫 hold-or-switch，保持还是切换。好的导播大部分时间什么都不做，镜头一保持就是好几秒。直接在十路里做分类，模型每秒都得重新学一遍当前镜头通常是对的，分数一抖，画面就来回乱切。

所以决定拆成两步。一个切换门判断要不要离开当前镜头，一个指针在其他九路里挑切到哪一路，按画面内容和英雄状态打分，而不是按输入里的位置。保持是默认，门打开才切。

第二个设计，播出的节目就是记忆。人类转播团队不会记得一分钟前每一路拍了什么，记得的是播出去的画面和说过的话。DirCast 也一样，低分辨率画面只留几秒，实验里是 8 秒，然后移出上下文，播出画面、事件和解说词留下。

推理时，过期内容直接从 KV 缓存里删掉，比赛再长，超过一个训练片段长度的旧内容也会移出，所以每秒成本恒定，一块 GPU 就能实时跑。

第三，接入游戏数据。光看画面，快开打的团战就像两群英雄在走路，地图另一头的击杀，只是十个缩略图里的一点变化。所以每次击杀事件，会在击杀者那一路镜头上挂一个标记。

训练用人类直播做监督，三个目标一起学。语言模型损失学解说内容，解说 token 上的二分类学什么时候开口，导播损失学切换门和指针。

直接模仿有两个缺口。第一个，训练时模型看到的永远是人选的画面，上线后看到的是自己选的，切错一次，就到了没见过的局面。所以训练时故意让播出镜头偏离人类选择几秒，目标仍是人类的选择，导播学会切回来，解说学会对着任何画面说话。

第二个缺口在标签。人类导播只是在几个好选择里挑了一个。所以在击杀前后几秒，击杀者和被击杀者的镜头都算对，这两路都没在播时，还要求切换门打开。

最后是推理的节奏。模型沉默几秒后，会以自己的沉默为条件，越来越不想说话，导播也一样。所以切换和开口的阈值，跟着模型最近的行为调整，向人类团队的比例靠拢，比人类安静了，开口门槛就降低，切得太多了，切换门槛就升高。

### 实验和效果

数据集是作者自己建的。27 场 2026 年的职业 Dota 2 比赛，用比赛录像在游戏客户端里给十个英雄各渲染一路镜头，再和官方直播对齐，逐秒标出人类导播播了哪一路、解说说了什么。

按整场比赛划分，20 场训练，3 场校准，4 场测试，测试集的队伍和选手训练里都没出现过。人类导播平均每分钟切 7.9 次。

模型基于 Qwen3-VL-8B，用 LoRA 微调，推理只用一块 GPU。

先看导播，表 1。和人类导播选同一路的比例叫 Top-1，DirCast 是 27.0，最强的基线是一个动作规则，25.4，从不切镜头的静态导播是 17.1。

零样本大模型表现很差。八个模型没有一个追上动作规则，GPT-5 只有 13.5，比从不切的静态导播还低。它们大多每分钟切 20 到 56 次，人类是 7.9 次，DirCast 是 6.7 次。

DirCast 播出了 89.1% 的击杀，高于所有基线，也高于人类导播的 50.3%。论文的解释是，人类导播经常停在一个镜头上，击杀却发生在别处。

再看解说，表 2，用的是 Proact-VL 的评测协议。核心指标 CC，是和 GPT-4o 的解说比，由裁判模型判胜率。DirCast 胜率 29.21%，最强的基线 LiveCC-7B-Base 是 19.38%，高了约十个点。

注意，十一个对比模型看的都是人类导播剪好的画面，DirCast 是对着自己导播的画面在说。它开口的时间，平均只比人类解说晚 0.30 秒。

但 F1 这一项，DirCast 是 78.22，低于 Proact-VL 的 83.21。论文解释，这个指标奖励一直说话，每秒都说就能拿到 84.8，而 Proact-VL 在 99.9% 的时间里都在说。

### 和以前方法的对比

一个模型还是两个，看表 3。和两个分别只学一个角色的模型比，联合模型两边都更好，Top-1 从 25.17 到 27.00，解说胜率从 24.36 到 29.21，共享权重，显存从 37.8 GB 降到 18.9 GB。

解说变好，是不是只因为画面选得好。作者让两个解说都对着人类导播的画面说，联合模型还是赢，27.84 对 25.67。作者的结论是，学会导播，本身让模型成了更好的解说。

消融在表 4。去掉 hold-or-switch 指针影响最大，Top-1 从 27.00 掉到 21.65。去掉状态 token，掉到 22.59，去掉训练时的节目扰动，掉到 22.90。

长直播测试在表 5。连续跑 288 秒，所有画面都保留，缓存涨到 9.26 万个 token，最慢的时段跟不上实时。只保留最近 36 秒，缓存稳定在 6900 个 token，每秒视频算 0.29 秒。

论文还挑了几个例子。这四次击杀，人类直播都停在一个无关的英雄身上，DirCast 在击杀那一秒切了过去，并在大约两秒内说出参与战斗的英雄。

我的判断是，这篇最有意思的，是把看什么也变成了模型要学的东西。以前的流式视频模型默认画面已经选好，而真实的多机位直播里，挑出值得看的那一路，恰恰是最难的部分。

### 局限

论文没有单独写局限，下面几条来自它自己的实验设置。

第一，评测范围窄。只有 Dota 2 一个游戏，测试 4 场，每个测试片段 36 秒。

第二，和人类导播的一致性还不高。Top-1 只比简单的动作规则高不到 2 个点。

第三，解说靠大模型当裁判。协议官方的裁判是 GPT-5.1，作者拿不到，所有模型都改用 GPT-5 来评。

第四，方法用到了游戏本身的数据。英雄状态和击杀事件都从比赛录像里取，十路镜头也是在游戏客户端里重新渲染的。

### 未来方向

作者称，训练数据的曲线还没饱和，新比赛只需要各路镜头和播出的直播，不需要人工标注，所以扩展到几百场比赛，是自然的下一步。

我的判断是，最值得看的，是它能不能走出 Dota 2。它靠游戏录像，能从任意视角重新渲染，还能直接拿到血量和击杀。真实的体育转播没有这样的录像，多机位素材和状态数据都要另想办法。

我的判断是，离替代转播团队还有距离。解说对 GPT-4o 的胜率不到三分之一，导播也只比规则略好。它证明的是一个模型同时导播和解说可行，还不是能直接上线。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from Alaya Lab, the University of Tokyo and collaborators. It's called DirCast, and it asks one model to be both the director and the caster of a live esports broadcast.

In a match broadcast, the director decides whose camera is on screen, and the caster talks over it. This paper wants a single model to do both, from raw footage, in real time.

Here's what that looks like in Dota 2. On top are the ten hero cameras, with the one it put on air framed. Below is the broadcast it produced, and the words it spoke.

### Problem

A Dota 2 match has ten heroes, one camera each. Every second the director picks a feed, and the caster talks over whatever is on air. Neither can wait for the future: when a fight breaks out across the map, the director has a few seconds to get there.

As the paper notes, that's why pro matches need trained crews, while the long tail of regional and amateur games gets one fixed camera and no commentary.

The hard part is that the two jobs are coupled. Ten feeds at one frame per second is more visual tokens than a streaming model can keep in context for long. And if the director misses a fight, the caster is looking at the wrong scene, and every later second inherits the mistake.

Training has two traps too. Human directors mostly hold their shot, so a model that imitates them learns never to cut. And a streaming model allowed to stay silent tends to go quiet and never really recover.

### Prior work

Earlier work solved half the problem each. One half is streaming commentary: models like LiveCC, StreamingVLM and Proact-VL read video frame by frame and decide when to speak and what to say.

The catch is that their input is a finished broadcast, where a human already picked every shot. The hardest call, what to show, was made for them. Give them ten raw feeds and they can't be used.

The other half is automatic directing and view selection in sports, but that work mostly picks views offline, not second by second, and without commentary.

### Key idea

The authors' starting point: a director and a caster aren't two systems that happen to run side by side. The director decides what the audience sees, the caster talks about what the audience sees, and both depend on what has been shown and said.

So one causal video language model plays both roles, with the commentary generated after the directing decision and conditioned on it. The caster always speaks over the shot the director chose.

### Method

Here's the framework. The input is one token stream that grows by a chunk every second. Each chunk starts with the game clock and that second's events, then the ten cameras at low resolution, each preceded by a state token with the hero's health, nearby enemies and recent damage.

Then comes a director token, where the model makes its call. The chosen camera is added again at high resolution, followed by a commentary token and that second's words.

Why two resolutions? Ten full-resolution feeds would be far too many tokens, and most show nothing worth describing. Low resolution is enough to see where the action is, while the caster needs a sharp picture to name heroes, items and abilities. In practice, the ten feeds are read at one hundred twenty-eight pixels and the on-air feed at two hundred eighty-eight.

The first design is called hold-or-switch. A good director mostly does nothing, holding a shot for several seconds. A plain classifier over ten cameras has to relearn every second that the current shot is usually right, and small wobbles in its scores become flickering cuts.

So the decision is split in two. A cut gate decides whether to leave the current shot, and a pointer picks which of the other nine to cut to, scoring cameras by what they show and their hero's state, not their position in the input. Holding is the default; a cut needs the gate to open.

The second design: the program is the memory. A human crew doesn't remember what every camera showed a minute ago, only what went on air and what was said. DirCast does the same, keeping low-resolution views for a few seconds, eight in the experiments, while on-air views, events and words stay.

At inference, expired entries are dropped from the key-value cache, and on long streams anything older than a training clip leaves too, so the cost per second is constant and it runs in real time on one GPU.

Third, the model is grounded in the game. From video alone, a fight about to start looks like two groups of heroes walking, and a kill across the map is a tiny change in one of ten thumbnails. So each kill event raises a flag on the camera of the hero who made it.

Training uses human broadcasts with three objectives. A language modeling loss teaches the commentary, a binary loss on the commentary token teaches when to speak, and a directing loss teaches the gate and the pointer.

Plain imitation leaves two gaps. In training, the model always sees the human's program, but live it sees its own, and one wrong cut lands it somewhere new. So during training the on-air camera drifts away from the human choice for a few seconds while the targets stay human, and the director learns to cut back while the caster learns to talk over any shot.

The second gap is in the labels. A human director picks one good option out of several. So around a kill, the killer's and the victim's cameras both count as correct, and if neither is on air, the gate is asked to open.

Finally, rhythm. A model that has been silent for a few seconds conditions on its own silence and grows quieter still, and the director drifts the same way. So the thresholds for cutting and speaking track the model's recent behavior, pulled toward human crew rates: too quiet, and the bar to speak drops; too many cuts, and the bar to cut rises.

### Results

The authors built the dataset themselves: twenty-seven professional Dota 2 matches from twenty twenty-six, one camera per hero rendered from the replay files in the game's own client, aligned with the official broadcast, and labeled every second with the feed the human director aired and what the casters said.

The split is by match: twenty for training, three for calibration, four for testing, with no test team or player seen in training. The test set has four hundred ninety-six clips, about five hours. Human directors cut seven point nine times a minute.

The model is Qwen3-VL-8B fine-tuned with LoRA, and inference runs on one GPU.

Directing first, in Table 1. Top-1 is how often the model airs the same feed as the human director. DirCast scores twenty-seven point zero, the strongest baseline, an action-based rule, twenty-five point four, and a static director that never cuts seventeen point one.

Zero-shot large models do badly. None of the eight reaches the action rule, and GPT-5 gets thirteen point five, below the director that never cuts. Most cut twenty to fifty-six times a minute, against seven point nine for humans and six point seven for DirCast.

DirCast puts eighty-nine point one percent of kills on air, more than every baseline and more than the human director at fifty point three. The paper's explanation: human directors often hold a shot while a kill happens elsewhere.

Now commentary, in Table 2, under the Proact-VL protocol. The main metric, CC, pits each model's commentary against GPT-4o's and has a judge model pick the winner. DirCast wins twenty-nine point two one percent, against nineteen point three eight for the strongest baseline, LiveCC-7B-Base, about ten points higher.

Note that all eleven comparison models watch the human-directed broadcast, while DirCast talks over its own program. On average, it starts speaking just zero point three seconds after the human casters.

On F1, though, DirCast's seventy-eight point two two trails Proact-VL's eighty-three point two one. The paper explains that F1 rewards talking nonstop: speaking every second already scores eighty-four point eight, and Proact-VL talks ninety-nine point nine percent of the time.

### Comparison

One model or two? Table 3. Against two single-role models, the joint model is better at both: Top-1 rises from twenty-five point one seven to twenty-seven, the commentary win rate from twenty-four point three six to twenty-nine point two one, and shared weights halve memory from thirty-seven point eight to eighteen point nine gigabytes.

Is the commentary better only because the shots are? The authors had both casters talk over the human program, and the joint model still wins, twenty-seven point eight four to twenty-five point six seven. Their conclusion: learning to direct makes the model a better caster.

Ablations are in Table 4. Removing the hold-or-switch pointer hurts most, dropping Top-1 from twenty-seven to twenty-one point six five. Without state tokens it falls to twenty-two point five nine, and without the perturbed program rollouts to twenty-two point nine.

Long streams are in Table 5. Over two hundred eighty-eight seconds, keeping every view grows the cache to ninety-two thousand six hundred tokens, and the slowest stretches fall behind real time. Keeping only the last thirty-six seconds holds the cache at sixty-nine hundred tokens, at zero point two nine seconds of compute per video second.

The paper also shows examples. In these four kills, the human broadcast sits on an uninvolved hero, while DirCast cuts to the fight in the second of the kill and names a hero involved within about two seconds.

My read is that the most interesting move here is making what to watch part of what the model learns. Earlier streaming video models assume the shot is already picked, but in a real multi-camera broadcast, picking the feed worth watching is exactly the hard part.

### Limitations

The paper has no limitations section, so these come from its own setup.

First, the evaluation is narrow: one game, four test matches, and test clips of thirty-six seconds.

Second, agreement with human directors is modest. Top-1 is less than two points above a simple action rule.

Third, commentary is scored by a model judge. The protocol's official judge is GPT-5.1, which the authors couldn't access, so every model was judged by GPT-5 instead.

Fourth, the method uses the game's own data. Hero states and kill events come from replay files, and the ten feeds are re-rendered in the game client.

### Future work

The authors say the data-scaling curves haven't saturated, and that new matches need only camera feeds and the aired broadcast, no manual labels, so scaling to hundreds of matches is the natural next step.

My read is that the real question is whether this leaves Dota 2. It relies on replays that can re-render any viewpoint and hand over health and kill events. Real sports broadcasts have nothing like that, so both the multi-camera footage and the state data would have to come from elsewhere.

My read is also that it's still far from replacing a broadcast crew. Its win rate against GPT-4o is under a third, and the director is only slightly better than a rule. It shows that one model can direct and commentate at once, not that it's ready to go on air.
