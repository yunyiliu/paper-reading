## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇 Adobe 和韩国 KAIST 的论文，名字叫 MoviEdit，副标题是，让长篇多镜头视频编辑变得简单。

一段十几秒、四五个镜头的视频，你说一句话，比如把这个男人的灰色开衫换成芥末黄，模型要在每一个镜头里，都把同一个人改对，而且改得一模一样。

### 问题

难在哪。多镜头视频，每切一次镜头，场景、视角、人物的样子都会变。编辑要跨过这些剪辑点，一直保持一致。

最直接的两种做法都不行。每个镜头单独编辑，镜头之间的联系就丢了，同一件衣服，每个镜头改出来可能都不一样。把整段原视频和目标视频放在一起处理，计算量又扛不住，因为全局注意力的成本随长度平方增长。

论文把问题拆成三个瓶颈，数据、模型和评测。数据上，需要镜头之间人物对得上的成对视频，作者说现实中没有这种数据。评测上，现有的 benchmark 主要是短的单镜头片段，测不出切镜头之后有没有改对人。

### 以前的方法

早期用扩散模型做视频编辑，大多是在推理时操控一个预训练好的生成模型，比如反演、改注意力，不需要成对数据，但作者认为，这类方法不够稳健，推理开销也大。

后来转向数据驱动，用成对的视频直接训练编辑模型。但这些端到端的模型，大多还是只做几秒钟的单镜头视频。

长视频生成那边，常见的办法是分块自回归，只保留最近一段上下文，或者用稀疏注意力，只让镜头之间交换少量信息。作者指出，这些办法省了算力，但限制了全局交互，长时间保持外观一致就很难。

### 核心想法

MoviEdit 的思路是三件事一起做，自动造数据，改模型结构，再做一个专门的 benchmark。

模型上最关键的想法是，先打草稿，再精修。先在压缩过的低分辨率视频上，整段一起编辑，得到一个全局一致的草稿，再拿这个草稿当锚点，一块一块地生成全分辨率的视频。

### 方法

先说数据。原始素材有三种，自己拍的多机位同步视频，同一场戏用两到四台相机同时拍，真实的影视片段，还有多镜头文生视频模型生成的视频。

主力是像素对齐的编辑数据。先让一个视觉语言模型找出视频里的人物，比如穿灰色开衫的男人。然后把每个镜头的第一帧拼成一张大图，用现成的图像编辑模型一次改完，这样所有镜头改出来是一致的。

接着，用一个单镜头视频编辑模型，把每张改好的第一帧，在各自的镜头里往后传播。两个阶段都有质检，影视素材在两个阶段分别淘汰了百分之三十二和百分之四十七。

第二种是主体定位数据。用视频分割模型，在所有镜头里追踪同一个人，再给他加高亮、描边或者填色，教模型认准要改的是谁。

第三种是半像素对齐的数据，用来做换环境、换媒介风格、改人物外形这种变化更大的编辑。做法是用多镜头生成模型，同时生成原视频和目标视频，共用每个镜头的描述和初始噪声，只有整体描述不同。

改人物外形的时候，两边前面一段去噪过程是共享的，用两个条件预测的平均值往下走，到某个时间点再分叉，各走各的。这一路的质检淘汰了百分之六十六的样本。

汇总下来，过滤前 205228 对，过滤后留下 86619 对，也就是摘要里的 8 万 7 千对。

再说模型。常见的长视频做法，是分块的因果扩散，每一块只看前面最近的几块。问题是，编辑的时间一长，外观会慢慢漂移，颜色也越来越饱和。

论文这张压力测试图，是在小规模训练设置下做的。同样是去掉一个人，朴素的自回归结果越往后越走样，有草稿约束的版本则保持稳定。

所以 MoviEdit 分两步。第一步叫 Draft Editor，草稿编辑器。把视频在空间和时间上降采样，长度只有原来的一半，然后整段一起做双向的扩散，得到一个全局一致的编辑草稿。

这个草稿有两个作用。一是给后面的生成提供稳定的外观锚点，压住漂移。二是因为整段一起改，隔得很远的两个镜头，也能保持一致。

第二步叫 Render Editor，渲染编辑器。在全分辨率上一块一块生成，每一块同时参考三样东西，刚生成的前几块，保证连贯，全局草稿，保证一致，还有对应的原视频片段，保证不该改的地方不变。

有意思的是，这两个编辑器用的是同一套权重，区别只在注意力的方式和分辨率。

为了跑得快，作者又做了蒸馏和 KV cache。蒸馏之后，草稿编辑器只用 12 步去噪，计算更重的渲染编辑器压到 4 步。KV cache 则把能复用的 token 缓存起来，不用重复计算。

### 实验和效果

训练设置。模型是 DiT 结构的混合专家模型，从一个只会单镜头编辑、没有多镜头能力的检查点开始，在 64 张 H200 上全参数微调，先训 17000 步，再为参考帧指导的编辑多训 8000 步。

评测用的是他们自己的 MoviEdit-Bench，64 段生成的多镜头视频，128 个编辑场景，分成八类，从单人到多人群戏，再到人和动物同框，认准目标的难度各不相同。

打分有七个维度，比如改没改对人、跨镜头是否一致、画质怎么样，很多判断交给一个视觉语言模型来做。最严格的一项叫整体编辑成功率，抽样的每一帧，要同时满足所有适用的标准，才算成功。

结果先看表 1，文本指导的编辑。整体编辑成功率，MoviEdit 是 0.790，最强的对手，Runway 的 Aleph 2.0 是 0.764，阿里云的 HappyHorse 是 0.623，Google DeepMind 的 Gemini Omni Flash 是 0.586。

开源模型差距更大，表里最好的 Wan-Edit 只有 0.378。

表 2 是另一种任务，给一张改好的参考帧，让模型把编辑传播到整段视频。如果参考帧是第一帧，Aleph 2.0 是 0.806，MoviEdit 是 0.803，基本打平。如果参考帧可以是任意一帧，也就是完整的 benchmark，MoviEdit 是 0.786，Aleph 2.0 是 0.781。

速度看表 4，540p、361 帧，在 8 张 H100 上。完整的时间注意力要 364.1 秒，加上滑动窗口、KV cache 和蒸馏之后，只要 11.8 秒，快了 30.9 倍，比 15 秒的视频本身还短。

扩展实验显示，训练步数和数据量越多，几项主要指标越高，作者认为继续扩大规模还有提升空间。

数据配方的消融，是小规模训练下的定性对比。加入半像素对齐数据后，结构和形状变化大的编辑做得更好。加入主体定位数据后，模型更能认准要改的对象。

还有一个泛化结果。模型只在 15 秒以内的视频上训练，但能处理 25 秒、最多 17 个镜头的视频。

### 和以前方法的对比

先看这张定性对比图，同一条指令，几个模型在多个镜头上的结果放在一起。作者说，开源基线常见的问题是，上一个镜头的瑕疵带到下一个镜头，切镜头后改错了位置，或者干脆没改成。

HappyHorse 和 Gemini Omni 一般能完成编辑，但会偏离原视频，镜头切换和节奏保不住，还会凭空多出动作。Aleph 2.0 是最强的基线，作者说，MoviEdit 在保留原视频、改对位置和贴合指令上更好。

但也要看表 1 里 MoviEdit 不领先的地方。跨镜头一致性，Aleph 2.0 是 0.981，MoviEdit 是 0.904。视觉语言模型给的画质分，MoviEdit 是 0.762，低于 Gemini Omni Flash 的 0.814，和 Aleph 2.0 的 0.801。

在外部的单镜头 benchmark 上，作者说 MoviEdit 和商用模型相当或接近。但表 9 里，FiVE-Bench 的视觉语言模型准确率，MoviEdit 是 0.6623，低于 Gemini Omni Flash、HappyHorse 和 Aleph 2.0，它们都在 0.77 以上。

我的判断是，这篇的价值不在某一个分数，而在整套结构。低分辨率整段打草稿，全分辨率分块渲染，同一套权重，既顾到全局一致，又控制住长视频的算力，这个思路对其他长视频任务也有参考价值。

我的判断是，也要看到几点保留。benchmark 是作者自己建的，打分大量依赖视觉语言模型，文本编辑上领先最强商用系统只有零点零几。训练还用了自家素材和专有数据，论文说 benchmark 打算公开，没有提模型权重，外部很难复现。

### 局限

作者自己展示的失败情况主要有两种，都和认准目标有关。

第一种，画面里有几个长得很像的人，编辑可能加到了错的人身上。第二种，目标只露出一部分，比如只看到身体的一部分，跨镜头就很难对上，编辑会不完整，甚至改错。

另外，MoviEdit 只做保留原有动作和叙事结构的编辑。要改动作、改剧情走向这种更自由的编辑，论文把它留给了以后。

### 未来方向

作者提了三个方向。一是用 Agent 式的编辑流程，只处理真正需要改的镜头或者人物，提高保真度和效率。二是用可控的渲染引擎，比如 Unreal Engine，来造更多训练数据。三是扩展到能改动作和叙事结构的自由编辑。

我的判断是，生成模型已经开始能做多镜头的长视频，下一步自然是要能改。MoviEdit 把数据、模型、评测一起补上了，如果之后模型也能开放出来，这个方向会走得快很多。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Adobe and KAIST in Korea. It's called MoviEdit, and the subtitle is long-form multi-shot video editing made easy.

The task is easy to picture. You have a fifteen-second clip with several shots, and you type one instruction, say, change the man's gray cardigan to mustard. The model has to find that same man in every shot and change him the same way every time.

### Problem

Here's why that's hard. Every cut changes the scene, the camera angle, and how the person looks. The edit has to survive all of those cuts.

The two obvious approaches both break. Edit each shot on its own, and you lose the links between shots, so the same jacket can come out differently every time. Process the whole source and target videos together, and compute blows up, because global attention cost grows with the square of the length.

The paper frames this as three bottlenecks: data, modeling, and evaluation. Training needs paired videos where the subject lines up across shots, and the authors say that data doesn't exist in the real world. And existing benchmarks mostly use short single-shot clips, so they can't tell whether a model edits the right person after a cut.

### Prior work

Early diffusion-based video editing mostly manipulated a pretrained generator at inference time, with tricks like inversion or attention editing. No paired data needed, but in the authors' view it's less robust and adds a lot of inference overhead.

Then the field moved to training editors directly on paired videos. But those end-to-end models still mostly handle a few seconds of single-shot video.

On the long-video generation side, the usual tools are chunk-by-chunk generation with a short context window, or sparse attention that lets shots exchange only a little information. Both save compute by limiting global interaction, the authors note, which makes consistent appearance over long spans hard.

### Key idea

MoviEdit's answer is to fix all three at once: generate the data automatically, change the architecture, and build a dedicated benchmark.

The central modeling idea is draft first, then refine. Edit the whole video together at low resolution to get a globally consistent draft, then use that draft as an anchor while generating the full-resolution video chunk by chunk.

### Method

Start with the data. There are three raw sources: in-house multi-camera footage, with two to four cameras filming the same scene at once; real film clips; and videos from a multi-shot text-to-video model.

The main data type is pixel-aligned editing. A vision-language model first names the subjects in the clip, like the man in the gray cardigan. Then the first frame of every shot goes into one image grid, and an off-the-shelf image editor edits the whole grid in a single pass, so every shot gets the same edit.

Next, a single-shot video editor propagates each edited first frame through its own shot. Both stages have quality checks, and for film clips they reject thirty-two and then forty-seven percent.

The second type is subject localization. Segmentation models track one person across all shots, and the person gets highlighted, outlined, or filled with color. That teaches the model exactly who is supposed to change.

The third type is semi-pixel-aligned data, for bigger edits like changing the environment, the visual medium, or a character's appearance. A multi-shot generator produces source and target together, sharing the per-shot captions and starting noise, with only the overall caption different.

For character changes, the two generations share the first part of denoising, following the average of both predictions, then fork and finish separately. Quality checks on this pipeline reject sixty-six percent of pairs.

In total, about two hundred and five thousand raw pairs become eighty-six thousand six hundred and nineteen after filtering, the eighty-seven thousand in the abstract.

Now the model. A common design for long video is chunk-wise causal diffusion, where each chunk only sees the few before it. The catch is that over a long edit, appearance slowly drifts and colors oversaturate.

This stress test, run in a small-scale training setting, shows it. Removing the same person, the naive autoregressive output degrades over time, while the draft-anchored version stays stable.

So MoviEdit works in two steps. Step one is the Draft Editor. The video is downsampled in space and time to half the length, then edited as one sequence with bidirectional diffusion, where every frame sees every other frame, giving a globally consistent draft.

That draft does two jobs. It gives later generation a stable appearance anchor against drift. And because the whole video is edited at once, even distant shots stay consistent.

Step two is the Render Editor. It generates full resolution chunk by chunk, and each chunk looks at three things: the chunks just generated, for continuity; the global draft, for consistency; and the matching source chunk, so what shouldn't change doesn't.

The neat part is that both editors share the same weights. They differ only in attention pattern and resolution.

For speed, the authors add distillation and a KV cache. After distillation, the Draft Editor uses twelve denoising steps, and the heavier Render Editor just four. The KV cache stores reusable tokens so they aren't recomputed.

### Results

The training setup. The model is a DiT-based mixture-of-experts, starting from a single-shot editing checkpoint with no multi-shot ability, fully fine-tuned on sixty-four H200 GPUs: seventeen thousand steps, then eight thousand more for reference-frame-guided editing.

Evaluation uses the authors' own MoviEdit-Bench: sixty-four generated multi-shot videos and one hundred twenty-eight editing scenarios in eight categories, from a single person, to groups of four or more, to humans with animals, with varying difficulty in finding the right target.

Scoring covers seven dimensions, such as editing the right subject, cross-shot consistency, and video quality, with a vision-language model making many of the calls. The strictest metric is overall edit success: a sampled frame only counts if it passes every applicable check.

First, Table 1, text-guided editing. On overall edit success, MoviEdit scores zero point seven nine zero. The strongest rival, Runway's Aleph 2.0, scores zero point seven six four, Alibaba Cloud's HappyHorse zero point six two three, and Google DeepMind's Gemini Omni Flash zero point five eight six.

Open-source models trail further. The best open baseline, Wan-Edit, gets zero point three seven eight.

Table 2 is the other task: given one edited reference frame, propagate the edit through the whole video. With the first frame as reference, Aleph 2.0 scores zero point eight zero six and MoviEdit zero point eight zero three, essentially a tie. When the reference can be any frame, the full benchmark, MoviEdit scores zero point seven eight six and Aleph 2.0 zero point seven eight one.

Speed is in Table 4: five forty p, three hundred sixty-one frames, eight H100s. Full temporal attention takes three hundred sixty-four seconds. With sliding windows, the KV cache and distillation, it's eleven point eight seconds, thirty point nine times faster, and shorter than the fifteen-second video itself.

A scaling study shows the main metrics rising with more training steps and more data, which the authors read as room for further gains.

The data-recipe ablation is a qualitative, small-scale comparison. Semi-pixel-aligned data helps with edits that change structure or shape. Subject-localization data helps the model find the right target.

One more generalization result. The model only trained on clips up to fifteen seconds, yet it handles twenty-five-second videos with up to seventeen shots.

### Comparison

Here is the qualitative comparison figure, the same instruction across several models and shots. According to the authors, open baselines tend to carry artifacts from one shot into the next, edit the wrong place after a cut, or fail to edit at all.

HappyHorse and Gemini Omni usually make the edit, but drift from the source, losing shot transitions and timing and adding motion that isn't there. Aleph 2.0 is the strongest baseline, and the authors say MoviEdit preserves the source, localizes the edit, and follows the prompt better.

But look at where MoviEdit doesn't lead in Table 1. On cross-shot consistency, Aleph 2.0 scores zero point nine eight one against MoviEdit's zero point nine zero four. On the vision-language quality score, MoviEdit's zero point seven six two is below Gemini Omni Flash at zero point eight one four and Aleph 2.0 at zero point eight zero one.

On outside single-shot benchmarks, the authors call MoviEdit comparable to or competitive with commercial models. But in Table 9, on FiVE-Bench's vision-language accuracy, MoviEdit scores zero point six six two three, below Gemini Omni Flash, HappyHorse and Aleph 2.0, which all top zero point seven seven.

My read is that the value isn't any single score, it's the structure. Draft the whole video at low resolution, render in chunks at full resolution, with one set of weights. That buys global consistency while keeping long-video compute in check, and the idea could carry over to other long-video tasks.

My read is also that there are caveats. The benchmark is the authors' own, much of the scoring leans on a vision-language model, and on text-guided editing the lead over the best commercial system is a few hundredths. Training used in-house footage and proprietary data, and the paper says the benchmark is meant for release but says nothing about model weights, so outside reproduction will be hard.

### Limitations

The failure cases the authors show are two kinds, both about finding the right target.

First, with several similar-looking people in frame, the edit can land on the wrong one. Second, when the target is only partly visible, say part of a body, matching it across shots gets hard, and the edit can be incomplete or wrong.

Also, MoviEdit only does edits that keep the original motion and story structure. Freer edits that change the action or the narrative are left for future work.

### Future work

The authors name three directions. Agent-style workflows that only process the shots or subjects that need changing, for better fidelity and efficiency. Controllable rendering engines like Unreal Engine as another source of training data. And free-form edits that change motion and narrative structure.

My read is that generation models are starting to produce multi-shot long video, and the next step is being able to edit it. MoviEdit fills in data, model and evaluation together, and if the model itself is ever opened up, this direction could move a lot faster.
