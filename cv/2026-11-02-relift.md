## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇以色列理工学院和英伟达的论文，名字叫 ReLift，副标题是，把稠密 3D 跟踪看成对抬升轨迹的修复。

它要做的事，是只用一段普通的单目视频，算出第一帧里的每一个像素点，在三维空间里随时间怎么移动。

### 问题

先说这件事有什么用。论文提到，稠密的 3D 点轨迹，可以用在机器人操作策略的学习、动态场景重建、传感器仿真，还有用运动轨迹来控制视频生成这些任务上。

难点在于，从单目视频算 3D 轨迹，要同时做好三件事。一是在不同帧之间认出同一个点，这叫对应关系。二是估计这个点离相机多远，也就是深度。三是点被挡住的时候，还要推断它跑到了哪里。

而能直接监督这种轨迹的 4D 标注数据，也就是三维空间加上时间的真值，非常少。

### 以前的方法

以前的主流做法，是训练一个端到端的前馈网络，输入视频，直接输出 3D 轨迹，把这三件事放在一起学。

这张图里能看到不少这样的方法，比如 4RC、V-DPM、TrackCraft3R，横轴是速度，纵轴是精度。

作者的出发点是，这三件事里有两件，已经有现成的强模型。专门的 2D 跟踪器很会找对应关系，几何模型能给出深度和相机位置，而且几何模型的训练，不需要标注好的动态 4D 轨迹。

作者也验证了这一点，原生的 2D 跟踪器 CoWTracker，在图像平面上的精度，超过了所有被测 3D 方法投影回图像之后的结果。

### 核心想法

所以核心想法很简单。对应关系交给 2D 跟踪器，深度交给几何模型，两者一组合，先得到一条粗的 3D 轨迹，作者叫它 lift，也就是抬升出来的轨迹。稀缺的 4D 监督，只花在剩下那件事上，就是修复错误，补全被挡住的运动。

这里有个很容易被忽略的发现。精度指的是预测点离真实位置足够近的比例。在同样的几何输入下，CoWTracker 加 VGGT-Ω 的抬升轨迹，被挡住的部分只做线性插值，还没经过任何学习修正，全部点的平均精度就有 84.9。用同一套几何的 TrackCraft3R 是 83.9，DELTAv2 是 81.1。

也就是说，一个简单的拼接基线，在同样的几何输入下，已经能追平甚至超过专门训练的 3D 跟踪器。

### 方法

具体流程看这张图。视频先交给冻结的预训练模型，2D 跟踪器给出每个点在图像上的位置和可见性，几何模型给出深度和相机。组合之后，得到带错误、也有缺口的 3D 轨迹，再交给一个修正器，输出每一帧的三维修正量，加回原来的轨迹上。

抬升本身很直接。2D 跟踪器决定这个点在哪条视线上，深度模型决定沿着这条视线走多远，从相机出发走这么远，就是这个点的 3D 位置。

作者把抬升轨迹的错误分成四类。2D 跟踪的对应错误，会让视线方向偏掉。深度的抖动和漂移，会让点沿着视线前后乱跑。深度取到了别的表面上，比如在物体边缘，或者跟踪点其实停在了遮挡物上，这叫错表面。最后是点被挡住或者跟丢了，观测直接缺失。

被挡住的点，取到的其实是遮挡物的深度，所以作者把跟踪器判为不可见的观测直接丢掉。缺口先用线性插值填上，最后一次观测之后，就让它保持在原地。

修正器的输入只有几何信息。每个点的每一帧，记录它相对起点的位移、这一帧有没有可靠观测、离最近的观测隔了几帧、视线方向和相机位置。为了省计算，每 4 帧打包成一个 token。

网络用的是交替注意力。一次沿时间方向，让同一条轨迹的前后帧互相看，一次跨轨迹，让同一时段里的不同点互相看。作者的假设是，时间上前后不一致、点和点之间对不上，本身就是修错的线索，而缺口前后的观测，加上相关点的运动，可以帮忙把缺口补上。

为了算得动，修正器每次只处理一组轨迹，默认 1024 条一组，各组独立计算，所以修正器的计算量随像素数线性增长。整个修正器只有 1910 万参数。

这篇最巧的地方在训练。直觉的做法，是在合成视频上真的跑一遍跟踪器和深度模型，再让修正器学着把结果修成真值。但这样一来，训练数据就和某一个模型的错误模式绑死了。

ReLift 训练时，根本不跑跟踪器和深度模型，也不渲染图像。它直接拿 PointOdyssey 数据集里干净的 3D 轨迹，人为加上随机的破坏，模拟抬升会出的错，再让网络把它还原回来。

破坏的方式，正好对应前面那四类错误。给视线加小角度的偏转，模拟对应错误。给距离加独立噪声和随时间相关的偏移，模拟抖动和漂移。偶尔加大的跳变，或者在遮挡开始和结束的附近，故意留下一些落在遮挡物深度上的点，模拟错表面。再用移动的遮挡块和连续丢帧，制造缺口。

这些噪声的强度、相关程度和离群比例，每个训练样本都重新随机抽，借鉴的是机器人领域的 domain randomization，目的是让修正器见过足够多样的错误，换一个跟踪器或者深度模型也能用。

损失函数同时监督位置，和相邻两帧之间的位移，输入里缺失的帧也要监督，运动轨迹的权重是静止轨迹的四倍。训练一共 15 万步，在一张 RTX PRO 6000 显卡上完成。

### 实验和效果

评测用的是 WorldTrack 的四个数据集，两个真实场景，Aria Digital Twin 和 Panoptic Studio，两个合成场景，PointOdyssey 和 Dynamic Replica，每段视频取前 84 帧。

指标叫 APD，先对预测结果做一次相似变换对齐，再算预测点离真实位置在一定距离以内的比例，阈值从 0.1 米到 1 米取平均，分全部点和运动的点两种算。

结果在表 1。值得一说的是，下面这六行，用的是同一个训练好的修正器，只换 2D 跟踪器和几何模型，没有重新训练。

最好的配置是 CoWTracker 加 4RC 的几何，四个数据集平均，全部点 89.01，运动点 86.95，两项都是表里最高。

这里有一个很干净的对比。4RC 本身也是一个 3D 跟踪方法，它自己的结果是 86.94 和 81.60。ReLift 用 4RC 的几何，换成自己的跟踪加修正，全部点高了 2.1，运动点高了 5.4。

修正到底加了多少，看这张图。在六种组合上，修正后的轨迹都比原始抬升轨迹好，提升最大的是被遮挡的点。

按表 2，四个数据集的平均精度，修正带来 1.6 到 3.6 个点的提升。被挡住的点提升更明显，CoWTracker 加 VGGT-Ω 这一组，从 71.6 提到了 80.8。

速度方面，用 VGGT-Ω 几何的两个配置，精度和 4RC 相当，整条流水线快 2.7 到 4.4 倍。在 512 分辨率下，4RC 要 31.8 秒，ReLift 的 AllTracker 加 VGGT-Ω 配置是 7.3 秒。

到 1024 分辨率，4RC、V-DPM 和 DELTAv2 都因为显存不够跑不了，ReLift 的两个 AllTracker 配置还能跑。

### 和以前方法的对比

定性对比里，论文挑了转动、大位移和遮挡的例子。比如一只狗的头被挡住后又出现，ReLift 能把轨迹接上，别的方法的轨迹会提前断掉。

再看最关键的消融，训练数据到底该用模拟的，还是用真实模型跑出来的。作者用同样的网络和训练量，比较了三种训练方式，结果在这张图。

用真实模型输出训练的两个版本，在训练场景上提升很大，分别是 14.1 和 8.3 个点，但换到测试集上就不稳定了，用 VGGT-Ω 输出训练的那个，在每个测试数据集上都比不修正还差。而模拟训练的单个模型，在每个数据集、两种几何输入下，都有提升。

作者也排除了一个疑问。用真实输出训练，要先筛掉对不齐的数据窗口，差距可能来自数据不同。于是他们让模拟训练用完全相同的数据窗口再跑一遍，优势还在。

哪种模拟破坏最重要？表 2 显示，去掉错表面这一类，提升几乎消失，比如 CoWTracker 加 VGGT-Ω，从提升 2.6 个点降到 0.6 个点。去掉相关噪声，影响就小得多。

我的判断是，这篇真正有价值的地方，是把问题拆开了。跟踪和几何各自用最好的现成模型，只训练一个只看几何、不认具体模型的小修正器。作者称，以后 2D 跟踪器或者深度模型变强，换上去就能用，不用重训，论文里在三种几何之间切换，平均精度也确实一路上升。

我的判断是，也要看到，修正本身带来的提升并不大，平均是 1.6 到 3.6 个点，最高的精度，很大程度来自选了更好的跟踪器和几何。而精度最高的 4RC 几何配置，整条流程要 40.4 秒，比 4RC 自己的 31.8 秒还慢。

### 局限

作者自己承认，性能对输入的几何质量很敏感。在另一个合成数据集 Kubric 上，用 DA3 和 VGGT-Ω 几何的版本，全部点的精度都比不过 4RC。

模拟训练也有没覆盖到的错误。相机位姿的误差，还有跟踪点持续跳到另一个物体的轨迹上，这两种都没有专门模拟。

另外，ReLift 的轨迹投影回图像，精度仍然低于原生的 CoWTracker。CoWTracker 的两个配置，到 1024 分辨率时显存也不够。

### 未来方向

作者提出的下一步，是让方法对几何误差和持续的对应错误更鲁棒，正好针对刚才那两个局限。论文说，项目页上有更多例子和代码。

我的判断是，这种拆分思路对下游很实用。比如做机器人，或者用运动轨迹控制视频生成，需要稠密 3D 轨迹的人，可以直接拿现有的跟踪和深度模型，再接一个 1910 万参数的修正器。不过论文里没有做这类下游任务的实验，实际效果还要再看。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from the Technion and NVIDIA. It's called ReLift, and the subtitle sums it up: dense 3D tracking as lifted trajectory repair.

The goal is to take one ordinary video from a single camera and recover how every pixel in the first frame moves through 3D space over time.

### Problem

Why would you want that? The paper points to robot manipulation policy learning, dynamic scene reconstruction, sensor simulation, and video generation controlled by motion trajectories.

The hard part is that you have to get three things right at once. You need correspondence, meaning you recognize the same point across frames. You need depth, meaning how far that point is from the camera. And when the point is hidden, you still need to infer where it went.

On top of that, 4D ground truth, meaning 3D positions over time, is scarce, so there is little data to supervise all of this directly.

### Prior work

The usual approach trains one feed-forward network end to end. Video goes in, 3D tracks come out, and the network learns all three jobs together.

This figure shows several of those methods, such as 4RC, V-DPM and TrackCraft3R, with speed on the horizontal axis and accuracy on the vertical axis.

The authors start from a simple observation. Two of the three jobs already have strong off-the-shelf models. Dedicated 2D trackers are very good at correspondence, and geometry models give depth and camera poses without needing annotated dynamic 4D trajectories.

They also check this. The native 2D tracker CoWTracker beats the image-plane accuracy of every 3D method they tested, once those methods are projected back into the image.

### Key idea

So the key idea is to divide the labor. Correspondence comes from a 2D tracker, depth comes from a geometry model, and combining them gives a rough 3D trajectory the authors call the lift. The scarce 4D supervision is spent only on what's left: repairing errors and filling in motion that was hidden.

Here's a finding that's easy to miss. Accuracy here means the share of predicted points that land close enough to the true position. With the same geometry inputs, CoWTracker plus VGGT-Omega lifts, with hidden stretches just linearly interpolated and no learned correction at all, already reach eighty-four point nine on all points. TrackCraft3R with the same geometry gets eighty-three point nine, and DELTAv2 gets eighty-one point one.

In other words, a simple stitched-together baseline can already match or beat dedicated 3D trackers when the geometry is held fixed.

### Method

Here is the pipeline. Frozen pretrained models process the video: the 2D tracker gives each point's image position and visibility, and the geometry model gives depth and cameras. Combining them yields 3D trajectories that are corrupted and full of gaps, and a corrector then predicts a 3D adjustment for every frame, which is added back on.

The lifting step itself is simple. The 2D track tells you which viewing ray the point sits on, the depth model tells you how far along that ray to go, and walking that far out from the camera gives the 3D position.

The authors sort lifting errors into four kinds. Correspondence errors from the 2D tracker tilt the viewing ray. Depth jitter and drift slide the point back and forth along the ray. Wrong-surface errors happen when the depth comes from some other surface, say at an object boundary, or when the track has latched onto an occluder. And finally, observations go missing when the point is hidden or the tracker loses it.

A hidden point picks up the occluder's depth, so the authors throw away any observation the tracker marks as not visible. The gaps are filled by linear interpolation, and after the last observation the point simply holds its position.

The corrector sees only geometry. For each point and frame it gets the offset from the starting position, whether this frame has a usable observation, how many frames away the nearest observation is, the viewing ray, and the camera position. To save compute, every four frames are packed into one token.

The network uses alternating attention. One step looks along time, so the frames of a single track can see each other. The next looks across tracks, so different points in the same time window can see each other. The authors' hypothesis is that inconsistencies over time and disagreements between tracks are themselves clues for repair, and that observations before and after a gap, plus related motion, can guide the fill-in.

To keep this tractable, the corrector handles tracks in groups, one thousand twenty-four by default, and each group is processed independently, so the corrector's cost grows linearly with the number of pixels. The whole corrector has just nineteen point one million parameters.

The clever part is training. The obvious approach would be to run real trackers and depth models on synthetic videos and teach the corrector to fix their output. But then the training data is tied to the error patterns of those particular models.

ReLift never runs a tracker or a depth model during training, and never renders an image. It takes clean 3D trajectories from the PointOdyssey dataset, deliberately corrupts them to mimic lifting errors, and trains the network to recover the clean version.

The corruptions mirror the four error types. Small angular tilts of the viewing ray stand in for correspondence errors. Independent noise and time-correlated offsets on the distance stand in for jitter and drift. Occasional large jumps, plus some points kept at the occluder's depth near the start or end of an occlusion, stand in for wrong surfaces. Moving occluder boxes and runs of dropped frames create the gaps.

The noise strengths, correlations and outlier rates are redrawn for every training example. That borrows domain randomization from robotics, and the point is to expose the corrector to enough variety that it still works when you swap in a different tracker or depth model.

The loss supervises both positions and frame-to-frame motion, including frames that were missing in the input, and moving tracks count four times as much as static ones. Training runs for one hundred fifty thousand steps on a single RTX PRO 6000 GPU.

### Results

Evaluation uses the four WorldTrack datasets. Two are real, Aria Digital Twin and Panoptic Studio, and two are synthetic, PointOdyssey and Dynamic Replica, using the first eighty-four frames of each clip.

The metric is called APD. Predictions are first aligned with a similarity transform, then you measure the share of points within a distance threshold of the truth, averaged over thresholds from ten centimeters to one meter, for all points and for moving points separately.

The results are in Table one. The key detail is that the six rows at the bottom all use one trained corrector. Only the 2D tracker and the geometry model change, with no retraining.

The best setup is CoWTracker with 4RC geometry. Averaged over the four datasets it scores eighty-nine point zero one on all points and eighty-six point nine five on moving points, the highest in the table on both.

There's a nice controlled comparison here. 4RC is itself a 3D tracking method, and its own scores are eighty-six point nine four and eighty-one point six. Using 4RC's geometry but swapping in ReLift's tracking and correction adds two point one points on all points and five point four on moving points.

How much does the correction itself add? This figure shows it. Across all six combinations, the corrected trajectories beat the raw lift, and the biggest gains are on hidden points.

In Table two, correction adds between one point six and three point six points of average accuracy. Hidden points gain much more: for CoWTracker with VGGT-Omega, they go from seventy-one point six to eighty point eight.

On speed, the two VGGT-Omega setups are competitive with 4RC in accuracy while running the full pipeline two point seven to four point four times faster. At five-twelve resolution, 4RC takes thirty-one point eight seconds, and ReLift with AllTracker and VGGT-Omega takes seven point three.

At ten twenty-four resolution, 4RC, V-DPM and DELTAv2 all run out of GPU memory, while ReLift's two AllTracker setups still run.

### Comparison

In the qualitative comparisons, the paper picks examples with rotation, large motion and occlusion. In one, a dog's head disappears behind something and comes back, and ReLift picks the track back up while the other methods' tracks stop short.

Now the most important ablation: should you train on simulated errors or on real model outputs? With the same network and training budget, the authors compare three training sources, shown in this figure.

The two models trained on real provider output improve a lot on their own training scenes, by fourteen point one and eight point three points, but the gains don't transfer reliably, and the one trained on VGGT-Omega output is worse than no correction on every test dataset. The single simulation-trained model improves on every dataset, with both geometry providers.

The authors also rule out one objection. Training on real outputs requires dropping windows where the ground truth can't be aligned, so the gap might just come from different data. They retrain the simulated version on exactly the same windows, and its advantage holds.

Which corruption matters most? Table two shows that removing wrong-surface corruptions almost wipes out the gain. For CoWTracker with VGGT-Omega, the improvement drops from two point six points to zero point six. Removing correlated noise matters much less.

My read is that the real value here is the decomposition. Use the best off-the-shelf models for tracking and geometry, and train only a small corrector that sees geometry and doesn't care which model produced it. The authors argue that better trackers or depth models can be swapped in without retraining, and in the paper, moving across three geometry providers does raise average accuracy each time.

My read is also that the correction itself adds a modest amount, one point six to three point six points on average, and much of the top accuracy comes from picking stronger trackers and geometry. The most accurate setup, with 4RC geometry, takes forty point four seconds end to end, slower than 4RC's own thirty-one point eight.

### Limitations

The authors acknowledge that performance is sensitive to the quality of the input geometry. On another synthetic benchmark, Kubric, the DA3 and VGGT-Omega versions trail 4RC on all-point accuracy.

The simulation also leaves some errors out. It doesn't model camera pose errors, and it doesn't model a track persistently jumping onto another object's trajectory.

Projected back into the image, ReLift's tracks are still less accurate than native CoWTracker. And the two CoWTracker setups run out of memory at ten twenty-four resolution.

### Future work

The authors name the next step as robustness to geometry errors and persistent correspondence errors, which targets exactly those gaps. The paper says the project page has more examples and code.

My read is that this decomposition is practical for anyone downstream who needs dense 3D tracks, say for robotics or motion-controlled video generation: take existing tracking and depth models and add a corrector of about nineteen million parameters. But the paper doesn't run any of those downstream tasks, so the real payoff is still to be shown.
