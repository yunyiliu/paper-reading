## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇苹果的论文，叫 Depth Pro 2，做的是单目度量深度估计，也就是只用一个普通摄像头，估计画面里每个像素离相机有多少米。

它的目标一句话就能说清楚，深度要准，边缘要清晰，放到视频里还要稳定不跳。作者称，以前的方法通常只能顾上清晰和稳定里的一头，而 Depth Pro 2 几样同时做到。

### 问题

先说为什么需要这个。论文开头举了个例子，你度假拍了一段狐獴的视频，回家看的时候觉得背景的树叶太乱，想后期把背景虚化掉。

要可靠地把狐獴和背景分开，每一帧都需要一张深度图。看这张图，同一段视频，不同方法给出的深度图差别很大，有的毛发边缘很清楚，但两帧之间狐獴的远近在变，有的前后很稳，但毛都糊成了一片。

作者把这个现象画成了这张散点图。横轴是时间一致性，越靠右越稳，纵轴是边界清晰度，越往上越清楚。单帧方法挤在左上角，清晰但不稳，多帧方法挤在右下角，稳定但发糊。

### 以前的方法

单帧方法，比如 InfiniDepth，一帧一帧单独处理，能把狐獴的毛勾勒得不错，但因为每帧各算各的，同一只狐獴在不同帧里被放在了不同的距离上。

多帧方法，比如 π3，能把狐獴稳定地放在同一个距离，但它处理的分辨率比输入视频低，毛的细节就被抹平了。作者认为，这类方法为了稳定，常常依赖很长的时间上下文，或者视频扩散模型，很吃资源，只好降低分辨率。

还有一类方法借鉴三维重建，把深度和相机位姿一起优化。作者指出，它们通常要同时处理很多视角，这也限制了能处理的图像分辨率，而高分辨率恰恰是清晰边界的前提。

### 核心想法

Depth Pro 2 的核心想法很简单，用一个网络，同时处理任意数量的帧，而且每一帧都用原始分辨率。多帧一起处理，深度就稳定，用原始分辨率，边界就清晰。

训练也不搞复杂的多阶段课程，直接用 1 到 32 帧的片段，把单张图片、视频和多视角数据混在一起训练。论文的说法是，单张图片教会它从一帧里推断真实尺度，带一致性损失的视频，教会它让估计在帧之间保持稳定。

### 方法

看这张结构图。主体是一个多视角 Transformer，一帧或者多帧一起输进去，最后每一帧各自经过一个 DPT 解码器，输出一张逆深度图。逆深度，就是距离的倒数。

帧和帧之间怎么交换信息呢。作者让前三分之一的 Transformer 层只在帧内部做注意力，因为前面的层主要编码低层特征，后面三分之二的层，在帧内注意力和跨帧注意力之间交替。

还有一个细节，位置编码里故意不放帧的顺序。这样网络对帧的排列顺序不敏感，可以处理任意多帧，也可以直接复用单图预训练模型的注意力权重，不用新加从头训练的层。

为了在原始分辨率上工作，主干从 DINOv3 初始化，它本身就是为高分辨率的稠密特征训练的。图像切成 16 乘 16 的小块，每块一个 token。位置坐标统一除以长边，这样长宽比的信息就不会丢。

那怎么得到以米为单位的深度。网络预测的是按焦距归一化的逆深度，再结合焦距换算成米。焦距如果照片的 EXIF 信息里有，就直接用，没有就用一个单独的小模型来估计。这个估计器只有 2350 万参数，论文报告，它给最小的模型增加大约百分之八的计算量，给最大的模型只增加大约百分之零点五。

训练数据是 19 个数据集，大部分是合成数据。有一个讲究，训练时完全不对真值深度做插值，因为在深度突变的边缘取平均，会产生飘在空中的假像素。

损失函数是四项直接相加。一项用有真实尺度的数据锚定米制深度，一项在所有数据上比较归一化之后的相对深度，一项只在合成数据上匹配深度的梯度，专门练边界，第四项是新加的多视角一致性损失。

这个一致性损失是这样算的。把一帧里的像素，用模型预测的深度反投影到三维空间，再用真实的相机位姿投到另一帧，看投过去的深度，和模型在那一帧预测的深度对不对得上。遮挡和运动物体会破坏这个假设，作者就先用真值深度检查一遍，偏差超过百分之二的像素直接剔除。

推理时有两种模式。缓冲流式，把视频切成互相重叠的片段，每段内部所有帧一起算，重叠部分加权融合。无缓冲流式，每来一帧马上出结果，只看自己和之前缓存的若干帧。

模型一共四个尺寸，从 2400 万参数到 8.7 亿参数。论文报告，在一块 B200 显卡上流式处理 1080p 视频，最小的 S 版每秒 53 帧，比大多数视频录制用的每秒 30 帧还快，最大的 H+ 版是每秒 8 帧。

### 实验和效果

实验全部是零样本，也就是测试的数据集训练时没见过。度量深度用了 11 个数据集，分单图和视频两种设置，视频设置里每个方法都拿到同样 50 帧的片段。跟其他方法比的，都是最大的 H+ 版。

先看单张图片的度量深度，表 2。指标是 δ1，就是误差在 1.25 倍以内的像素比例。按各数据集的排名取平均，Depth Pro 2 在给真实焦距时平均排名 2.4，用自己估计的焦距时是 2.7，都是第一，第二名 UniK3D 分别是 3.6 和 3.1。

再看视频设置，表 3。Depth Pro 2 给真实焦距时平均排名 1.0，也就是在零样本的方法里，四个数据集全部第一，用估计焦距时是 1.2。比如在 Sintel 上，给真实焦距时，它的 δ1 是 51.2，其他方法里最高的 Depth Pro 是 36.5。

边界清晰度看表 4，用的是边界 F1 分数。单图设置下它平均排名 1.6，视频设置下是 1.3，都排第一，两个设置的第二名都是 InfiniDepth，分别是 3.0 和 2.7。

时间稳定性看表 5，指标叫 jitter，衡量同一个点的深度误差在相邻帧之间跳动多少，越低越好。Depth Pro 2 平均排名 1.5，第一。在 Sintel 上它是 1.39，其他方法里最好的是 DepthCrafter 的 2.09。

焦距估计在表 6。六个数据集上全部排第一，焦距误差在百分之二十五以内的图片比例，平均是百分之八十八点六，前作 Depth Pro 是百分之七十一点七。

论文还测了一个下游应用，用深度图做背景虚化。同一个渲染器，只换深度来源，Depth Pro 2 在五项指标上全部最好，和 Depth Pro 比，PSNR 高了 0.75 分贝。

### 和以前方法的对比

那么稳定性到底是不是来自跨帧注意力。作者做了个测试，推理时随机切断帧与帧之间的注意力连接。切得越多，jitter 越高，从全部保留时的 0.24，一路升到全部切断时的 0.84。

清晰度是不是来自分辨率。把输入短边缩到 360 像素，边界 F1 只有 24.1，用原始分辨率是 57.7，翻了一倍多。

训练上的几个选择也做了对照实验。只用正方形裁剪训练，Waymo 上的度量 δ1 掉了 46.6 个百分点，作者认为，模型需要见过各种长宽比，才能恢复真实尺度。把 DINOv3 换成 DINOv2，ARKitScenes 上的度量 δ1 掉了 12.8 个点。去掉多视角一致性损失，jitter 上升 0.05。

和前作 Depth Pro 相比，一个关键差别是，Depth Pro 会把每张图缩放成 1536 乘 1536，而 Depth Pro 2 直接在原始分辨率上训练和推理。论文的对照实验显示，训练时不做缩放，边界精度比 Depth Pro 的做法高 5 个百分点以上。

我的判断是，这篇最有意思的地方，是它没有用视频扩散模型，也没有加专门的时序模块，就是一个单图 ViT，加上跨帧注意力和一致性损失，就把清晰和稳定这对矛盾同时解决了。方案越简单，越容易被别人复用。

### 局限

论文没有单独的局限章节，但从数据里能看到几点。第一，它不是每个数据集都第一。比如时间稳定性在 Spring 上，VeloDepth 是 0.72，比 Depth Pro 2 的 0.86 更好，单图边界 F1 在 Sintel 上，InfiniDepth 的 44.8 也高于它的 40.2。

第二，在相对深度的视频评测里，它的平均排名是第二，不是第一。

第三，训练数据里有大量内部数据，包括两千四百多万帧内部合成数据，和三百多万帧带飞行时间传感器深度标注的手持手机视频。对照实验显示，去掉内部数据，Waymo 上的精度、边界清晰度和 jitter 反而变好，但 ARKitScenes 上的度量 δ1 会掉 45.2 个百分点。训练本身也用了 128 块 B200 显卡。

第四，实时性有限。论文的测试显示，无缓冲流式在 1080p 下能达到每秒 30 帧的，只有 S 和 B 两个小模型。另外，表 5 的 jitter 是在合成数据上沿点轨迹算的。

第五，多帧输入时，模型假设整段视频的焦距不变，取每帧估计值的中位数。

### 未来方向

论文没有专门写未来方向。它交出来的，是一整套可以直接用的模型，四个尺寸，支持图片和视频，支持流式处理。作者还给了推荐设置，缓冲模式用 16 帧一段、重叠 4 帧，无缓冲模式缓存 16 帧。

我的判断是，最直接的受益者是视频后期和三维相关的应用，比如视频背景虚化，和用单张照片生成新视角，论文附录已经展示了这两类例子。

我的判断是，接下来值得关注两点，一是变焦这种焦距不断变化的视频，在焦距固定的假设下表现如何，二是这么依赖内部数据的方案，别人只用公开数据，能复现到什么程度。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from Apple. It's called Depth Pro 2, and it tackles metric monocular depth: from a single ordinary camera, estimate how many meters away every pixel is.

The goal fits in one line. The depth should be accurate, the edges should be sharp, and on video it should hold still from frame to frame. The authors claim earlier methods usually get either sharpness or stability, and that Depth Pro 2 gets all of it at once.

### Problem

Why would you want this? The paper opens with an example. You filmed a meerkat on holiday, and back home the leaves behind it look busy, so you'd like to blur the background after the fact.

To separate the meerkat from the background reliably, you need a depth map for every frame. In this figure, the same clip gives very different depth maps. One method draws crisp fur but moves the meerkat closer and farther between frames. Another keeps it steady but smears the fur.

The authors turn that into this scatter plot. Temporal consistency runs along the horizontal axis, more stable to the right, and boundary sharpness runs up the vertical axis. Single-frame methods bunch in the top left, sharp but unstable. Multi-frame methods bunch in the bottom right, stable but blurry.

### Prior work

A single-frame method like InfiniDepth handles each frame on its own. It outlines the fur well, but because every frame is computed separately, the same meerkat lands at different distances in different frames.

A multi-frame method like pi three keeps the meerkat at a steady distance, but it runs below the video's resolution, so the fur gets smoothed away. The authors argue these methods buy stability with attention over many frames or with video diffusion priors, which cost a lot of memory and compute, so they drop resolution, and the boundaries blur.

Another line borrows from 3D reconstruction and optimizes depth together with camera poses. The authors point out that these methods are built to handle many views at once, which again limits the image resolution they can process, and high resolution is exactly what sharp boundaries need.

### Key idea

The key idea is simple. One network processes any number of frames together, each at its native resolution. Processing frames jointly makes depth stable. Native resolution keeps boundaries sharp.

Training skips the multi-stage curricula, too. The model trains on clips of one to thirty-two frames, mixing single images, video and multi-view data. In the paper's words, single images teach it to infer metric scale from one frame, and video with a consistency loss teaches it to keep its estimates stable across frames.

### Method

Here's the architecture. At its core is a multi-view transformer that takes one frame or many frames together. Each frame then goes through its own DPT decoder, which outputs an inverse depth map. Inverse depth is just one over distance.

How do frames share information? The first third of the transformer blocks only attend within a frame, since early blocks mostly encode low-level features. The remaining two thirds alternate between attention within a frame and attention across frames.

One more detail: the position encoding deliberately leaves out frame order. That makes the network indifferent to how frames are ordered, so it can take any number of them and reuse the attention weights of a single-image pretrained model, without adding new blocks trained from scratch.

To work at native resolution, the backbone starts from DINOv3, which was trained to produce dense, high-resolution features. The image is cut into sixteen by sixteen patches, one token each. Patch coordinates are divided by the longer side rather than normalized per axis, so the aspect ratio isn't thrown away.

So how do you get depth in meters? The network predicts inverse depth normalized by focal length, and the focal length converts it to meters. If the photo's EXIF data has the focal length, it's used directly. If not, a separate small model estimates it. That estimator has twenty-three and a half million parameters, and the paper reports it adds about eight percent to the compute of the smallest model and about half a percent to the largest.

Training uses nineteen datasets, mostly synthetic. One careful choice: ground-truth depth is never interpolated during training, because averaging across a depth edge creates flying pixels.

The loss is a plain sum of four terms. One anchors metric depth on data with true scale. One compares normalized relative depth on all data. One matches depth gradients, on synthetic data only, to train the boundaries. The fourth is a new multi-view consistency loss.

Here's how the consistency loss works. Take a pixel in one frame, lift it into 3D using the predicted depth, and project it into another frame using the ground-truth camera poses. Then check whether that depth agrees with what the model predicted in the other frame. Occlusions and moving objects break this assumption, so the authors first warp with the ground-truth depth and drop pixels that are off by more than two percent.

At inference there are two modes. Buffered streaming cuts the video into overlapping clips, processes all frames in a clip together, and blends the overlaps. Unbuffered streaming returns depth the moment each frame arrives, looking only at that frame and a cache of earlier ones.

The family comes in four sizes, from about twenty-four million to eight hundred seventy-two million parameters. The paper reports that streaming ten-eighty p video on one B200 GPU, the smallest model, S, runs at fifty-three frames per second, faster than the thirty frames per second most video is shot at. The largest, H plus, runs at eight.

### Results

All tests are zero-shot, meaning the test datasets were not seen in training. Metric depth is tested on eleven datasets, in a single-image setting and a video setting, where every method gets the same fifty-frame clips. Comparisons use the largest model, H plus.

First, metric depth on single images, Table 2. The metric is delta one, the share of pixels within a factor of one point two five of the truth. Averaging the per-dataset ranks, Depth Pro 2 scores two point four with ground-truth focal length and two point seven with its own estimate, first in both. The runner-up, UniK3D, scores three point six and three point one.

Now the video setting, Table 3. With ground-truth focal length, Depth Pro 2 has an average rank of one point zero, first among zero-shot methods on all four datasets, and one point two with estimated focal length. On Sintel with ground-truth focal length, for example, its delta one is fifty-one point two, while the best other method, Depth Pro, gets thirty-six point five.

Boundary sharpness is in Table 4, measured by boundary F1. The average rank is one point six on single images and one point three on video, first in both. Second place in both is InfiniDepth, at three point zero and two point seven.

Temporal stability is in Table 5. The metric, jitter, measures how much the depth error at a tracked point jumps between neighboring frames. Lower is better. Depth Pro 2 has an average rank of one point five, first. On Sintel it scores one point three nine, against two point zero nine for the best other method, DepthCrafter.

Focal length estimation is in Table 6. It ranks first on all six datasets. On average, eighty-eight point six percent of images get a focal length within twenty-five percent of the truth, versus seventy-one point seven percent for Depth Pro.

The paper also tests a downstream task, using depth to blur the background. The renderer stays fixed and only the depth source changes. Depth Pro 2 is best on all five metrics, and beats Depth Pro on PSNR by zero point seven five decibels.

### Comparison

So does the stability really come from cross-frame attention? The authors test this by randomly cutting attention links between frames at inference. The more they cut, the higher the jitter, rising from zero point two four with all links kept to zero point eight four with all of them cut.

Does sharpness come from resolution? With the input's short side shrunk to three hundred sixty pixels, boundary F1 is just twenty-four point one. At native resolution it's fifty-seven point seven, more than double.

Training choices were tested too. Training only on square crops drops metric delta one on Waymo by forty-six point six percentage points, which the authors read as the model needing varied aspect ratios to recover metric scale. Swapping DINOv3 for DINOv2 costs twelve point eight points of metric delta one on ARKitScenes. Removing the multi-view consistency loss raises jitter by zero point zero five.

Against its predecessor, Depth Pro, one key difference is that Depth Pro resizes every image to fifteen thirty-six by fifteen thirty-six, while Depth Pro 2 trains and runs at native resolution. The paper's controlled experiments show that training without resizing improves boundary accuracy by more than five percentage points over Depth Pro's approach.

My read is that the most interesting thing here is what's missing. No video diffusion model, no special temporal module. It's a single-image ViT with cross-frame attention and a consistency loss, and native resolution plus joint processing resolve the sharp-versus-stable trade-off. A recipe that simple is easy for others to reuse.

### Limitations

The paper has no separate limitations section, but the numbers show a few. First, it isn't first everywhere. On Spring, VeloDepth's jitter of zero point seven two beats Depth Pro 2's zero point eight six. And on single-image boundary F1 for Sintel, InfiniDepth's forty-four point eight is above its forty point two.

Second, in the relative-depth evaluation on video, its average rank is second, not first.

Third, the training mix leans on internal data, including over twenty-four million frames of internal synthetic data and over three million frames of real smartphone video with time-of-flight depth labels. Remove the internal data, and accuracy on Waymo, boundary sharpness and jitter actually improve, but metric delta one on ARKitScenes falls by forty-five point two percentage points. Training also used one hundred twenty-eight B200 GPUs.

Fourth, real time is limited. In the paper's measurements, only the two small models, S and B, reach thirty frames per second at ten-eighty p in unbuffered streaming. Also, the jitter in Table 5 is computed along point tracks on synthetic data.

Fifth, for multi-frame input, the model assumes the focal length is constant across the clip and takes the median of the per-frame estimates.

### Future work

The paper doesn't write a future-work section. What it delivers is a ready-to-use model family: four sizes, images and video, streaming included. The authors also give recommended settings: sixteen-frame clips with an overlap of four in buffered mode, and a sixteen-frame cache in unbuffered mode.

My read is that the first beneficiaries are video editing and 3D applications, like background blur on video and new views from a single photo. The appendix already shows examples of both.

My read is that two things are worth watching next. One, how it handles zoom, where the focal length keeps changing, given the fixed-focal assumption. Two, how far others can reproduce a recipe that leans this much on internal data, using only public datasets.
