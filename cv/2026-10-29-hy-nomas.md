## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇腾讯 Hy 视觉生成团队的论文，名字叫 Hy-Nomas，副标题是，走向自由形式的交互式世界生成。

简单说，它做的是一个可以边看边指挥的视频世界模型。你打一句话，比如让一条龙出现，或者按键盘往前走，它就接着刚才的画面，生成下一段视频，一轮一轮往下走。

模型主干是一个 80 亿参数的扩散模型。作者称，模型权重、代码和评测集都一并公开。

### 问题

先说什么是视频世界模型。论文的定义是，一个可以交互的虚拟环境，用户看到当前画面，决定做什么，模型再生成新的画面，既要反映这条指令，也要延续之前的历史。

普通的文生视频，是一段提示词提前定好一个短片。世界模型要在生成过程中不断接收新指令，还要保留之前动作的后果。

难点有好几个。第一，每一段生成的视频，都会成为下一段的依据，小错误会越滚越大，出现画面漂移、动作不合理，甚至整个场景崩掉。

第二，多轮交互里，场景信息要一直保留，但一个动作指令，只应该在它该发生的那段时间里生效。第三，文字指令和镜头控制要能配合，镜头走开再回来，之前看到的东西还得在。

### 以前的方法

论文对比的是三个代表性的系统，Google DeepMind 的 Genie 3，阿里云的 HappyOyster，还有开源的 LingBot-World v2。

按论文的说法，它们各有短板。Genie 3 的官方 API 不支持用文字交互。HappyOyster 的文字和镜头分别是两种模式，不能在同一段视频里交替使用。

LingBot-World v2 虽然都能覆盖，但它的文字控制，是从视觉语言模型生成的候选里挑一个，而不是直接接收用户写的任意指令。

镜头控制方面，论文说，现有的一些交互式世界模型，要么把平移和旋转都量化成固定的几个方向，要么旋转是连续的，平移只能用键盘的离散动作。

### 核心想法

Hy-Nomas 的核心设计，是把结构化的视频描述，和一种能区分动作的因果注意力对齐起来。

意思是，描述场景的文字，后面每一段都能看到，而描述动作的文字，只在它对应的那一段、那个时间窗口里起作用。场景要一直记住，动作做完了就别再重复。

另外，它用一个视觉语言模型当提示词增强器，论文叫 Prompt Enhancer，把用户随手写的一句话，加上之前的对话和视频历史，翻译成下一段的结构化描述和时长。

### 方法

先看数据。训练视频的描述是结构化的 JSON。每个主体有固定编号和外观档案，每一段记录场景的变化，动作单独记录，带开始和结束时间。

镜头运动的句子用标签包起来。如果这段视频估出了可靠的相机轨迹，就把这些句子删掉，用轨迹来监督。没有轨迹，就保留文字，让模型学会用文字控制镜头。

再看模型。它从腾讯的 HunyuanVideo 1.5 出发，那是一个双向注意力的文生视频模型。作者先把它改成分段因果注意力，同一段之内互相可见，跨段只能看前面，不能看后面。

但作者发现了两个毛病。一是同一段里，动作的先后顺序会乱，因为画面能看到整段所有的动作文字。二是后面的段会重演前面的动作，因为之前的动作描述一直看得见。

解决办法，就是这张图右边的动作感知注意力。每段文字拆成一个场景描述的头，加上几个动作尾巴。场景头对后面的段一直开放，动作尾巴只对自己那一段、自己那个时间窗口开放。作者称，用了以后，动作顺序错误和跨段重复都大幅减少。

镜头控制，是在每个模块里加一个交叉注意力，输入的是帧和帧之间的相对位姿，平移和旋转都是连续的。用键盘玩的时候，W、A、S、D 和方向键会被换算成相机轨迹，速度、幅度和时长不用重新训练就能调。

训练时，每一段有百分之五十的概率，同时去掉相机轨迹和描述镜头的文字。这样模型在同一个视频里，既见过文字控制，也见过镜头控制，推理时才能交替使用。

训练一共五个阶段，就是这张表。先把潜空间换成压缩更狠的 DCAE-V，空间压缩从 16 倍提到 32 倍。然后是持续预训练，依次加入分段因果、动作感知和镜头控制。再用 Kimi K3 生成更细的描述，做高质量退火。

第四阶段叫 Segment Forcing。训练时让模型拿自己生成的历史当上下文，而不是拿真实视频，这样训练和推理看到的东西更一致，提前适应自己犯的错。

最后是扩散蒸馏，每一段的采样步数，从 100 步降到 4 步。上下文最长 36 万个 token，论文说足够生成分钟级的 720p 视频。

部署方面，作者用了 INT8 量化、滑动窗口注意力和 CPU 卸载，让它能在一张 RTX 4090 上跑。论文举的例子是，七段、100 秒、1280 乘 704、每秒 24 帧的视频，峰值显存 20.53 GiB。

想定制风格，就在蒸馏之前的模型上训练 LoRA，再合并进蒸馏后的模型，不用重新蒸馏。论文说，大约 100 个样本这个量级就够了。

### 实验和效果

评测用的是作者自建的 Hy-Nomas-Bench，一共 280 个案例，分四项，多轮文字交互 60 个，镜头导航 100 个，文字和镜头交替 60 个，空间记忆 60 个。

打分方式是两两对比的人工评测，每组三个标注员，判断 Hy-Nomas 更好、打平还是更差。需要初始图片的模型，统一用 Hy-Nomas 生成的第一帧来初始化。

先看多轮文字交互。在提示词遵循上，Hy-Nomas 对 HappyOyster 赢了百分之 65，输了百分之 10，对 LingBot-World v2 赢了百分之 75，输了百分之 8.3。

物理合理性上，对 HappyOyster 赢百分之 45，打平百分之 43.3，对 LingBot-World v2 赢百分之 68.3。

镜头导航的优势小一些。对 Genie 3，赢百分之 32，打平百分之 54，输百分之 14。对另外两家，也是赢的比输的多，但多数是打平。作者的解释是，镜头控制这个方向，研究得本来就更多。

文字和镜头交替这一项，只能和改造过的 LingBot-World v2 比。提示词遵循赢百分之 75，物理合理性赢百分之 73.3，镜头控制两家接近，打平占了百分之 68.3。

空间记忆是它唯一输给对手的一项。对 Genie 3，赢百分之 13.3，输百分之 30，作者承认 Genie 3 的空间一致性更强。对 HappyOyster 大致打平，对 LingBot-World v2 赢百分之 58.3，只输百分之 1.7。

提示词增强器也单独测了。作者在 Qwen3.8-27B 上微调出来的版本，按要求格式输出的成功率接近百分之百，原版 Qwen3.8-27B 大约百分之 50，Kimi-K3 大约百分之 70。

### 和以前方法的对比

和 Genie 3、HappyOyster 比，Hy-Nomas 的不同在于，一个模型里同时支持任意文字指令和连续镜头控制，能在同一个会话里来回切换，不用重置世界。

作者在讨论部分提出一个假设，文字动作控制做得好，很大程度来自精细的描述，扩散模型不用自己去想象没说清的内容，只要把描述好的场景画对。这一点论文没有做实验单独验证。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把世界模型的控制接口理清楚了。场景描述长期有效，动作描述限时有效，这个区分很朴素，但正好对准了多轮交互里动作重复和顺序错乱的问题。

我的判断是，也要看到评测的局限。所有对比都来自作者自建的评测集、作者组织的人工两两比较，没有画质之类的常规指标，动作感知注意力的效果也只有文字描述，没有给出数字，说服力要打点折扣。

### 局限

作者自己承认的局限有几条。第一，空间记忆还落后 Genie 3。作者猜测，一个原因可能是 Genie 能借助街景数据，训练里多加一些绕回原地的镜头轨迹，也可能有帮助。

第二，用 LoRA 微调电影风格以后，画面更好看了，但镜头控制退步，赢百分之 19，输百分之 37，作者认为是微调数据里的镜头轨迹太单一。

第三，单卡部署用的量化和稀疏注意力，可能带来细微的差别，段数越多越明显。作者还指出，时间上的因果，不等于物理上的因果，要学到合理的动作后果，需要把动作和结果连起来的数据。

### 未来方向

作者提了几个方向。一是做成 Agent 化的世界模型，把旧的上下文放到 CPU 内存或硬盘上，回到某个房间时，只取回和它相关的画面。

他们举了一个挂钟的例子。离开房间十分钟再回来，钟应该还在原处，但显示的时间要晚十分钟。光取回旧画面做不到这一点，作者设想，可以让一个 Agent 记下状态、算出更新，再交给生成模型。

二是机器人。作者认为，要用到机器人控制上，需要用和机器人动作配对的视频来训练，还要检验它的预测能不能帮助任务成功。目前只做了 LoRA 微调的初步尝试。

我的判断是，开源权重加上单卡能跑，会让交互式世界模型从少数大厂的演示，变成更多团队能动手实验的工具。接下来要看，别人用这套公开的评测集，能不能复现这些结论。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Tencent's Hy Visual Generation Team. It's called Hy-Nomas, and the subtitle is "Towards Free-form Interactive World Generation."

In plain terms, it's a video world model you can steer while you watch. Type a line, say, "a dragon appears," or press a key to walk forward, and it picks up from the current frame and generates the next segment of video, turn after turn.

The backbone is an eight-billion-parameter diffusion model. The authors say they are releasing the weights, the code and the benchmarks.

### Problem

First, what is a video world model? The paper defines it as an interactive virtual environment. You look at the current scene, decide what to do, and the model renders new frames that reflect your command and carry on from everything that came before.

That's different from ordinary text-to-video, where one prompt fixes a short clip in advance. A world model has to accept new commands while it's generating, and keep the consequences of earlier actions.

There are several hard parts. First, every generated segment becomes the evidence for the next one, so small errors compound into drifting appearance, implausible motion, or a scene that falls apart.

Second, over many turns the scene information has to stay available, while an action instruction should only take effect in the time window it was meant for. Third, text commands and camera control have to work together, and when the camera leaves and comes back, what was there before should still be there.

### Prior work

The paper compares against three representative systems: Genie 3 from Google DeepMind, HappyOyster from Alibaba Cloud, and the open-source LingBot-World v2.

According to the paper, each has a gap. Genie 3's official API doesn't support text-prompt interaction. HappyOyster handles text and camera in two separate modes, so you can't alternate between them within one video.

LingBot-World v2 can cover every setting, but its text control works by picking among proposals generated by a vision-language model, rather than taking free-form instructions from the user.

On camera control, the paper says some recent interactive world models quantize both translation and rotation into a few fixed directions, while others keep rotation continuous but restrict translation to discrete keyboard actions.

### Key idea

The central design in Hy-Nomas is to align structured video captions with a causal attention scheme that knows what an action is.

Text that describes the scene stays visible to every later segment. Text that describes an action only applies inside its own segment and its own time window. Remember the scene; don't replay the action.

On top of that, a vision-language model acts as a Prompt Enhancer. It takes whatever the user typed, plus the conversation and video so far, and turns it into a structured caption and a duration for the next segment.

### Method

Start with the data. Training captions are structured JSON. Each subject gets a fixed identifier and a static profile, each segment records what changed in the scene, and actions are logged separately with start and end times.

Camera-motion sentences are wrapped in tags. When a clip has a reliable estimated camera trajectory, those sentences are removed and the trajectory supervises the camera instead. When it doesn't, the text stays, so the model also learns to move the camera from words.

Now the model. It starts from Tencent's HunyuanVideo 1.5, a text-to-video model with bidirectional attention. The authors first switch it to segment-wise causal attention: tokens see everything within their own segment, and earlier segments, but never later ones.

That produced two problems. Within a segment, actions sometimes happened in the wrong order, because every frame could see every action in the caption. And later segments would replay earlier actions, because old action descriptions were still visible.

The fix is the action-aware attention on the right of this figure. Each segment's text is split into a descriptive head and several action tails. The head stays open to later segments; each action tail is visible only within its own segment and time window. The authors report that this substantially reduces both ordering errors and repeated actions.

Camera control is a cross-attention module in every block, fed with frame-to-frame relative poses, so both translation and rotation are continuous. When you play with a keyboard, W, A, S, D and the arrow keys are converted into a camera trajectory, and speed, scale and duration can be changed without retraining.

During training, each segment has a fifty percent chance of dropping both the camera trajectory and the camera-related text. So within a single video the model sees both text-controlled and camera-controlled segments, which is what lets it alternate between them at inference.

Training runs in five stages, shown in this table. First the latent space is moved to a more compressed autoencoder, DCAE-V, raising spatial compression from sixteen to thirty-two times. Then continual pre-training adds segment causality, action-aware masking and camera control in turn. Then a high-quality annealing stage uses more detailed captions written by Kimi K3.

Stage four is called Segment Forcing. The model is trained with its own generated history as context instead of real video, so training looks more like inference, and the model learns to cope with its own mistakes.

Last comes diffusion distillation, which cuts sampling from one hundred steps to four per segment. The context goes up to three hundred sixty thousand tokens, which the paper says is enough for minute-long video at seven-twenty p.

For deployment, the authors use INT8 quantization, sliding-window attention and CPU offloading so it runs on a single RTX 4090. Their example: a hundred-second video in seven segments, at twelve-eighty by seven-oh-four and twenty-four frames per second, peaks at twenty point five three gibibytes of GPU memory.

One more practical piece. To customize a style, you train a LoRA on the model before distillation, then merge it into the distilled model, with no need to redo distillation. The paper says on the order of a hundred samples is enough.

### Results

Evaluation uses the authors' own Hy-Nomas-Bench: two hundred eighty cases in four parts. Sixty for multi-turn text interaction, a hundred for camera navigation, sixty for interleaved text and camera, and sixty for spatial memory.

Scoring is pairwise human evaluation, with three annotators per group judging whether Hy-Nomas is better, tied or worse. Models that need a starting image are all initialized with the first frame generated by Hy-Nomas.

Multi-turn text first, in Figure thirteen. On prompt following, Hy-Nomas wins sixty-five percent against HappyOyster and loses ten percent. Against LingBot-World v2 it wins seventy-five percent and loses eight point three.

On physical plausibility, it wins forty-five percent against HappyOyster, with forty-three point three percent ties, and sixty-eight point three percent against LingBot-World v2.

Camera navigation is a closer call. Against Genie 3 it wins thirty-two percent, ties fifty-four, and loses fourteen. Against the other two it also wins more than it loses, but most cases are ties. The authors put that down to camera control being a more widely studied problem.

The interleaved text-and-camera test can only be run against an adapted LingBot-World v2. Hy-Nomas wins seventy-five percent on prompt following and seventy-three point three on physical plausibility. On camera control the two are close, with sixty-eight point three percent ties.

Spatial memory is the one test where it loses to a rival. Against Genie 3 it wins thirteen point three percent and loses thirty, and the authors acknowledge that Genie 3 keeps space more consistent. Against HappyOyster it's roughly even. Against LingBot-World v2 it wins fifty-eight point three percent and loses only one point seven.

The Prompt Enhancer was tested on its own too. The authors' version, fine-tuned from Qwen3.8-27B, follows the required output format almost one hundred percent of the time, versus roughly fifty percent for the original Qwen3.8-27B and roughly seventy for Kimi-K3.

### Comparison

To sum up: compared with Genie 3 and HappyOyster, what sets Hy-Nomas apart is one model that takes both free-form text and continuous camera control, and switches between them inside one session without resetting the world.

In the discussion, the authors offer a hypothesis: much of the text-driven action control comes from detailed captions. The diffusion model no longer has to imagine what wasn't specified; it just has to render the described scene correctly. The paper doesn't run an experiment that isolates this.

My read is that the most valuable thing here is a cleaner control interface for world models. Scene text lasts; action text expires. It's a simple distinction, but it targets exactly the repeated actions and out-of-order actions that plague multi-turn generation.

My read is also that the evaluation has limits. Every comparison comes from the authors' own benchmark and their own pairwise human judges, there are no standard quality metrics, and the effect of action-aware attention is described in words without numbers. That should temper how much weight the results carry.

### Limitations

The authors name several limitations themselves. First, spatial memory still trails Genie 3. They guess that Genie's grounding in Street View data may help it, and that training with more looping camera trajectories could help Hy-Nomas.

Second, a cinematic-style LoRA makes the video look better but hurts camera control: nineteen percent wins against thirty-seven percent losses. The authors blame the narrow range of camera trajectories in the fine-tuning data.

Third, the quantization and sparse attention used for single-GPU deployment can introduce subtle differences that grow more visible over successive segments. The authors also point out that causal information flow in time is not physical causality; learning plausible consequences of actions needs data that links actions to outcomes.

### Future work

For future work, the authors suggest a few directions. One is an agentic world model: move older context to CPU memory or storage, and when the user returns to a room, retrieve only the relevant earlier views.

They give the example of a wall clock. Leave a room for ten minutes and come back, and the clock should be in the same place but show a time ten minutes later. Retrieving an old frame can't do that; the authors suggest an agent could track the state, update it, and pass it to the generator.

Another is robotics. The authors say robot control would need training on videos paired with robot actions, plus tests of whether the predictions actually help tasks succeed. So far they have only a first step, a LoRA fine-tune.

My read is that open weights plus a single consumer GPU could move interactive world models from a few big-lab demos to something many more teams can experiment with. The thing to watch is whether others, using this public benchmark, reproduce these human-evaluation results.
