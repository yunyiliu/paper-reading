## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇英伟达的论文，合作者来自约翰斯·霍普金斯大学、香港理工大学等学校，论文叫 VFlash，讲的是怎么让视觉语言模型，又快又准地画出视觉规划。

它让模型一次前向计算，就输出一整条轨迹或者一个框，而不是一个坐标一个坐标地往外蹦。结果不光更快，在机器人轨迹上还更准。

### 问题

先说背景。现在做机器人和自动驾驶，常用视觉语言模型，也就是 VLM，当上层的大脑。它不一定只用文字思考，也可以输出视觉规划，比如点、框和轨迹，告诉下游要去哪里、东西会怎么动。

问题是，自回归的 VLM 生成这些规划，是一个坐标 token 接一个坐标 token 地生成。轨迹越长、物体越多、同时要 2D 和 3D，token 就越多，每多一个 token，就多一步串行计算。

对机器人来说，这直接卡住了反应速度。论文测了一个机器人操作流程，在 Jetson Thor 这种机载平台上，用自回归解码，每一步要 3552.7 毫秒，而在这个平台上，论文测的几个任务里，解码占了每一步时间的百分之 75 到 87。

### 以前的方法

想加速，常见的做法是投机解码，也就是 speculative decoding。先让一个小的草稿模型一口气猜出好几个 token，再让大模型并行检查，从头往后对，对到第一个不一致的地方就停。

这篇论文的直接基础叫 DFlash。它用一个轻量的块扩散草稿模型，一次前向就猜出一整块 token，再由目标模型验证，保证输出和原来的自回归模型一致。

但这套机制用在坐标上，效果打了折扣。论文测到，DFlash 在子任务文字上，每轮能接受 5.5 个 token，到了轨迹里面只有 2.2 个。原因是坐标只要和大模型的选择差一格，验证就失败，哪怕几何上只差一点点。结果解码时间只省了一半左右。

也有专门针对视觉输出的工作。LocateAnything 能把框当成一个整体并行解码，但只支持固定大小的 2D 图元。Spec-VLA 允许动作 token 稍有偏差，但仍然逐个 token 验证，每次前向只能确定 2.1 到 2.94 个 token。

### 核心想法

作者的观察是，视觉规划有一个文字没有的特点，它的结构是事先知道的。一旦确定了是框还是轨迹、几维、有几个点，每个位置该放什么，在解码之前就定了。

所以可以把一整个几何对象当成一个块，一次猜完。更关键的一点是，坐标好不好，应该看空间上准不准，而不是看它和大模型逐个 token 是不是一致。

于是 VFlash 做了两个改变。训练时，草稿模型直接学标注好的真实坐标，而不是模仿大模型的输出。推理时，只要猜出来的结构合法，就整块接受，不再逐个 token 验证。作者把这叫 Flash Primitive Decoding，简称 FPD。

### 方法

具体流程看这张图。模型先像平常一样输出文字，比如子任务描述。需要画轨迹的时候，它先写一个工具调用，声明这个块有多长，然后输出一个表示图元开始的特殊 token。

块的长度和图元类型一起，决定了整个布局。比如一个框是 6 个位置，起始符、4 个坐标和结束符。在 MolmoSpaces 实验里，一条同时带 2D 和 3D 坐标的轨迹，是 54 个 token。

坐标怎么表示也有讲究。他们的基础模型给每个坐标值单独一个 token，从 0 到 1000，一共 1001 个。机器人的 3D 坐标量化到 1 厘米的精度。对于用普通文字写数字的模型，就给每个坐标固定一个符号位加五位数字。

草稿模型本身很小，5 层 Transformer，可训练参数 5.37 亿。它读取冻结的目标 VLM 里五层的特征，在一次前向里，把块里所有被遮住的位置一起填出来。目标模型是 Qwen3-4B 语言模型，加上 MoonViT 视觉编码器。

推理时，每个位置只允许出现合法的 token，坐标位置只能是数字，分隔符的位置是固定的。填完之后检查格式，框还要额外过一道置信度检查。通过就整块接受，不通过，就退回让大模型按原来的方式重新生成。

这样算下来，一个合法的图元，只需要一次草稿前向，加一次目标模型前向来更新缓存。自回归则是每个坐标都要一次目标前向。所以规划画得再细，串行的步数也不会增加。

### 实验和效果

实验覆盖面很广，有 2D 轨迹、框、3D 机器人轨迹、稠密点运动预测，再加上机器人操作和自动驾驶两类下游任务。大多数对比里，自回归基线和 VFlash 用的是同一个 4B 目标模型，只换解码方式。

先看 3D 机器人轨迹，这是表 4。在 InternA1、AgiBot 和 MolmoBot 三个数据集、三种训练变体上，VFlash 的四项指标，全部好于自回归。

平均位移误差下降了百分之 18 到 61。在 AgiBot 上，误差 5 厘米以内的路径点比例，从百分之 50 升到 73，在同时输出 2D 和 3D 的设定下，从百分之 40 升到 74。

自回归模型要同时输出 2D 和 3D 时，3D 误差比只输出 3D 高百分之 15 到 51，而 VFlash 一次并行出两段，精度基本不受影响。

2D 轨迹在表 2。VFlash 在两个基准上，六项误差全部低于自回归目标，幅度是百分之 3 到 7。

框的生成在表 3，结果就复杂一些。带回退机制时，平均 F1 和自回归基本持平，速度快 2.6 到 2.8 倍。在宽松的 IoU 0.5 标准下更好，在最严格的 0.95 标准下更差。如果不带回退、直接接受，在密集场景的 Dense200 上，平均 F1 会掉大约 20 个点。

稠密 3D 点运动预测在表 5，用的是已发布的 MolmoMotion 模型当目标。论文报告，单次生成快了大约 25 倍，在 H100 上，HOT3D 从 79.8 秒降到 3.21 秒。但精度有涨有跌，DAVIS 上平均误差降了百分之 27，WorldTrack 的终点误差却升了百分之 15。

下游任务先看 MolmoSpaces 机器人操作，在表 6。同一个世界动作模型，原版平均成功率是百分之 62.6，换上 VFlash 生成的规划做条件之后，提到了百分之 70.2。

表 7 做了拆分。同样的流程，规划换成自回归生成的，成功率只有百分之 61.0。不用轨迹、只用图像特征，是百分之 63.7。只有 3D 的规划是百分之 69.3，2D 加 3D 最好。

在 LIBERO 系列上，扰动越大，提升越大，标准 LIBERO 只多 0.8 个点，加了扰动的 LIBERO-PRO 多 6.8 个点。自动驾驶上，NAVSIM 加了强化学习之后，PDMS 到 90.7，和 Qwen-Drive-1.0 持平，Waymo 上和列出的最好结果差 0.05。

速度看图 4。在 MolmoSpaces 流程里，Jetson Thor 上每一步从 3552.7 毫秒降到 703.3 毫秒，作为对比，DFlash 是 1828.4 毫秒。整体上，每步延迟降了 2.6 到 5.2 倍，提升几乎全部来自解码，解码本身快了 12 到 42 倍。

### 和以前方法的对比

和 DFlash 这类投机解码比，最大的区别是放弃了无损。投机解码保证输出和大模型一致，VFlash 不保证，它只保证结构合法，评判标准换成了空间精度，和对下游动作有没有用。

和 LocateAnything 比，它把一次出一整个图元的思路，从固定大小的 2D 框，扩展到了变长轨迹、2D 加 3D 联合输出和稠密运动。和 Spec-VLA 比，它不再逐个 token 验证，而是整个图元一次接受。

我的判断是，这篇最值得记住的不是加速，而是一个反直觉的结果，一个 5 层的草稿模型，在轨迹精度上反而超过了它所依附的 4B 大模型。论文的解释是图元结构已知，我猜训练目标换成真实坐标也起了作用，但论文没有把这两点单独拆开验证。

### 局限

论文没有单独讲局限，但实验里能看出几点。第一，加速是有代价的，输出不再和大模型一致。框的结果说明，没有回退时精度会明显下降，最严格标准下的定位也不如自回归。

第二，稠密运动的 25 倍，作者自己说是初步测量，只比了单次生成，而且精度指标有好有坏。另外在稠密运动里，帧和帧之间仍然是串行生成的。

第三，机器人操作的下游实验，每一行只有一次训练和一次评测，没有多个随机种子。MolmoSpaces 的提升，同时包含了换 VLM 和加规划两个变化，表 7 才把解码方式单独拆开。

第四，自动驾驶上，VFlash 和最强的系统打平或略低，没有超过。Waymo 上强化学习的检查点，是在提供奖励的同一个验证集上选的，不过最终分数用的是分开的测试集。

### 未来方向

作者提到的下一步，是在固定的延迟预算里，从同一组观察同时生成多个互补的图元，看能不能改善控制。

我的判断是，对机器人来说，VLM 当大脑的一大障碍就是慢。如果画轨迹的成本几乎不随点数增加，规划就可以画得更细，更新得更频繁。

我的判断是，接下来要看真机。这篇的下游实验，都是在仿真和离线数据集上做的，还没有真机闭环测试。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from NVIDIA, with co-authors at Johns Hopkins, Hong Kong Polytechnic University and others. It's called VFlash, and it's about getting vision-language models to draw visual plans quickly and accurately.

The short version: the model outputs a whole trajectory or a whole bounding box in a single forward pass, instead of spitting out one coordinate at a time. And on robot trajectories, it's not just faster. It's more accurate.

### Problem

Some background. In robotics and self-driving, vision-language models, or VLMs, are increasingly used as the high-level brain. They don't have to think only in words. They can output visual plans, points, boxes and trajectories, that tell the rest of the system where to go and how things will move.

The problem is that an autoregressive VLM writes these plans one coordinate token at a time. Longer trajectories, more objects, or 2D and 3D together all mean more tokens, and every extra token is another sequential step.

For a robot, that caps how fast it can react. In one manipulation pipeline the paper measures on Jetson Thor, an embedded platform for robots and vehicles, autoregressive decoding takes three thousand five hundred fifty-two milliseconds per step, and across the tasks the paper profiles on Thor, decoding is seventy-five to eighty-seven percent of each step.

### Prior work

The usual fix is speculative decoding. A small draft model guesses several tokens at once, and the big model checks them in parallel, from left to right, stopping at the first one it disagrees with.

This paper builds directly on DFlash. DFlash uses a lightweight block-diffusion drafter that guesses a whole block of tokens in one forward pass, and then the target model verifies it, so the output stays identical to the original autoregressive model.

But that verification step works poorly on coordinates. The paper finds that DFlash accepts five point five tokens per round on sub-task text, but only two point two inside trajectories. A coordinate that's one bin off from the big model's choice fails verification, even if the geometric error is tiny. So DFlash only roughly halves decoding time.

There's also work aimed at visual outputs. LocateAnything decodes boxes as units in parallel, but only fixed-size 2D primitives. Spec-VLA tolerates action tokens that are close to the target's, but still verifies them one by one, committing only about two to three tokens per pass.

### Key idea

The authors' observation is that visual plans have something text doesn't: a structure you know in advance. Once you know it's a box or a trajectory, how many dimensions, and how many points, the role of every position is fixed before decoding starts.

So you can treat a whole geometric object as one block and guess it in one shot. The more important point: a coordinate should be judged by whether it's spatially accurate, not by whether it matches the big model token for token.

That leads to two changes. In training, the drafter learns directly from annotated ground-truth coordinates, not from the big model's outputs. At inference, any draft with a valid structure is accepted in full, with no token-level verification. The authors call this Flash Primitive Decoding, or FPD.

### Method

Here's the flow in this figure. The model first writes text as usual, say a sub-task description. When it needs a trajectory, it writes a tool call declaring how long the block is, then emits a special token that marks the start of a primitive.

The block length and the primitive type together fix the layout. A box, for example, is six positions: a start marker, four coordinates, and an end marker. In the MolmoSpaces experiments, one trajectory with both 2D and 3D coordinates is fifty-four tokens.

The coordinate format matters too. Their base model gives every coordinate value its own token, zero to one thousand, so one thousand and one in total. Robot 3D coordinates are quantized to one-centimeter resolution. For models that write numbers as ordinary text, each coordinate gets a fixed sign slot plus five digit slots, so every position keeps its role.

The drafter itself is small: five transformer layers, five hundred thirty-seven million trainable parameters. It reads features from five layers of the frozen target VLM, and fills in every masked position of the block in one forward pass. The target is a Qwen3 four-billion-parameter language model with a MoonViT vision encoder.

At inference, each position can only produce legal tokens. Coordinate slots must be numbers, and delimiters sit in fixed places. The draft is then checked for valid format, and boxes also go through a confidence check. If it passes, the whole block is accepted. If not, the big model regenerates it the normal way.

Do the accounting, and a valid primitive costs one drafter pass plus one target pass to update the cache. Autoregressive decoding costs one target pass per coordinate. So a more detailed plan doesn't add sequential steps.

### Results

The experiments are broad: 2D trajectories, boxes, 3D robot trajectories, dense point-motion forecasting, plus two downstream settings, robot manipulation and driving. In most comparisons, the autoregressive baseline and VFlash share the same four-billion-parameter target and differ only in the decoder.

Start with 3D robot trajectories, Table four. Across the InternA1, AgiBot and MolmoBot datasets, and three training variants, VFlash beats autoregressive decoding on all four metrics.

Average displacement error drops by eighteen to sixty-one percent. On AgiBot, the share of waypoints within five centimeters goes from fifty to seventy-three percent, and in the joint 2D-plus-3D setting, from forty to seventy-four percent.

One detail stands out. When the autoregressive model has to output 2D and 3D together, its 3D error is fifteen to fifty-one percent higher than when it outputs 3D alone. VFlash produces both segments in one parallel pass, and its accuracy barely moves.

2D trajectories are in Table two. On both benchmarks, VFlash lowers all six error measures of its autoregressive target, by three to seven percent.

Boxes, in Table three, are more mixed. With a fallback mechanism, mean F1 roughly matches autoregressive decoding, at two point six to two point eight times the speed. It's better at the loose IoU threshold of point five, and worse at the strict point nine five. Accept every draft directly, without fallback, and mean F1 on the crowded Dense200 set drops by about twenty points.

Dense 3D point motion is in Table five, using the released MolmoMotion model as the target. The paper reports roughly twenty-five times faster single-rollout generation: on an H100, HOT3D goes from seventy-nine point eight seconds to three point two one. But accuracy goes both ways: average error on DAVIS falls twenty-seven percent, while final-point error on WorldTrack rises fifteen percent.

Downstream, start with MolmoSpaces robot manipulation, Table six. The same world action model goes from sixty-two point six percent average success in its original form to seventy point two percent when it's conditioned on VFlash plans.

Table seven breaks that down. Swap in autoregressive-generated plans and success is only sixty-one percent. Use image features instead of the trajectory, sixty-three point seven. A 3D-only plan gets sixty-nine point three, and 2D plus 3D is best.

On the LIBERO family, the gain grows with perturbation: zero point eight points on standard LIBERO, six point eight on the perturbed LIBERO-PRO. In driving, with reinforcement learning, NAVSIM reaches a PDMS of ninety point seven, matching Qwen-Drive-1.0, and on Waymo it's within zero point zero five of the best listed result.

Speed is in Figure four. In the MolmoSpaces pipeline on Jetson Thor, each step drops from three thousand five hundred fifty-two milliseconds to seven hundred three. DFlash, for comparison, is one thousand eight hundred twenty-eight. Overall, per-step latency falls two point six to five point two times, almost entirely from decoding, which itself gets twelve to forty-two times faster.

### Comparison

Compared with DFlash and speculative decoding in general, the big difference is giving up losslessness. Speculative decoding guarantees the same output as the big model. VFlash doesn't. It only guarantees valid structure, and it's judged instead on spatial accuracy and usefulness for the downstream action.

Compared with LocateAnything, it extends whole-primitive decoding from fixed-size 2D boxes to variable-length trajectories, joint 2D and 3D outputs, and dense motion. Compared with Spec-VLA, it stops verifying token by token and accepts the whole primitive at once.

My read is that the thing to remember here isn't the speedup, it's the counterintuitive result: a five-layer drafter is more accurate on trajectories than the four-billion-parameter model it sits on. The paper attributes this to the known structure of primitives. I suspect training on ground-truth coordinates also helps, but the paper doesn't separate those two factors.

### Limitations

There's no dedicated limitations section, but the experiments show a few. First, the speed has a price: outputs no longer match the big model. The box results show accuracy drops clearly without fallback, and strict-threshold localization is worse than autoregressive.

Second, the twenty-five-times figure for dense motion is, in the authors' words, a preliminary single-rollout measurement, and its accuracy metrics are mixed. Also, in dense motion, frames are still generated one after another.

Third, in the downstream robot manipulation experiments, each row is one training run and one evaluation run, with no multiple seeds. And the MolmoSpaces gain combines two changes, a new VLM and the added plan; only Table seven isolates the decoder.

Fourth, in driving, VFlash ties or trails the strongest systems rather than beating them. On Waymo, the reinforcement-learning checkpoint was selected on the same validation set that supplies the reward, though the final scores come from a separate test split.

### Future work

The next step the authors name is generating several complementary primitives from the same observations within a fixed latency budget, and seeing whether that improves control.

My read is that speed is one of the main obstacles to using VLMs as a robot's brain. If drawing a trajectory costs almost nothing extra per point, plans can be more detailed and updated more often.

My read is that real robots are the next test. The downstream experiments here are all in simulation or on offline datasets, with no closed-loop tests on physical hardware.
