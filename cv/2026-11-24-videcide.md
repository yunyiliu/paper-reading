## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇南洋理工大学 S-Lab、KlingAI Research 和香港中文大学的论文，叫 ViDecide，意思是教视频扩散模型一步一步地推理和规划。

现在的视频生成模型，画面已经很逼真了，可是论文里，按常规方式训练的模型画推箱子的解题过程，箱子会凭空冒出来。这篇论文认为，问题不在数据不够，而在训练方式本身。

### 问题

先说背景。语言模型用文字一步步推理，视频模型也可以在画面里一帧一帧把解题过程演出来，所以视频模型被看成视觉推理和规划的基础。

但多步推理，视频模型做得很差。论文第一张图里，商用模型 Seedance 2.5 为了摆成目标形状，一次搬起两块积木互换位置，可规则是一次只能动一块。专门用推理数据加强化学习训练过的 VBVR-Pro-RLVR，玩贪吃蛇的时候，蛇没吃东西就变长，身体还分了叉。

作者认为，根子在训练方式和推理需求对不上，有三层。第一，模型一次性生成固定长度的所有帧，不管先后顺序，要走几步也事先定死了。第二，训练重点是画质，主要在低噪声下学细节，可真正的决定是在高噪声下做的。第三，现有的扩散强化学习给整段视频打一个分，几步走错，整段都被惩罚。

### 以前的方法

以前提升视频模型推理能力，主要靠两条路，在大规模推理数据上微调，或者用可以自动验证对错的奖励做强化学习。作者指出，就算直接在目标任务的标准解上微调，这些错误还是会出现。

也有人改生成结构。HDR 先生成粗的关键帧，再逐层补细节，S2PD 先串行去噪，再切换到并行。但它们仍然把一段推理视频当成固定长度的片段来生成。

### 核心想法

这篇的核心想法是，把决策当成视频推理的基本单位，每一个决策，就是画面里一次状态变化。就像语言模型一个 token 一个 token 地写，视频模型应该一步一步地生成状态，并且自己决定什么时候结束。

### 方法

方法分三块。第一块，因果生成加自适应终止。模型每次只生成一小段，这一段以之前所有的画面为条件。另外加一个视觉上的结束帧，带红色边框，作用就像语言模型的结束 token，模型画出它，生成就停止。

每一小段多长，是可调的。规则类小游戏一次生成一个潜空间帧，对应 4 帧画面，机器人任务一个动作要持续很多帧，可以一次生成 8 个潜空间帧，对应 32 帧画面。

第二块，决策导向的训练调度。扩散模型训练时，会随机挑一个加噪强度。噪声小的时候，下一帧大部分已经看得见，模型只是在补细节，噪声大的时候，下一帧被遮住了，模型必须根据规则和历史，推断接下来该发生什么。所以作者把训练的采样往高噪声那一侧推，用一个偏移参数 s 控制，Wan2.2 默认是 5，他们用 10。

第三块，叫纠错微调，Corrective Fine-tuning，简称 CFT。监督训练之后，模型在推理时依赖自己生成的历史，一步走错，整个解就失败了。

CFT 的做法是，先让当前模型在训练题上跑，收集失败的视频，然后从验证器发现失败的位置往前退几步，重新采样，留下成功的续写。失败和成功第一次分开的那一步，叫分歧步。

关键的一招是，把当初生成错误决策的那一份噪声留下来，训练模型从同一份噪声生成成功的那个决策。成功续写的剩余部分，用常规目标继续训练，同时混入专家视频，防止原有能力退化。这个过程还可以重复多轮。

和以前的强化学习比，CFT 不是给整段视频一个分数，而是在出错的那一步给出明确的正确答案，而且这个答案是模型自己生成的，在它自己的分布之内。它是一个回归目标，不需要策略梯度。

### 实验和效果

实验用了三个基准。VideoRLVR 里的 FlowFree 连线和推箱子，每个游戏一万道训练题、一千道测试题。VBVR-MultiStep 里的六个规划任务，比如汉诺塔、滑块拼图、贪吃蛇。还有作者自己搭的 RoboTwin-Plan，在仿真里让双臂机器人搬积木，有三个任务，各两种布局。

主干模型是 Wan2.2-5B，全量微调，另外还从零训练了一个 1.32 亿参数的小模型 DiT-B 做验证。指标是严格成功率，每一步都守规则，并且最后到达目标，才算解对。规则验证器会逐帧读出状态，把整个过程回放一遍。

先看生成方式，结果在表 1。FlowFree 上，逐帧因果生成是 77.9%，传统的双向一次性生成只有 1.1%，最强的结构化方法 S2PD 是 68.1%。零样本的 Seedance 2.5 和 Veo 3.1，在 FlowFree 上都是 0，而且它们用的还是更宽松的判分标准。

图 4 把失败拆开看。双向生成的失败，大部分是违反规则的非法状态变化，逐帧因果生成则很少破坏规则。机器人任务上，一次生成 8 个潜空间帧的分块生成效果最好。

再看训练调度，表 4。在机器人任务上，用默认的偏移 5，成功率只有 33.3%，偏移加到 10，升到 73.9%。从零训练的小模型，不做偏移几乎一道题都解不出。而且在 FlowFree 和推箱子上，不做偏移训练 10 轮，也比不过偏移 10 只训练 5 轮。

最后是纠错微调，表 5。CFT 在原始测试集上，比监督训练的模型再提高 4.1 到 7.8 个点，FlowFree 到 84.4%，推箱子 90.8%，VBVR-MultiStep 82.2%，机器人任务 78.0%。

把三块加起来，就是第一张图右边这组柱子，四个测试集的平均严格成功率，从标准微调的 23.3%，提到 83.8%。

### 和以前方法的对比

和以前的方法比，最强的 S2PD 在这四个测试集上平均是 67.8%，ViDecide 是 83.8%。

后训练方法里，同样起点、同样预算下，Flow-GRPO 和 DiffusionNFT 这两种扩散强化学习几乎没有提升。继续在专家视频上监督训练，也没有稳定提升，在 FlowFree 和 VBVR-MultiStep 上还下降了。

最接近的对手，是只在成功视频上微调的 RFT。它在原始测试集上和 CFT 差得不多，但在更难的推箱子测试上，RFT 是 61.6%，CFT 是 78.2%，而且学得慢很多。图 5 里，训练半个 epoch，CFT 在难推箱子上就提高了 20 多个点。

那噪声映射到底有没有用。作者做了对照，用同样的成功续写，但换成新的随机噪声训练。CFT 在每个原始测试集上都更好，最多高 7.3 个点。在推箱子的 149 个分歧点上，用失败那一份噪声，CFT 有 57.0% 的情况能走出成功的那一步，对照组是 48.3%。

还有一个对比很有意思。规则游戏可以用精确求解器给出每一道失败题的正确解，但用求解器的解来训练，FlowFree 只到 48.2%，CFT 是 84.4%。作者的解释是，求解器的解不在模型自己的分布里，用它们训练反而会干扰生成本身。

泛化测试在表 7。推箱子训练时最多 3 个箱子，测试 4 个和 5 个，FlowFree 训练最多 8 乘 8，测试 9 乘 9。双向生成几乎全军覆没，CFT 分别做到 74%、54% 和 38%。在 9 乘 9 的 FlowFree 上，解题过程比所有训练视频都长，模型还是在解完之后自己停了下来。

速度方面，按附录，ViDecide 每个视频要 3.9 到 11.5 秒，双向生成是 2.7 到 3.1 秒，S2PD 是 13.2 到 14.6 秒。

我的判断是，这篇最有说服力的地方在于实验设计，同一个主干、同样的数据和训练预算，每次只换一个设计，三块改动各自的贡献都能看清楚。

不过附录里有一组数据值得一看。作者从 FlowFree 和推箱子的每个测试集里各抽 50 道题，交给语言模型，让它用文字给出走法，Claude Opus 5.5 在 FlowFree 两组测试上都是 100%，ViDecide 是 82% 和 66%，开源的 Qwen3.8-27B 则比 ViDecide 低 14 到 54 个点。作者的解释是，视频模型要把每一个中间状态都画出来，任何一帧画错都算失败。我的判断是，单论这类解谜，像 Claude Opus 5.5 这样的前沿语言模型用文字作答目前明显更好，视频模型的价值，更多在于能把物理过程本身画出来。

### 局限

作者自己列了三条局限。第一，为了在算力预算内保持对照干净，每个基准单独训练一个模型，还没有在更大、更杂的数据上训练一个通用模型。

第二，CFT 依赖规则验证器逐步定位错误。规则明确的任务可以做验证器，开放式任务就很难。

第三，机器人这部分，是在仿真里把生成的计划当视频来评，没有转成真实机器人的动作。另外正文提到，在新布局上，CFT 因为用自己生成的视频训练，画面瑕疵更多，成功率反而比纠错微调之前略低。

### 未来方向

作者计划扩展到更广的推理数据，用学习出来的验证器或者视觉语言模型来判分，并做真实机器人的规划，比如用逆动力学模型把视频转成动作。

我的判断是，这篇把语言模型推理的那套做法，逐步生成、自己决定何时停、从自己的错误里学，系统地搬到了视频扩散模型上，每一步都有对照实验。

我的判断是，接下来要看两件事，一是把多种任务混在一起训练一个模型，这些收益还在不在，二是生成的计划能不能真正变成机器人的动作。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from S-Lab at Nanyang Technological University, KlingAI Research, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong. It's called ViDecide, and the goal is to teach video diffusion models to reason and plan step by step.

Video generators can make strikingly realistic footage. But in this paper, when a conventionally trained model shows how to solve a Sokoban puzzle, boxes appear out of nowhere. This paper argues the problem isn't a lack of data. It's the way these models are trained.

### Problem

Some context. Language models reason step by step in words. Video models could, in principle, do the same in pixels, playing out a solution frame by frame. That's why many people see video models as a foundation for visual reasoning and planning.

In practice, they're bad at multi-step reasoning. In the paper's first figure, the commercial model Seedance 2.5 reaches a target block arrangement by lifting two blocks and swapping them, when the rule says move one block at a time. And VBVR-Pro-RLVR, a model trained on reasoning data with reinforcement learning, plays Snake by growing without eating, until its body forks.

The authors trace this to a mismatch between how the models are trained and what reasoning needs, at three levels. First, the model generates every frame of a fixed-length clip at once, ignoring the order of steps and fixing the number of steps in advance. Second, training is tuned for visual quality, mostly at low noise, while the actual decisions are made at high noise. Third, existing diffusion reinforcement learning gives the whole video one score, so a few wrong moves penalize everything.

### Prior work

Previous work tried two main routes: fine-tuning on large reasoning datasets, and reinforcement learning with rewards that can be checked automatically. The authors point out that the failures persist even when a model is fine-tuned on expert solutions of the target tasks.

Others changed the generation structure. HDR denoises a hierarchy of keyframes from coarse to fine, and S2PD switches from serial to parallel denoising. But both still treat a reasoning video as a fixed-length clip.

### Key idea

The key idea here is to make the decision the basic unit of video reasoning, where each decision is one change of state on screen. Just as a language model writes one token at a time, a video model should generate one step at a time, and decide for itself when it's done.

### Method

The method has three parts. Part one is causal generation with adaptive termination. The model generates one short segment at a time, each conditioned on everything before it. It also learns a visual end-of-sequence frame, marked with a red border, which works like the end token in a language model. Once the model draws it, generation stops.

The segment length is a knob. For rule-based puzzles, the model generates one latent frame at a time, which is four video frames. For robot tasks, where a single move spans many frames, it can generate eight latent frames at a time, which is thirty-two video frames.

Part two is a decision-focused training schedule. Diffusion training picks a random noise level for each example. At low noise, most of the next frame is already visible, so the model is just filling in detail. At high noise, the next frame is hidden, and the model has to work out what should happen from the rules and the history. So the authors push training toward high noise with a shift parameter, s. Wan2.2's default is five. They use ten.

Part three is Corrective Fine-tuning, or CFT. After supervised training, the model has to build on its own generated history at inference time, so one wrong step sinks the whole solution.

Here's how CFT works. Run the current model on training puzzles and collect the failures. Then back up a few steps from where the verifier caught the failure, resample, and keep the continuations that succeed. The first step where the failed and successful versions part ways is called the divergence step.

The key trick is to keep the exact noise that produced the bad decision, and train the model to turn that same noise into the successful decision. The rest of the successful continuation is trained with the usual objective, and expert videos are mixed in so the model doesn't lose what it already knows. The whole procedure can be repeated for several rounds.

Compared with reinforcement learning, CFT doesn't hand the whole video a single score. It gives an explicit correct answer at the step that went wrong, and that answer comes from the model itself, so it stays within the model's own distribution. It's a regression objective, with no policy gradient.

### Results

There are three benchmarks. From VideoRLVR, the FlowFree line-connecting game and Sokoban, each with ten thousand training and one thousand test puzzles. From VBVR-MultiStep, six planning tasks such as Tower of Hanoi, sliding puzzles and Snake. And RoboTwin-Plan, which the authors built: a simulated dual-arm robot moving blocks, with three tasks in two layouts each.

The main backbone is Wan2.2-5B, fully fine-tuned, and they also train a one hundred thirty-two million parameter DiT-B from scratch as a check. The metric is strict success rate: a video counts only if every step obeys the rules and it ends at the goal. A rule verifier reads the state of every frame and replays the whole solution.

First, the generation paradigm, in Table 1. On FlowFree, frame-by-frame causal generation reaches seventy-seven point nine percent. Standard bidirectional generation, all frames at once, gets one point one, and the strongest structured method, S2PD, sixty-eight point one. Zero-shot, Seedance 2.5 and Veo 3.1 both score zero on FlowFree, and that's under a more lenient grading standard.

Figure 4 breaks down the failures. Most of bidirectional generation's failures are invalid transitions that break the rules. Frame-by-frame causal generation rarely breaks them. On the robot tasks, chunks of eight latent frames work best.

Next, the training schedule, in Table 4. On the robot tasks, the default shift of five gives thirty-three point three percent. A shift of ten gives seventy-three point nine. The small model trained from scratch solves almost nothing without a shift. And on FlowFree and Sokoban, training without the shift for ten epochs still falls short of a shift of ten trained for only five.

Then Corrective Fine-tuning, in Table 5. On the original test sets, CFT adds four point one to seven point eight points on top of the supervised model: eighty-four point four on FlowFree, ninety point eight on Sokoban, eighty-two point two on VBVR-MultiStep, and seventy-eight on the robot tasks.

Put the three parts together and you get the bars on the right of Figure 1. Averaged over four test sets, strict success goes from twenty-three point three percent with standard fine-tuning to eighty-three point eight.

### Comparison

Against prior methods, the strongest one, S2PD, averages sixty-seven point eight percent on those four test sets. ViDecide averages eighty-three point eight.

Among post-training methods, starting from the same model with the same budget, the two diffusion reinforcement learning methods, Flow-GRPO and DiffusionNFT, bring little or no gain. Continued supervised training on expert videos doesn't help consistently either, and actually drops on FlowFree and VBVR-MultiStep.

The closest competitor is RFT, which fine-tunes only on the model's successful videos. It comes close on the original test sets, but on the harder Sokoban set it scores sixty-one point six against CFT's seventy-eight point two, and it learns much more slowly. In Figure 5, within half an epoch, CFT is already up more than twenty points on hard Sokoban.

Does the noise trick actually matter? The control uses the same successful continuations but trains with fresh random noise. CFT beats it on every original test set, by up to seven point three points. At one hundred forty-nine divergence points in Sokoban, the noise from the failed rollout now produces the successful move fifty-seven percent of the time after CFT, against forty-eight point three for the control.

One more comparison stands out. For rule-based games, an exact solver can supply the right answer for every failure. But training on solver corrections only reaches forty-eight point two percent on FlowFree, against eighty-four point four for CFT. The authors' explanation is that solver solutions lie outside the model's own distribution, so fitting them disturbs the generation itself.

Generalization is in Table 7. Sokoban training used at most three boxes; the test uses four and five. FlowFree training went up to eight by eight; the test uses nine by nine. Bidirectional generation solves almost none. CFT reaches seventy-four, fifty-four and thirty-eight percent. On nine by nine FlowFree, the solutions run longer than any training video, and the model still stops right after solving the board.

On speed, the appendix reports that ViDecide takes three point nine to eleven point five seconds per video, against two point seven to three point one for bidirectional generation and thirteen point two to fourteen point six for S2PD.

My read is that the paper's strongest asset is its experimental design. Same backbone, same data, same training budget, one design change at a time, so you can see what each of the three parts contributes.

But one appendix table is worth a look. The authors took fifty puzzles from each FlowFree and Sokoban test set and gave them to language models, which answer with moves as text. Claude Opus 5.5 scores one hundred percent on both FlowFree sets, where ViDecide gets eighty-two and sixty-six, while ViDecide beats the open Qwen3.8-27B by fourteen to fifty-four points. The authors' explanation is that a video model has to draw every intermediate state, so one bad frame fails the whole video. My read is that for puzzles like these, a frontier language model like Claude Opus 5.5 answering in text is clearly ahead right now, and the case for video models rests more on their ability to depict the physical process itself.

### Limitations

The authors list three limitations. First, to keep comparisons clean within their compute budget, they train one model per benchmark. They haven't yet trained a single general model on larger, more varied data.

Second, CFT relies on a rule verifier to locate mistakes step by step. That's feasible for tasks with explicit rules, and much harder for open-ended ones.

Third, the robot results are generated plans judged as videos in simulation, not converted into real robot actions. Separately, the results section notes that on new layouts, CFT's training on its own generated videos causes more visual defects, which leaves its success rate slightly below the model before Corrective Fine-tuning.

### Future work

Next, the authors plan to move to broader reasoning data, to learned verifiers or vision-language judges, and to real-world robot planning, for example converting videos into actions with an inverse dynamics model.

My read is that what this paper does is carry the playbook of language-model reasoning, generate step by step, decide when to stop, learn from your own mistakes, over to video diffusion, systematically, with a controlled experiment behind each step.

My read is that two things are worth watching next. Whether these gains survive when one model is trained on many tasks at once, and whether the generated plans can actually become robot actions.
