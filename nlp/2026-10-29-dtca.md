## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇浙江大学的论文，合作方还有新加坡国立大学和 SAP，名字叫 DTCA，意思是去噪过程中的 token 功劳分配。

它研究的是扩散语言模型的强化学习。简单说，模型做对一道数学题，拿到一个奖励，这个奖励该怎么分给答案里的每一个 token。

### 问题

先说什么是扩散语言模型。我们常用的大模型是自回归的，从左到右一个 token 一个 token 往外写。扩散语言模型不一样，它先把整段回答都盖住，变成一排空格，然后分很多步，每一步填上几个位置，直到全部填完。

论文把某一步填上一个 token 叫做提交，英文是 commitment。一个 token 一旦提交，后面的步骤都能看到它，它会影响剩下那些空格该填什么。

最近有不少工作用强化学习训练这类模型做推理。常见做法是，同一道题采样几个回答，按最终答案对不对打分，再看每个回答比同组其他回答好多少，这就是 GRPO 一类的方法。

问题在于，奖励只有一个，是给整段回答的。哪个 token 在去噪过程中帮了忙，哪个只是凑数，光看最终奖励说不清楚，这就是功劳分配，也叫 credit assignment 的问题。

### 以前的方法

论文对比了一批扩散模型的强化学习方法，比如 d1、ESPO、TraceRL、Coupled-GRPO 和 RLDF。作者的概括是，它们主要在改优化目标、改似然估计，或者利用去噪轨迹和中间状态。

其中 RLDF 是这篇最直接的基础。它不在每一个去噪步骤上训练，而是从一次生成里挑一部分步骤来训练。DTCA 沿用了这个框架，改的是挑哪些步骤，以及一步之内的 token 怎么分功劳。

作者认为，在去噪过程中给单个 token 细粒度地分功劳，这件事还没有被充分研究。自回归模型上已经有不少 token 级的方法，但它们是按从左到右的生成方式设计的。

### 核心想法

作者的想法是，一个 token 的功劳，可以看它提交之后，剩下那些空格是不是变得更好猜了。

但直接逐个 token 去算，有两个麻烦。一步里会同时提交好几个 token，很难把各自的作用分开，而且同一类估计在不同回答之间波动很大。

所以他们换了一个粒度，按词性分组，在组内取平均，就是名词、动词、数词、标点这些类别。

这张图是他们在 GSM8K 小学数学题上做的初步分析。左边是不同词性在各个去噪步骤的提交比例，名词一直比较高，动词一开始很高，后面明显下降。右边是提交之后带来的置信度提升，动词最大，其次是名词和专有名词，限定词和介词比较小。

作者据此认为，词性能区分不同的去噪行为，适合拿来分组估计功劳。

### 方法

方法分三块，这张总览图画得很清楚，先估计功劳，再分配功劳，最后按熵做校准。

第一块，估计功劳。对每一个提交的 token，看它提交前后，剩下那些空格的最大预测概率加起来变了多少。变高了，就记为它的贡献，变低了，就记为零。

这一步要为每个 token 额外跑前向计算，放在训练里实时做太贵。所以 DTCA 在强化学习开始之前，先离线跑一批去噪过程，每一步提交两个 token，逐个评估，然后按词性求平均。

词性用 spaCy 来标注，一共 18 类。每一类的平均贡献，再线性映射到 0.8 到 1.2 之间，作为这个词性的权重。作者说，限制范围是为了不让功劳缩放得太夸张。训练过程中，这张权重表固定不变。

第二块，用权重挑去噪步骤。每一步打一个分，就是这一步提交的每个 token，用词性权重乘上一减去它提交时的置信度，再加起来。提交得越没把握，词性权重越高，这一步的分数就越高。

然后按分数从高到低选，直到累计分数达到总分的百分之七十，选出的步数限制在 2 到 32 之间。所以每个回答选多少步，是自适应的。

第三块，在选中的步骤里分功劳。每个 token 的功劳系数，是它词性的权重，除以这一步所有训练目标权重的平均值。这样一步之内平均还是 1，整体奖励信号的大小不变，只是在 token 之间重新分配。

最后是熵校准。熵可以理解成模型在这个位置有多犹豫。这个模块借鉴了 OPEFO 方法，对每个 token 估计，这次更新会让熵升高，还是降低。

在每个回答内部，把让熵升高的更新和让熵降低的更新分开算总量，得到一个平衡系数，限制在正负 0.95 之间。如果降熵的一方占优，就放大升熵的更新，减弱降熵的更新，反过来也一样。

校准之后，再把这个回答的总功劳恢复到原来的大小。附录按一阶近似做了估算，显示这样处理后，预估的升熵和降熵这两股力量基本能相互抵消。

### 实验和效果

实验设置。起点是 LLaDA-8B-Instruct，一个 8B 规模的扩散语言模型，不做额外的监督微调，直接在 MATH 训练集上做强化学习。

每道题采样 4 个回答，训练时回答最长 256 个 token，奖励看数学答案对不对。评测用 MATH500 和 GSM8K，只看最终答案，生成长度分别设成 128、256 和 512。

对比的基线，分数都是作者在同一套评测设置下自己复现的，包括原始模型、LLaDA-1.5、一个监督微调版本，以及刚才说的五个强化学习方法。

结果在表 1。两个基准、三种长度，一共六个设置，DTCA 都是最高分。

和原始模型比，MATH500 上分别提高 9.0、6.8 和 4.8 个百分点，GSM8K 上提高 5.3、2.1 和 2.5 个百分点。比如 MATH500 在 128 长度下，从 30.8 提到 39.8。

作者指出，两个基准上提升最大的，都是最短的 128 长度，所以在去噪计算有限的时候，这个方法收益更明显。

消融实验也在表 1。把按词性分功劳换成平均分配，在 256 长度下，MATH500 掉 3.8 个点，GSM8K 掉 2.4 个点。去掉熵校准，在 512 长度下掉得更多，MATH500 掉 4.2 个点，GSM8K 掉 3.5 个点。

训练曲线在这张图。平均分配的版本，奖励曲线和完整版差不多，但梯度一直更大。去掉熵校准的版本，KL 散度涨得更快，也就是离原始模型偏得更快，训练后期奖励也更低。

附录还直接对比了 DTCA 和 RLDF 的训练过程。在最后 100 次更新里，DTCA 的平均奖励是 0.537，RLDF 是 0.482，同时 DTCA 的熵更高，KL 散度是 0.36，RLDF 是 0.89。

作者还看了训练后的模型怎么去噪。和原始模型比，DTCA 在各个阶段都少提交数字，多提交标点。动词的提交比例变化不大，但提交时更有把握。

### 和以前方法的对比

按表 1 算，和每一列里最强的对比方法比，DTCA 领先 1.4 到 2.5 个百分点。比如 MATH500 在 512 长度下，RLDF 是 42.6，DTCA 是 44.0。

挑步骤这一环，附录表 2 和 RLDF 做了对比。在挑出的步骤数量相同时，DTCA 选中的步骤覆盖了百分之 40.59 的低置信度部分，RLDF 只有百分之 19.93。

我的判断是，表 1 里有一个细节值得注意。单独去掉任何一块，DTCA 都会在一些设置下不如 RLDF。比如 MATH500 在 512 长度下，两个消融版本是 39.8 和 41.2，都低于 RLDF 的 42.6。也就是说，要在所有设置上都超过 RLDF，需要几块合在一起。

我的判断是，这些领先大多在一到两个百分点，论文没有报告多次训练的波动范围，评测也是每道题只生成一次。差距不算大，还需要更多重复实验才能确认它稳定。

### 局限

作者自己写了几条局限。第一，方法依赖词性标注和对齐的质量，对不上词性的 token，只能归到其他类。

第二，用剩下空格的置信度提升来定义贡献，作者认为这个定义还需要更严格的理论表述。

第三，实验只做了数学推理，只用了 LLaDA-8B-Instruct 一个模型。换到代码生成这类任务，词性分组可能要调整，在其他扩散模型架构上，也还需要验证。

### 未来方向

未来方向，作者提到更准确的 token 级标注，更有理论依据的贡献估计，以及扩展到代码和更多扩散模型。论文给出了代码仓库的地址。

我的判断是，这篇最有意思的地方，是用扩散模型自己的生成过程来分功劳。一个 token 填进去之后，别的空格有没有变好猜，这是自回归模型没有的信号。

我的判断是，词性只是很粗的分组，同样是数字，有的是关键的中间结果，有的只是从题目里抄过来的。下一步值得看的，是能不能在不多花太多计算的前提下，做到按具体位置的更细估计。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper is from Zhejiang University, with co-authors at the National University of Singapore and SAP. It's called DTCA, short for Denoising Token Credit Assignment.

It's about reinforcement learning for diffusion language models. Put simply: when the model solves a math problem and gets one reward, how should that reward be shared out among the tokens in its answer?

### Problem

First, what's a diffusion language model? The models most of us use are autoregressive. They write left to right, one token at a time. A diffusion language model starts with the whole answer masked out, a row of blanks, and fills in a few positions per step until nothing is left.

The paper calls filling in a token at some step a commitment. Once a token is committed, every later step can see it, so it shapes what goes into the remaining blanks.

A number of recent papers train these models for reasoning with reinforcement learning. The usual recipe: sample several answers to the same problem, score each by whether the final answer is right, and measure how much better each answer did than the rest of its group. That's the GRPO family.

The catch is that there's a single reward for the whole answer. Which tokens actually helped during denoising, and which were just along for the ride? The final reward can't tell you. That's the credit assignment problem.

### Prior work

The paper compares against a set of diffusion RL methods: d1, ESPO, TraceRL, Coupled-GRPO and RLDF. In the authors' summary, these mostly change the optimization objective, change how likelihood is estimated, or make use of denoising trajectories and intermediate states.

RLDF is the direct starting point here. Instead of training on every denoising step, it trains on a subset of steps picked from each generation. DTCA keeps that framework and changes two things: which steps get picked, and how credit is split among the tokens within a step.

The authors argue that fine-grained, per-token credit during denoising is still underexplored. There's plenty of token-level credit work for autoregressive models, but it was designed around left-to-right generation.

### Key idea

The core idea: judge a token's contribution by whether, once it's committed, the remaining blanks become easier to predict.

Doing that token by token runs into two problems. Several tokens are committed in the same step, so their individual effects are hard to separate. And the estimates swing a lot from one sampled answer to the next.

So they change the granularity. They group tokens by part of speech, nouns, verbs, numerals, punctuation and so on, and average within each group.

This figure is their preliminary analysis on GSM8K, the grade-school math set. On the left, the share of commitments each part of speech makes across denoising steps: nouns stay fairly high, while verbs start high and then drop off. On the right, the confidence gain after a commitment: verbs give the largest gain, then nouns and proper nouns, while determiners and adpositions give less.

From this, the authors conclude that parts of speech capture different denoising behavior, which makes them a useful way to group tokens when estimating credit.

### Method

The method has three parts, and the overview figure lays them out: estimate contributions, assign credit, then calibrate by entropy.

Part one, estimating contributions. For each committed token, compare the remaining blanks before and after it goes in, summing the top predicted probability at each blank. If that sum goes up, the increase is the token's contribution. If it goes down, the contribution is zero.

That needs extra forward passes for every token, which is too expensive to do live during training. So DTCA runs a batch of denoising passes offline, before reinforcement learning starts. Two tokens are committed per step, each is scored separately, and the scores are averaged by part of speech.

Parts of speech come from spaCy, eighteen categories in all. Each category's average contribution is mapped linearly into a range from zero point eight to one point two, and that's the category's weight. The authors say the narrow range keeps credit from being scaled too aggressively. The weight table stays fixed for the whole of training.

Part two, using the weights to pick denoising steps. Each step gets a score: for every token committed at that step, multiply its part-of-speech weight by one minus its confidence when it was committed, and add those up. Less confident commitments and higher-weight categories push a step's score up.

Steps are then taken from the highest score down until they cover seventy percent of the total, with between two and thirty-two steps chosen. So the number of steps adapts to each answer.

Part three, assigning credit inside the chosen steps. A token's credit factor is its category weight divided by the average weight of all training targets in that step. The average stays at one, so the overall size of the learning signal doesn't change. It just gets redistributed among tokens.

Last comes entropy calibration. Think of entropy as how undecided the model is at a position. This module borrows from a method called OPEFO and estimates, for each token, whether this update will push entropy up or down.

Within each answer, the entropy-raising and entropy-lowering updates are totaled separately, giving a balancing coefficient clipped between minus and plus zero point nine five. If the entropy-lowering side dominates, the entropy-raising updates are scaled up and the entropy-lowering ones scaled down, and vice versa.

After calibration, the answer's total credit is restored to its original size. A first-order estimate in the appendix shows that, with this in place, the predicted entropy increase and decrease nearly cancel out.

### Results

The setup. Training starts from LLaDA-8B-Instruct, an eight-billion-parameter diffusion language model, with no extra supervised fine-tuning, and runs reinforcement learning on the MATH training set.

Each problem gets four sampled answers, training answers are capped at two hundred fifty-six tokens, and the reward is whether the math is correct. Evaluation is on MATH500 and GSM8K, final answer only, at generation lengths of one twenty-eight, two fifty-six and five twelve.

All the baseline scores are the authors' own reproductions under one evaluation protocol: the original model, LLaDA-1.5, a supervised fine-tuning variant, and the five RL methods I mentioned.

The results are in Table 1. Across two benchmarks and three lengths, six settings in all, DTCA has the top score every time.

Against the original model, MATH500 goes up by nine point zero, six point eight and four point eight percentage points, and GSM8K by five point three, two point one and two point five. At length one twenty-eight on MATH500, for example, accuracy rises from thirty point eight to thirty-nine point eight.

The authors point out that the gains are largest at the shortest length, so the method helps most when denoising compute is tight.

The ablations are in Table 1 too. Swap the part-of-speech credit for uniform credit, and at length two fifty-six MATH500 drops three point eight points and GSM8K drops two point four. Remove entropy calibration, and the drop is bigger at length five twelve: four point two on MATH500 and three point five on GSM8K.

The training curves are in this figure. With uniform credit, reward tracks the full method closely, but gradient norms stay larger throughout. Without entropy calibration, KL divergence climbs faster, meaning the model drifts from where it started more quickly, and late-stage reward is lower.

The appendix also compares DTCA and RLDF training head to head. Over the last hundred updates, DTCA's average reward is zero point five three seven against zero point four eight two for RLDF. DTCA also keeps higher entropy, and its KL divergence is zero point three six, against zero point eight nine.

The authors also looked at how the trained model denoises. Compared with the original model, DTCA commits fewer numerals and more punctuation at every stage. Verbs are committed about as often as before, but with more confidence.

### Comparison

Working from Table 1, DTCA leads the strongest baseline in each column by between one point four and two point five percentage points. On MATH500 at length five twelve, for instance, RLDF scores forty-two point six and DTCA forty-four point zero.

For step selection, Table 2 in the appendix compares against RLDF. When both pick the same number of steps, DTCA's steps cover forty point five nine percent of the low-confidence mass, against nineteen point nine three percent for RLDF.

My read is that one detail in Table 1 deserves attention. Take away either piece and DTCA falls behind RLDF in at least some settings. On MATH500 at length five twelve, the two ablations score thirty-nine point eight and forty-one point two, both under RLDF's forty-two point six. So beating RLDF everywhere takes the pieces working together.

My read is also that most of these leads are one to two points, the paper reports no spread across repeated training runs, and evaluation generates just one answer per question. The margins are modest, and repeated runs would be needed to show they hold up.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, the method depends on the quality of part-of-speech tagging and alignment. Tokens that can't be matched to a tag get lumped into a catch-all category.

Second, the authors say that defining contribution as the confidence gain on the remaining blanks still calls for a more principled theoretical formulation.

Third, the experiments cover only math reasoning and only one model, LLaDA-8B-Instruct. Tasks like code generation may need a different token grouping, and other diffusion architectures still need testing.

### Future work

Looking ahead, the authors point to more accurate token-level labeling, better-grounded contribution estimates, and extending the method to code and to more diffusion models. The paper gives a link to its code repository.

My read is that the most interesting move here is using the diffusion model's own generation process to assign credit. Whether the other blanks get easier to fill once a token is in place is a signal autoregressive models simply don't have.

My read is that part of speech is a very coarse grouping, though. One number might be a key intermediate result, another just copied from the question. The thing to watch is whether someone can get finer, position-level estimates without paying much more compute.
