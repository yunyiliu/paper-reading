## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇理海大学、英属哥伦比亚大学和 Vector 研究所的论文，名字叫 LEV，副标题是，用循环的决策模型，想得又快又深。

一句话说，它让一个专门做选择题的模型，遇到难题时把自己的同一组层多跑几遍，而不是多写几段推理文字。

### 问题

先说决策模型，比如闭源的 Jev 和它的开源复现 Kev。给它一段材料和一道有固定选项的题，它不生成文字，一次前向计算就给每个选项一个概率，几十毫秒出结果。

论文把它比作心理学里的系统一，也就是快速的直觉反应。问题在于，它的深度在训练时就定死了，简单题和难题花一样多的计算。

作者先做了一个对照。在 JevBench 公开的 231 道题上，40 亿参数的 Kev-4B 比 8 亿参数的 Kev-0.8B 多答对 28 道，而且全部来自中等和困难两档，简单题两个模型本来就全对。

更关键的是，两个模型结果不同的题只有 42 道。小模型自己的置信度就能找到它们，把它最没把握的一半交给大模型，效果几乎追平全用大模型。所以，多出来的计算只在少数题上有用，便宜的第一遍就能把这些题挑出来。

### 以前的方法

现有的加计算办法行不行。第一种是级联，小模型没把握就转给大模型。但 Kev-0.8B 中位延迟已经是 79 毫秒，Kev-4B 是 108 毫秒，被转走的题要从头再读一遍，比直接用大模型还慢，还得多部署一个模型。

第二种是先写推理再作答。论文引用 JevBench 公布的结果，两个开源服务开了推理，困难题准确率高 9 到 13 个点，但困难题的中位耗时变成三到四倍，简单题也要付这个代价。

第三种是循环，也就是把同一组层重复跑几遍，不加参数就能加深度。作者在 Qwen3.5-0.8B 上，把中间 8 层改成循环，再按 Kev 的方法训练，准确率变化不到半个点。控制循环的门几乎一直停在零，等于循环根本没打开。

### 核心想法

作者的想法是，与其事后加循环，不如直接用预训练时就是循环结构的语言模型做底座，比如 Ouro 和 Nanbeige4.2，它们从一开始就学会了用循环。

在这个底座上，每跑完一步循环，都让同一个读出头去读一次选项，所有步一起训练。这样一个模型文件，就能在任意深度作答，难题多跑几步，简单题跑一步就走，论文把它叫做系统 1.5。

### 方法

具体结构看这张图。一个请求里，一段材料加上几个问题和各自的选项，打包成一个序列。同一组解码层跑 R 遍，图里 R 等于 4。

每一步结束，一个共享的指针头读取每个选项和决策标记位置的状态，给出各个选项的概率。训练时把每一步的损失取平均，所以每一步都被训练成一个合格的决策模型。

好处是，只跑到第 k 步，就只算前 k 步，成本大约是跑满的 R 分之 k。再多跑一步，是接着上一步的隐藏状态往下算，不用把输入重新读一遍。

关键是什么时候停。每一步之后，一个很小的停止器判断哪些题还要继续。它是每个深度一个逻辑回归，输入是最近两步读数的几个特征，比如最高概率、前两名的差距、答案变没变。

它预测的不是答案对不对，而是当前答案和最深一步的答案是否一致。阈值的定法是，在保证至少 98% 的最终答案和最深答案一致的前提下，选最省计算的那个。

因为目标是模型自己最深一步的答案，拟合停止器和阈值都不需要标注，部署时可以直接用自己线上没有标签的请求来拟合。

为什么不用常见的置信度阈值。作者发现，浅层读数在新数据上过度自信。LEV-2.6B 在第一步就停下的题，在校准集上正确率是 94.8%，换到新来源的 Kev-Transfer 上只有 78.9%，置信度却差不多。

而且置信度能区分答对和答错，AUROC 在 0.67 到 0.83，但要找出哪些错题多跑几步能改对，AUROC 只有 0.46 到 0.61，而 0.5 就相当于随机猜。

### 实验和效果

实验用了三个循环底座，Ouro 的 14 亿和 26 亿参数版本各跑 4 步，Nanbeige4.2 的 30 亿版本跑 2 步。对照组是 17 亿到 40 亿参数的三个普通非循环 Qwen 模型，数据和训练方法完全相同。

所有模型都用 LoRA 在同样的 31329 条数据上训练一轮，标签都不来自 Jev，所以不是从对手那里蒸馏来的。评测用作者自建、在任何模型读过之前就冻结的 LevBench，由 14 个训练中没出现过的数据集组成的 Kev-Breadth，以及 JevBench 的 231 道公开题。

先看表 1。同一套权重，多跑几步，准确率就往上涨。论文报告，LEV-2.6B 在 JevBench 上，第一步是 63.2%，跑满四步是 76.2%，提高了 13 个点。

收益递减很明显。第一个多出来的步骤拿走了大部分提升，LEV-2.6B 在两个留出测试集上的平均分，从 51.3% 涨到 62.0%，跑满四步是 65.7%，Ouro 模型的第四步最多只多一个点。

而且离训练数据越远，深度越有用。在训练来源上，从一步到四步大约涨 7 个点，在四个离训练数据更远的测试集上涨 9.0 到 19.5 个点。

提升主要来自难题。在 JevBench 的 111 道难题里，LEV-2.6B 从一步到四步净多答对 19 道，其中 29 道由错变对，10 道由对变错，简单题几乎每一步都全对。

论文开头这张图显示，停止器在简单题上平均只跑 1.2 步，中等题 2.4 步，难题 3.0 步，而它并没有看到题目难度。

深度也会把答案改坏。在 LevBench 上，第一个多出来的步骤改对了 20.4% 的题，也改错了 8.6%。还有 12.6% 的题，浅层答对、最深一步反而答错，停止器现在的目标救不回这些题。

按需深度的效果在表 2。在两个留出测试集上，停止器只用了跑满时 76% 到 85% 的计算，准确率和跑满相比差不到一个点。

作者还留了一个锁定测试集，在训练方法和停止器都定下来以后只读一次。结果一致，按需深度相比最深一步最多少 1.0 个点。

拟合停止器需要的无标签数据也不多，每份 500 道题，LEV-2.6B 就和用全部数据拟合只差 0.2 个点。

训练上，所有深度一起训练，和单独为某个深度训练的模型相比，第二步和第四步打平，第一步最多落后 1.3 个点。而只训练最后一步，浅层读数就基本没法用。

### 和以前方法的对比

同样延迟下和普通模型比，LEV-3B 跑一步，在留出平均分上比 Qwen3-1.7B 高 4.0 个点，延迟是 23 毫秒对 22 毫秒。LEV-2.6B 跑两步，比 Qwen3.5-2B 高 3.1 个点，101 毫秒对 105 毫秒。不过最小的 LEV-1.4B，并没有超过 Qwen3-1.7B。

和级联比，在 39 到 46 毫秒这个区间，按需深度的 LEV-1.4B 和 LEV-3B，比唯一能做到这个延迟的级联高 7.0 到 8.8 个点，比最好的普通方案高 5.4 到 7.5 个点。但到了 150 毫秒左右，LEV-2.6B 并不比单独用 Kev-4B 好。

但要看清楚，作者自己也说，优势在延迟，不在计算量。部分原因是普通模型在 22 到 79 毫秒之间正好没有可选的，而按浮点运算量对齐，每个 LEV 模型都持平或不如普通方案。

同样，LEV-3B 在 JevBench 上和 Qwen3.5-4B 打平，LEV-2.6B 和 Kev-4B 打平，参数更少，运算量却没少，因为每一步循环都要跑一遍整个底座。

和闭源的 Jev 比，差距还很大。Jev 1.13.0 在 JevBench 上是 86.6%，LEV 最好的是 76.2%。困难题上的校准误差也比 Kev 和 Jev 差，作者认为原因在他们单阶段的训练方法，而不是循环本身。

我的判断是，对做线上分类、审核这类高并发服务的团队，一个模型文件覆盖几个速度档位、按题权衡延迟和准确率，很实用，不过目前最明显的优势在 50 毫秒以内。

### 局限

作者列了几条局限。第一，只测了 14 亿到 30 亿参数的循环底座，因为公开的只有这么大，更大的模型效果如何还不知道。

第二，JevBench 只用了 231 道公开题，还需要更多决策类基准来补充。

第三，省下的计算是按循环步数算的，要变成真实的时间，需要能把不同深度的请求一起批处理的服务系统。延迟也是一次只跑一个请求测的，所有系统都没做融合算子之类的优化。

### 未来方向

作者提了两个方向。一是设计一个新的停止目标，把那些浅层答对、深层答错的题也救回来，这样准确率有可能超过跑满。论文给了一个理想上限，如果用正确答案让每道题停在最早答对的那一步，在调参集上能到 85.9%，成本只有跑满的 0.34，而跑满是 77.4%。

二是换更大的循环底座，看难题上的收益会不会随规模变大。代码和模型，论文给了 GitHub 和 Hugging Face 的链接。

我的判断是，这篇的价值在于证明了，对选择题式的决策模型，循环底座加上不需要标签的停止器，可以不生成一个字，就按题分配计算。

我的判断是，也要看到，在 JevBench 上，它的绝对准确率离闭源的 Jev 还有十个点的差距，按运算量算，也不比普通模型更划算。能不能落地，要看循环底座能不能做大，服务系统能不能把省下的步数变成省下的时间。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Lehigh University, the University of British Columbia and the Vector Institute, and it's called LEV, subtitled Thinking Fast and Deep with Looped Decision Models.

The short version: when a model that only answers multiple-choice questions hits a hard one, it reruns its own layers a few more times instead of writing out reasoning text.

### Problem

First, what's a decision model? You hand it some material and a question with a fixed set of options, the way the closed model Jev and its open reimplementation Kev work, and instead of generating text it runs one forward pass and returns a probability for each option, in tens of milliseconds.

The paper calls this a System One, the fast, intuitive mode from dual-process theory. The catch is that its depth is fixed at training time, so an easy question and a hard one get exactly the same compute, and a hard question can't get more thought.

The authors start with a comparison. On the two hundred thirty-one public JevBench items, the four-billion-parameter Kev-4B gets twenty-eight more right than the point-eight-billion Kev-0.8B, and every one of those comes from the standard and hard tiers, because both models already get every easy item right.

More to the point, the two models disagree on only forty-two items. The small model's own confidence finds them: hand its least confident half to the big model, and you nearly match running the big model on everything. So extra compute only pays on a few questions, and a cheap first read can tell you which.

### Prior work

So do existing ways of adding compute work? The first is a cascade, where the small model passes questions it's unsure about to a larger one. But Kev-0.8B already takes seventy-nine milliseconds at the median against one hundred eight for Kev-4B, so every escalated question is read again from scratch, takes longer than the big model alone, and you have to host a second model.

The second is to write out reasoning before answering. Citing published JevBench results for two open servers, the paper shows thinking lifts hard-tier accuracy by nine to thirteen points, but makes the median hard item three to four times slower, and easy questions pay that price too.

The third is a loop, rerunning the same block of layers to add depth without adding parameters. The authors looped eight middle layers of Qwen3.5-0.8B and trained it with Kev's recipe, and accuracy moved by less than half a point. The gates that control the loop stayed near zero, so the loop effectively never opened.

### Key idea

Their idea: instead of bolting a loop on afterwards, build on a language model that was pretrained as a loop, like Ouro or Nanbeige four point two, which learned to use its loop from the start.

On that backbone, the same readout head reads the options after every loop step, and all steps are trained together. One checkpoint can then answer at any depth, running more steps on hard questions and stopping after one on easy ones, which the paper calls a System One point Five model.

### Method

Here's the architecture. A request packs some material followed by several questions, each with its options, into one sequence. The same stack of decoder layers runs R times, and in this figure R is four.

After each step, a shared pointer head reads the hidden states at each option and at a decide token, and turns them into option probabilities. Training averages the loss over all steps, so every depth is trained to be a working decision model.

The payoff is that stopping at step k only computes the first k steps, roughly k over R of the full cost. And another step simply continues from the previous hidden states, so the input is never read again.

Then the key question: when to stop. After each step, a small stopper decides which questions still need another one. It's one logistic regression per depth, fed a few features from this step's readout and the previous one, like the top probability, the margin between the two leading options, and whether the answer changed.

It doesn't predict whether the answer is correct; it predicts whether the current answer already agrees with the deepest one. The threshold is the cheapest one under which at least ninety-eight percent of final answers still agree with the deepest answers.

The nice property is that the target is the model's own deepest answer, so neither the stopper nor its threshold needs labels. In deployment, you can fit it on your own unlabelled traffic.

Why not the usual confidence threshold? The authors find that shallow readouts are overconfident on new data. The questions LEV-2.6B stops at depth one are ninety-four point eight percent correct on the calibration set, but only seventy-eight point nine percent on the new-source Kev-Transfer panel, at almost the same confidence.

And while confidence separates right from wrong answers with an AUROC of point six seven to point eight three, at spotting the wrong answers that more steps would fix it only manages point four six to point six one, where point five is a coin flip.

### Results

The setup. Three looped backbones: Ouro at one point four and two point six billion parameters, each running four steps, and Nanbeige four point two at three billion, running two. The controls are three ordinary non-looped Qwen models at one point seven, two and four billion parameters, trained on exactly the same data with the same recipe.

Every model gets one epoch of LoRA on the same thirty-one thousand three hundred twenty-nine records, and no label comes from Jev, so nothing is distilled from the model it's compared with. For evaluation, LevBench is a suite the authors built and froze before any model saw it, Kev-Breadth holds fourteen datasets never used in training, and they also report the two hundred thirty-one public JevBench items.

Start with Table one. Same weights, more steps, higher accuracy. The paper reports that LEV-2.6B goes from sixty-three point two percent on JevBench at depth one to seventy-six point two at depth four, a thirteen-point gain.

Returns diminish fast. The first extra step does most of the work, lifting the held-out average of LEV-2.6B from fifty-one point three to sixty-two percent, against sixty-five point seven at depth four, and the fourth step of the Ouro models adds at most one point.

And depth helps more the further you get from the training data. On the training sources, going from one step to four adds about seven points, while the four panels further away gain nine to nineteen and a half points.

The gains come from hard questions. On JevBench's one hundred eleven hard items, LEV-2.6B gains a net nineteen from depth one to depth four, with twenty-nine flipping to correct and ten flipping to wrong, while the easy tier is nearly solved at every depth.

The paper's first figure shows the stopper spending one point two steps on easy items, two point four on standard and three on hard, without ever seeing how hard they are.

Depth can also break answers. On LevBench, the first extra step fixes twenty point four percent of questions and breaks eight point six percent. And for twelve point six percent, some shallower step was right but the deepest is wrong, which a stopper aimed at the deepest answer can't recover.

On-demand depth is in Table two. On the two held-out panels, the stopper uses seventy-six to eighty-five percent of the full compute and stays within a point of the deepest readout.

The authors also kept a locked test set, read once after the recipe and the stopper were fixed. It agrees: on-demand depth loses at most one point against the deepest readout.

Fitting the stopper doesn't take much unlabelled data either: with five hundred questions per fold, LEV-2.6B lands within point two of a full fit.

On training, fitting all depths jointly matches models trained for a single depth at steps two and four, and trails by at most one point three at step one. Train only the last step, on the other hand, and the shallow readouts become unusable.

### Comparison

Against ordinary models at the same latency: LEV-3B at one step beats Qwen3-1.7B by four points on the held-out average, at twenty-three milliseconds versus twenty-two. LEV-2.6B at two steps beats Qwen3.5-2B by three point one points, at one hundred one versus one hundred five milliseconds. The smallest, LEV-1.4B, does not beat Qwen3-1.7B, though.

Against cascades, at thirty-nine to forty-six milliseconds, on-demand LEV-1.4B and LEV-3B are seven to eight point eight points more accurate than the only cascade that reaches that latency, and five point four to seven point five above the best dense option. But at around one hundred fifty milliseconds, LEV-2.6B is no better than Kev-4B on its own.

Be clear about what this means. The authors say it themselves: the advantage is in latency, not compute. Part of it is simply that no dense option answers between twenty-two and seventy-nine milliseconds, and counted in floating-point operations, every LEV model is level with or below the dense options.

Likewise, LEV-3B ties Qwen3.5-4B on JevBench and LEV-2.6B ties Kev-4B, with fewer parameters but not fewer operations, because every loop step runs the whole backbone.

Against the closed Jev, the gap is still large. Jev version one point one three scores eighty-six point six percent on JevBench, while the best LEV scores seventy-six point two. Calibration on hard items is also worse than Kev and Jev, which the authors attribute to their single-stage recipe rather than to the loop.

My read is that for teams running high-volume classification or moderation, one checkpoint that covers several speed tiers and trades latency for accuracy per question is genuinely practical, but for now the clearest advantage is under fifty milliseconds.

### Limitations

The authors list a few limitations. First, they only test looped backbones from one point four to three billion parameters, because those are the public sizes, so how it behaves on larger models is unknown.

Second, JevBench results rest on its two hundred thirty-one public items, and more decision benchmarks would broaden the picture.

Third, the savings are counted in loop steps, and turning them into wall-clock time needs a serving path that batches requests at different depths. Latency was also measured one request at a time, with no fused kernels or similar optimizations for any system.

### Future work

The authors point to two directions. One is a stopping target that also rescues the questions a shallower step gets right and the deepest gets wrong, which could push accuracy past the deepest readout. As an ideal ceiling, an oracle that uses the correct answers to stop each question at its earliest right step reaches eighty-five point nine percent on the tuning panels at point three four of the full cost, against seventy-seven point four for the deepest readout.

The other is larger looped backbones, to see whether the gains on hard questions grow with scale. The paper links code on GitHub and models on Hugging Face.

My read is that the contribution is showing that, for multiple-choice decision models, a looped backbone plus a label-free stopper can allocate compute per question without generating a single token.

My read is also that on JevBench the absolute accuracy is still about ten points behind the closed Jev, and counted in operations it's no cheaper than a dense model. Whether it lands depends on looped backbones getting bigger, and on serving systems turning saved steps into saved time.
