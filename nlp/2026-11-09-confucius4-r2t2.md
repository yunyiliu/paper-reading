## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇网易有道的论文，模型叫 Confucius4-R2T2，R2T2 是 Real Real-Time Transcription 的缩写，意思是真正的实时转写。

它要解决的问题是，语音实时转文字的时候，已经吐出来的字，能不能保证以后再也不改。

### 问题

先说背景。流式语音识别，就是一边听一边出字，不等整句话说完。

现在它的输出越来越多是给机器用的，比如同声传译系统，或者语音 Agent，它们拿到一个词就开始干活，翻译、决定什么时候回复、调用哪个工具。

问题在于，流式识别会改字。前面先出了一个词，后面听到更多声音，又把它改掉了，这种现象叫 flicker，也就是闪烁。对人来说只是字幕跳了一下，对机器来说，基于这个词做的工作就白做了，在 Agent 里，甚至可能已经执行了一个错误的动作。

所以作者把输出稳定，也就是已经发出的文字永远不再修改，当成和准确率、延迟同样重要的要求。他们给了一个严格的定义，叫稳定前缀模型，任何时刻的输出，都必须以之前的输出开头。

### 以前的方法

以前的方法大致两类。一类是在推理时加规则，比如 local agreement，连续两次识别结果里相同的开头部分才确认输出，或者每次都把结果的最后几个词扣住不放。

另一类是在训练上下功夫，比如用随机截断的样本来训练，让模型少改字。这些办法能降低改字的频率，但都不能保证永远不改。

还有一条路是改模型结构，比如把编码器改成因果的，只看过去的声音。论文指出，这类结构本身并不保证文字不改。论文还引用了另一项研究，把 Whisper 的编码器因果化以后，在块长超过大约 300 毫秒时，效果反而不如不改。

### 核心想法

这篇的核心想法是，不改结构，也不在外面硬加规则，而是教模型自己判断，听到的声音什么时候已经够了，可以把字定下来，不够的时候就先等一等。

那该定下哪些字呢。作者提出一个概念，叫最长稳定前缀，Longest Stable Prefix，简称 LSP。做法是拿同一个模型，先让它听完整句话，得到完整结果。再把音频每 80 毫秒截一段，从头开始越喂越长，记录每一次的输出。

在某个时刻，回头看模型到目前为止的所有输出，挑出那些整句都是完整结果开头一部分的，其中最长的一个，就是这个时刻的 LSP。换句话说，它是模型自己真的说过的一句话，而且这句话和最终结果完全对得上。

这张表把它和两个已有的定义做了对比。和其中按最终结果给部分分的那个定义比，作者的版本更保守，一次输出只要有一个词对不上，整次输出就不算，没有部分分。好处是标签一定是模型真实产生过的输出，不会让模型去学一个它当时其实还没想定的状态。作者在附录里证明，这个标签只会变长、不会缩短，最后等于完整结果，而且在既是完整结果的开头、又是模型真实输出过的标签里，它是最长的。

### 方法

具体怎么做。底座是 Qwen3-ASR-1.7B，由一个 3 亿参数的音频编码器、一个投影层和 Qwen3-1.7B 语言模型组成。R2T2 没有改任何结构，没有加新的层。

为什么选这个底座。作者定义了一个指标，叫 streamability，可流式性，用来衡量一个模型改成流式的潜力。在 1860 条英文语音上，参与比较的几个模型里，Qwen3-ASR-1.7B 的归一化可流式性最高，是 0.493。

训练数据是大约 1500 小时的中英文音频，60 万条，全部是自有数据。作者还加了噪声和混响，并且故意把一部分样本的语言标签弄反，专门针对模型在噪声里乱编、或者该转写却去翻译这些毛病。

标注怎么做，看这张图。句子是 I have two cats。模型每 80 毫秒看一次，I have 很早就稳定了，后面的词先猜成 to，又变成 too，最后才定成 two。稳定的时段记成区间，不稳定的时段，标签就是稳定前缀后面加一个特殊标记 Wait，意思是先等等。

训练时，在随机位置把音频截断，模型只看截断点之前的部分，学着输出对应的稳定前缀，或者稳定前缀加 Wait。Wait 就是词表里一个普通的 token，这样什么时候出字和出什么字，就被分开监督了。

训练分两步。先做监督微调，让模型熟悉这种输出方式。然后用 GRPO 强化学习，奖励由八项组成，权重最大的是字错率，其他几项有的继续给识别打分，有的惩罚重复、中英文混乱、多吐字，以及 Wait 放错位置。两步都冻结音频编码器和投影层，只训练语言模型。

推理时也有讲究。主实验每 160 毫秒更新一次，如果模型第一步就预测 Wait，这次更新直接结束，不用逐字生成。再加上缓存复用，以及只保留最近一段音频和文字，每次更新的计算量不会随着音频变长一直涨。

还有一个旋钮，叫 U，表示最后几个 token 先不放出去，下次更新时再确认。U 等于 0 偏向低延迟，U 等于 1 偏向准确，用的是同一个模型，不用重新训练。

### 实验和效果

实验在 8 个英文测试集和 5 个中文测试集上做，英文看词错率，中文看字错率，都是越低越好。

结果在表 3。同样用 160 毫秒的块，原始 Qwen3-ASR 的总平均错误率是 21.01，R2T2 在 U 等于 1 时是 5.76，降了将近四分之三，而且每个测试集都更好。

同一个底座听完整句话再识别，离线结果是 5.15，R2T2 只差大约 0.6 个百分点。

和其他系统比，在表 3 参与排名的配置里，5.76 是最低的总平均错误率，低于三家商用 API，AssemblyAI 是 8.27，两家匿名商用系统分别是 6.23 和 6.01。不过只看中文，这两家匿名商用系统的字错率更低，作者也指出，它们的文字要更晚才稳定下来。

延迟看这张图。作者用的指标叫回溯出字延迟，大意是一个词说完以后，过多久它的文字才彻底固定。英文上，R2T2 偏准确模式平均大约 0.76 秒，偏低延迟模式大约 0.42 秒。作为对照，让底座用 2 秒的块、同样只放出不再修改的文字，平均延迟大约 2 秒。

作者还报告，用 80 毫秒的块加 U 等于 0，中英文平均延迟都能压到半秒以下，代价是准确率差一些。

最后是跨语言。R2T2 只用中英文训练，但在另外 10 种语言、18 个测试集上，160 毫秒、U 等于 1 的设置下，错误率全部低于原始底座同样设置的结果。

### 和以前方法的对比

和以前的方法比，最大的不同是，稳定是模型学出来的，不是靠外面的规则硬卡。论文指出，local agreement 这类规则背后的识别模型其实还在不停改主意，只是改的部分没放出来。R2T2 是自己学会了，不确定就等。

和改结构的路线比，它保留了原来的双向编码器，在每个音频窗口里仍然能前后都看，所以准确率能贴近离线水平。代价是每次更新都要重新处理当前窗口，作者用缓存和 Wait 提前结束来压这部分成本。

我的判断是，这篇最有价值的地方，是把目标说清楚了。不是尽量少改字，而是绝不改字，然后在这个约束下再比准确率和延迟。对做同传、做语音 Agent 的团队来说，这个保证可能比平均分再低零点几更实用。

我的判断是，也要看到它在新语言上还没追上底座。表 8 里，10 种新语言平均下来，R2T2 偏准确模式是 8.40，底座用 2 秒块是 7.23，离线是 5.99。

### 局限

论文里能看到的局限，主要有三条。

第一，延迟是在音频时间轴上算的，不包括真实运行时的计算、排队和传输，作者明确说，没有用实际运行时间去验证。闪烁的频率，论文也没有直接测量。

第二，训练数据是自有数据，没有公开。评测集虽然没有用于训练，但作者提醒，底座模型在预训练时可能见过其中一部分。

第三，LSP 标签偏保守，一个词对不上整次输出就不算，会让模型倾向于晚一点出字。奖励里八个部分各自起多大作用，作者说还需要对照的消融实验，论文里没有给出。

### 未来方向

未来方向，作者提了两个开放问题。一是可流式性这个指标，能不能推广到同声传译这类更一般的流式任务。二是现在模型是自己给自己标注，换一个更强的老师模型，能不能训出更好的流式模型。

另外，真实部署时的性能和更广的泛化能力，作者说是下一步的重点。模型权重和代码已经开源。

我的判断是，语音 Agent 越来越多，绝不改字会从加分项变成基本要求。这篇给了一个不改结构、不需要外部老师的做法，做语音产品的团队值得拿来试一试。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from NetEase Youdao, and the model is called Confucius4-R2T2. R2T2 stands for Real Real-Time Transcription.

The question it tackles: when speech is turned into text live, can you guarantee that words already on the screen will never change?

### Problem

Some background first. Streaming speech recognition means producing text while someone is still talking, without waiting for the end of the sentence.

More and more, that text is read by machines rather than people. A live translation system, or a voice agent, starts working the moment a word arrives. It translates, decides when to reply, or picks a tool to call.

The catch is that streaming recognizers revise. A word comes out early, more audio arrives, and the word gets replaced. This is called flicker. For a human reader, a caption jumps. For a machine, the work built on that word is wasted, and an agent may already have taken the wrong action.

So the authors treat stability, meaning that released text is never modified, as a requirement on the same level as accuracy and latency. They define a stable-prefix model strictly: at any moment, the output must begin with everything it output before.

### Prior work

Earlier approaches fall into two groups. One adds rules at inference time. Local agreement, for example, only commits the part that two consecutive hypotheses share, and hold-n simply withholds the last few tokens each time.

The other group works on training, for instance by training on randomly truncated examples so the model revises less. These methods reduce revisions, but none of them guarantees that earlier tokens are never changed.

A third route changes the architecture, for example by making the encoder causal so it only looks at past audio. The paper points out that this alone doesn't guarantee final text. It also cites work reporting that causalizing Whisper's encoder does worse than leaving it intact once chunks are longer than about three hundred milliseconds.

### Key idea

The key idea here is to change neither the architecture nor bolt on an outside rule. Instead, the model learns to judge when the audio it has heard is enough to commit a word, and to wait when it isn't.

So which words should be committed? The authors introduce the Longest Stable Prefix, or LSP. Take one model and let it hear the whole utterance to get its full output. Then feed it the audio again, eighty milliseconds longer each time, and record every output along the way.

At any moment, look back over all the outputs so far. Keep only those that are, in their entirety, the beginning of the full output. The longest of those is the LSP at that moment. In other words, it's something the model actually said, and it fully agrees with where the model ends up.

This table compares it with two existing definitions. Compared with the final-anchored one, which gives partial credit, the authors' version is stricter: if a single word of an output disagrees, that whole output gets no credit at all. The upside is that the label is always an output the model really produced, so the model is never trained toward a state it hadn't actually settled. In the appendix the authors prove that this label only grows, ends at the full output, and is the longest label that is both a beginning of the full output and a real earlier output of the model.

### Method

Now the details. The base is Qwen3-ASR-1.7B, made of a three-hundred-million-parameter audio encoder, a projector, and the Qwen3-1.7B language model. R2T2 changes nothing in the architecture and adds no layers.

Why this base? The authors define a measure they call streamability, the potential of a model to be turned into a streaming one. On one thousand eight hundred sixty English utterances, among the models compared, Qwen3-ASR-1.7B had the highest normalized streamability, zero point four nine three.

Training uses about fifteen hundred hours of Chinese and English audio, six hundred thousand utterances, all proprietary. The authors add noise and reverberation, and flip the language tag on some samples, targeting failure modes like hallucinating on noise or translating instead of transcribing.

Here is how labeling works, in this figure. The sentence is "I have two cats." The model looks every eighty milliseconds. "I have" settles early, but the next word is first guessed as "to", then "too", and only later becomes "two". Stable stretches are recorded as intervals. In the unstable stretches, the label is the stable prefix followed by a special Wait token, meaning hold on.

During training, the audio is cut at a random point, the model sees only the first part, and it learns to output the matching stable prefix, or the stable prefix plus Wait. Wait is just an ordinary token in the vocabulary, so when to emit and what to emit are supervised separately.

Training has two stages. Supervised fine-tuning gets the model used to this output format. Then reinforcement learning with GRPO uses a reward with eight parts. Character error rate carries the most weight. The others either score recognition further or penalize repetition, mixed-up languages, overly long output, and a misplaced Wait. In both stages the audio encoder and projector are frozen, and only the language model is trained.

Inference has its own tricks. In the main experiments the model updates every one hundred sixty milliseconds. If its first prediction is Wait, the update ends right there with no token-by-token decoding. Add cache reuse and keeping only the most recent stretch of audio and text, and the cost per update stops growing as the stream gets longer.

There is also a knob called U, the number of final tokens held back until the next update confirms them. U equals zero favors low latency, U equals one favors accuracy, and it's the same model either way, with no retraining.

### Results

The experiments use eight English and five Chinese test sets, with word error rate for English and character error rate for Chinese. Lower is better.

The results are in Table three. At the same one-hundred-sixty-millisecond chunks, the original Qwen3-ASR has a total average error of twenty-one point zero one. R2T2 with U equals one gets five point seven six, nearly three quarters lower, and better on every test set.

When the same base model hears the whole utterance before transcribing, it scores five point one five offline. R2T2 is only about zero point six points behind.

Against other systems, five point seven six is the lowest total average among the ranked configurations in Table three, below all three commercial APIs: AssemblyAI at eight point two seven, and two unnamed commercial systems at six point two three and six point zero one. On Chinese alone, though, those two unnamed systems have lower character error rates, and the authors note that their text settles later.

For latency, look at this figure. The authors measure what they call retrospective emission lag, roughly how long after a word ends its text becomes permanently fixed. In English, R2T2 averages about zero point seven six seconds in its accuracy mode and about zero point four two seconds in its latency mode. For comparison, the base model with two-second chunks, releasing only text that won't change, averages about two seconds.

The authors also report that with eighty-millisecond chunks and U equals zero, mean lag stays below half a second in both languages, at some cost in accuracy.

Finally, other languages. R2T2 was trained only on Chinese and English, yet on eighteen test sets across ten more languages, at one hundred sixty milliseconds with U equals one, its error is lower than the base model's at the same setting on every one.

### Comparison

Compared with earlier work, the big difference is that stability is learned, not enforced by an outside rule. The paper notes that behind rules like local agreement, the recognizer keeps changing its mind, and the changes are just hidden. R2T2 itself learns to wait when it's unsure.

Compared with the architecture route, it keeps the original bidirectional encoder, which still looks both ways within each audio window, so accuracy stays close to offline. The price is reprocessing the current window at every update, which the authors contain with caching and early stopping on Wait.

My read is that the most valuable thing here is a clear target. Not fewer revisions, but no revisions, and then compete on accuracy and latency under that constraint. For teams building live translation or voice agents, that guarantee may matter more than a few tenths of a point of error.

My read is that it also hasn't caught up with its base on new languages. In Table eight, averaged over the ten new languages, R2T2's accuracy mode scores eight point four zero, while the base gets seven point two three with two-second chunks and five point nine nine offline.

### Limitations

The paper shows three main limitations.

First, lag is measured on the audio timeline. It leaves out computation, queueing and transport, and the authors say plainly that runtime measurements did not verify it. Flicker frequency itself isn't measured either.

Second, the training data is proprietary and not released. The test sets weren't used for training, but the authors caution that the base model may have seen some of them during pre-training.

Third, the LSP label is conservative. One mismatched word voids a whole output, which biases the model toward emitting later. And how much each of the eight reward parts contributes would need controlled ablations, which the paper doesn't provide.

### Future work

On future work, the authors raise two open questions. One is whether streamability can serve as a gauge for streaming tasks in general, such as simultaneous translation. The other is whether a stronger teacher could produce a better streaming model, since today the model labels its own data.

They also name deployment performance and broader generalization as next priorities. The model weights and code are open source.

My read is that as voice agents spread, never revising text will go from a nice-to-have to a baseline requirement. This paper offers a way to get there without changing the architecture or needing an outside teacher, and teams building voice products should give it a try.
