## 中文讲解

### 开场

这里是每日前沿文章。今天讲一篇蚂蚁国际和新加坡国立大学的论文，叫 PAR，全称是 Progress-Aligned Recurrence，可以理解成，和进度对齐的循环。

它研究的问题是，让模型在推理时多转几圈，能不能真的解出比训练时长得多的题。论文报告，一个 0.5B 参数的 Qwen 模型，训练时最多只见过 32 步的题，测试到 512 步，也就是 16 倍长度，平均全对率还有 95.5%。

### 问题

先说背景。这篇讲的是 length generalization，长度泛化，就是在短题上训练，能不能做对同一类、但更长的题。这里的长度，指题目里操作的步数。

作者用的测试叫状态追踪。有五个盒子，每个盒子里放一样不同的东西，题目给出一串操作，每一步交换两个盒子，或者原样不动，最后问某个盒子里是什么。规则从头到尾不变，难点只在于步数越来越多，每一步都得做对。

有一类模型很适合这种事，叫 looped language model，循环语言模型。它把同一组 Transformer 层反复用好几遍，每多转一圈，就多一份计算，而且不需要把中间步骤写成文字。

问题是，多转几圈，并不会自动解出更长的题。论文的实验里，训练最多 16 步，把核心层加深到 4 倍的模型，和推理时多跑几圈的循环模型，都外推不到 2 倍长度。

### 以前的方法

以前的工作给过两条线索。一条是让循环的圈数跟着题目长度走，另一条是对中间状态做监督，教预训练模型一步一步地查表。

但关键问题是，每一圈到底该输出什么。如果每一圈都要求给出最终答案，前几圈根本还没算到那一步，就会被逼着走捷径。已有研究在图可达性任务上就发现，这样做会鼓励模型过早给答案，外推能力变弱。

另一个容易被忽视的问题是，同一组层反复用几百遍，传给下一圈的隐藏状态，数值尺度会慢慢变。作者发现，只给中间目标、不控制这个尺度，题目稍微长一点，准确率就掉了。

### 核心想法

PAR 的核心想法，一句话就能说清，第 k 圈只负责做完前 k 步。

也就是说，第一圈的输出，要等于做完第一次交换后的状态，第二圈等于做完两次交换后的状态，以此类推。作者把这叫做深度和长度的对应关系，题越长，需要的圈数就越多。

第二个想法，是让每一圈的步子保持稳定。每转完一圈，就把隐藏状态的大小拉回到第一圈时的水平，作者叫它 norm anchoring，范数锚定。目标规定了每一圈该走多远，锚定保证同一组层反复用的时候，状态的大小不跑偏。

### 方法

具体结构是这样的。作者把 Qwen2.5-0.5B 的 24 层切成三段，前 8 层只跑一次，用来读题，中间 8 层共享权重、反复循环，后 8 层负责从任意一圈读出答案。

有个细节很巧。连接进核心层的那个小矩阵，初始化的方式让模型只转一圈时，和原来的预训练模型完全一样，所以这是在已有模型上改出来的，不是从头训练。

训练目标就是这个式子，第 k 圈的输出，和做完前 k 步的真实状态算交叉熵。如果每一圈都拿最终答案来监督，对应关系就被破坏了，前面的圈会被要求给出它还算不出来的答案。

范数锚定的做法很直接，每一圈结束后，把每个 token 的状态按比例缩放，让它的均方根大小和第一圈一样。另外，针对很长的题目，还可以在一个简短的微调阶段，把位置编号随机放大，这叫 positional stretch，位置拉伸。

第三个问题是，测试时模型不知道题有多长，该转几圈停下来。PAR 让负责读答案的那几层，同时回答一个问题，算完了没有，输出 Yes 或 No，不增加任何参数。

停止规则是，找到第一个同时满足两个条件的圈，它的 Yes 概率超过一半，而且从这一圈开始的 8 圈里，至少有 6 圈也超过一半，然后采用这一圈的答案。

最后一个问题是，真实数据里通常没有中间步骤的标签。作者的办法叫前缀自蒸馏。一道题的前 k 步，本身就是一道更短的题，而短题，正是模型最先学会的。

所以让一个老师模型去读截断到前 k 步的题，把它的答案当作第 k 圈的目标，老师是学生权重的滑动平均，或者就是学生自己。这样只用最终答案，就能把每一圈的分工学出来。

### 实验和效果

实验设置。主要任务就是五个盒子换位置，还有一个每步轮换三个盒子的版本。在 swap-S5 这个设定里，训练最多 32 步，每个长度测 1024 条序列，五个盒子全部答对才算对。

基线用的是同一个 0.5B 底座和同样的数据，包括把每一步状态写出来的显式思维链，只跑一圈的固定深度模型，48 层的加深模型，和随机圈数的循环模型。另一个对手 RLT，用的是它原论文报告的数字。

结果在表 1。swap-S5 上，到 512 步，也就是 16 倍训练长度，PAR 六个随机种子的平均全对率是 95.5%，RLT 报告的是 55.7%，显式思维链只有 0.8%。而且 PAR 的每一个种子，都超过了 RLT 的平均值。

再看外推倍数。论文的定义是，准确率掉到随机和满分中间那条线时的长度，除以训练长度。测试最长到 256 步，所以 16 倍是能测到的上限。PAR 在 S5 和 A5 上所有种子都到了 16 倍，固定深度、随机圈数、48 层加深的模型，都不到 2 倍。

作者的结论是，光加圈数，或者光加参数，都替代不了这种对应关系。这张图很直观，只有 PAR 那条线一直贴着顶，其他几种训练方法，到 64 步时都已经掉到随机水平附近，显式思维链甚至更低。

换更大的底座呢。Qwen2.5 的 1.5B、3B、7B，到 16 倍长度，PAR 最好的种子都是 100%，固定深度的模型最高只有 26.5%。但 PAR 的平均分只有 57.8 到 80.7，不同种子之间差得很大。

自动停止的效果在表 4。不告诉模型题目多长，让它自己判断什么时候停，16 倍长度时全对率是 92.9%，和直接给它正确圈数的 93.2% 几乎一样。

为什么停的时机这么重要，看这张图。在正确的那一圈，答案准确率最高，再多转几圈，准确率很快掉下来，而算完了这个信号，正好在那一圈亮起来，之后一直保持。

没有中间标签的前缀自蒸馏，在 S5 上 16 倍长度做到 85%，是只用最终答案的几种方法里，唯一守住高准确率的。在 swap-S5 上，再加上位置拉伸微调，16 倍长度最高到 99.7%。

### 和以前方法的对比

关键的消融在表 7。每次只改一个条件，而且在同一个底座上，把前人的几种监督方法也复现了一遍。

每一圈都监督最终答案，外推倍数停在 2 倍左右。只在最后一圈监督最终答案，是 1.48 倍。只在最后一圈监督、再加上范数锚定，有一个种子到了 8 倍，但三个种子在 256 步的平均准确率只有 26.8%。

反过来，有中间目标、但没有范数锚定，大约 1.8 倍就停了。两个条件都有，PAR 三个种子全部到 16 倍，256 步的准确率是 100、100 和 99。

还有一个对照很说明问题。只给固定深度和随机圈数的模型加位置拉伸，到 48 步就是随机水平。但给一个已经学会对应关系、每圈走两步的 PAR 变体加上它，单次运行在 512 步，也就是 32 倍训练长度，还有 99% 的准确率。作者的说法是，稳定性只能让已有的步骤可以反复使用，并不能凭空造出这个步骤。

我的判断是，这篇真正的贡献，是给循环里的每一圈，定义了一份具体的工作。很多人把循环次数当作一个可以往上拧的旋钮，这篇的实验说明，不规定每圈做什么，多转几圈很可能是白转。

我的判断是，也要看到对比上的一个弱点。和 RLT 的比较，用的是 RLT 论文报告的数字，不是同一套设置重新跑的，而且论文把 RLT 归在从头训练的循环模型里，PAR 是在预训练模型上改的，两边条件并不完全一样。

### 局限

作者自己写了几条局限。第一，所有任务都是算法类或者模板化的状态追踪，中间状态定义得很清楚，能不能用到开放式的自然语言推理，还没有做。

第二，没有顺序结构的任务不适用。比如图可达性，它是同时向外扩展一圈邻居，没有一个标准的第 k 步状态，所有方法都在 3.5 倍以下，PAR 自己是 2.6 倍。

第三，模型变大以后很不稳定。在 Qwen2.5 的 1.5B 到 7B 上，16 倍长度时，种子之间的标准差有 33 到 43 个百分点，准确率也不是随模型变大一路上升。而 swap-S5、自动停止和前缀自蒸馏，都只在 0.5B 上测过。

### 未来方向

自然语言方面，论文做了一个初步测试，用 BIG-Bench-Hard 风格、用自然语言描述交换物体的题，训练最多 16 次交换，带位置拉伸的 PAR 变体，在 32 次时是 94% 到 96%，64 次时是 79%，随机猜是 17%。

作者给的方向是，把这种对应关系推广到开放式的自然语言推理，弄清楚大模型上种子差异大的原因，让循环加深能稳定地随规模变好。他们还提到一点，因为每一圈都对应一个中间答案，这种不写出文字的推理，反而可以一圈一圈解码出来检查。

我的判断是，这对做推理的人有一个直接的启发，与其只问模型要多少算力，不如先问每一份算力该完成什么。但它离真实应用还有距离，核心结果都在合成任务上，最强的数字来自 0.5B 的小模型，换到开放问题上会怎样，还要看后续工作。

## English walkthrough

### Intro

This is Daily Frontier Papers. Today's paper comes from Ant International and the National University of Singapore. It's called PAR, short for Progress-Aligned Recurrence.

The question is whether letting a model loop more at inference time actually lets it solve problems much longer than anything it trained on. The headline number: a half-billion-parameter Qwen model, trained on at most thirty-two steps, still gets ninety-five point five percent of sequences fully right at five hundred and twelve steps. That's sixteen times the training length.

### Problem

Some background. The topic is length generalization: if you train on short problems, can you solve longer instances of the same task? Here, length means the number of operations in the problem.

The test bed is state tracking. Picture five boxes, each holding a different item. The prompt lists a sequence of moves, each one swapping two boxes or leaving them as they are, and then asks what's in a given box. The rules never change. The only difficulty is that there are more and more steps, and every one has to be right.

There's a family of models that seems built for this: looped language models. They run the same block of transformer layers over and over, so each extra loop is extra computation, without writing the intermediate steps out as text.

The trouble is that more loops don't automatically buy you longer problems. In the paper's experiments, trained on up to sixteen steps, a model with four times the core depth, and a looped model run for more iterations, both extrapolate less than two times.

### Prior work

Earlier work offers two leads. One ties the number of loops to the length of the problem. The other supervises intermediate states, teaching a pretrained model to do lookups one step at a time.

But the central question is what each loop should be asked to predict. If every loop has to produce the final answer, the early loops haven't done enough work yet, so they're pushed toward shortcuts. Prior work on graph reachability found exactly that: it encourages early predictions and weakens extrapolation.

The other, easily overlooked issue is scale. When the same layers are applied hundreds of times, the hidden state handed from loop to loop can change in magnitude. The authors found that intermediate targets without any control over that scale lose accuracy once problems get a bit longer.

### Key idea

PAR's core idea fits in one sentence: loop k is responsible for the first k steps, and nothing more.

So the first loop's output should be the state after the first swap, the second loop's the state after two swaps, and so on. The authors call this a depth–length correspondence. Longer problems need more loops.

The second idea keeps each step stable. After every loop, the hidden state is rescaled back to the size it had after the first loop. They call it norm anchoring. The targets say how far each loop should go, and the anchoring keeps the state's magnitude fixed as the shared block gets reused.

### Method

Here's the architecture. The authors split the twenty-four layers of Qwen2.5 half-billion into three parts. The first eight run once to read the problem, the middle eight share weights and loop, and the last eight read out an answer from any loop.

One neat detail. The small matrix feeding into the looped core is initialized so that, with a single loop, the model computes exactly what the original pretrained model did. So this is a retrofit of an existing model, not training from scratch.

The training objective is this equation: the output of loop k is trained with cross-entropy against the true state after the first k steps. Supervise every loop with the final answer instead, and the correspondence breaks, because early loops are asked for an answer they can't compute yet.

Norm anchoring itself is simple. After each loop, every token's state is rescaled so its root-mean-square size matches the first loop. For very long prompts there's also an optional short fine-tuning stage that randomly stretches the position indices, called positional stretch.

The third problem: at test time the model doesn't know how long the problem is, so when should it stop looping? PAR has the same layers that read out the answer also answer a yes-or-no question, am I done, with no extra parameters.

The stopping rule finds the first loop that meets two conditions: the probability of yes is above one half, and at least six of the eight loops starting there agree. It returns that loop's answer.

The last problem is that real data rarely comes with labels for intermediate steps. The authors' fix is prefix self-distillation. The first k steps of a problem are themselves a shorter problem, and shorter problems are exactly what a model learns first.

So a teacher model reads the problem cut off after k steps, and its answer becomes the target for loop k. The teacher is either a moving average of the student's weights or the live student itself. That way the per-loop job can be learned from final answers alone.

### Results

The setup. The main task is the five-box swapping problem, plus a variant where each move rotates three boxes. In the swap S-five setting, training uses at most thirty-two moves, each length is tested on one thousand and twenty-four sequences, and a sequence only counts if all five boxes are right.

The baselines share the same half-billion backbone and data: explicit chain of thought that writes out the state at every step, a fixed-depth model that runs once, a deepened forty-eight-layer model, and a looped model with a random number of loops. One more competitor, RLT, is compared using the numbers reported in its own paper.

The results are in Table 1. On swap S-five at five hundred and twelve moves, sixteen times the training length, PAR averages ninety-five point five percent across six random seeds. RLT reported fifty-five point seven, and explicit chain of thought gets zero point eight. Every PAR seed beats RLT's mean.

Next, the extrapolation factor. The paper defines it as the length where accuracy drops below the midpoint between chance and perfect, divided by the training length. Tests go up to two hundred and fifty-six steps, so sixteen times is the most it can measure. PAR hits sixteen on S-five and A-five for every seed. The fixed-depth, random-loop and forty-eight-layer models all stay under two.

The authors' conclusion is that neither more loops nor more parameters substitute for the correspondence. This plot makes it obvious: only the PAR line stays pinned at the top, and every other training recipe is down near chance by sixty-four steps, with explicit chain of thought even lower.

What about bigger backbones? On Qwen2.5 at one point five, three and seven billion parameters, the best PAR seed hits one hundred percent at sixteen times the length, while fixed-depth models top out at twenty-six point five. But PAR's mean is only fifty-seven point eight to eighty point seven, because seeds vary a lot.

Learned stopping is in Table 4. Without being told the problem length, the model decides when to stop and gets ninety-two point nine percent at sixteen times, almost identical to the ninety-three point two it gets when handed the correct loop count.

Why timing matters so much is clear from this figure. Accuracy peaks exactly at the right loop and drops fast a few loops later, and the done signal switches on right at that loop and stays on.

Prefix self-distillation, with no intermediate labels, reaches eighty-five percent at sixteen times on S-five, the only final-answer-only method that holds up. On swap S-five, with an added positional-stretch fine-tune, it gets as high as ninety-nine point seven percent at sixteen times.

### Comparison

The key ablation is Table 7. It changes one condition at a time, and it reimplements several earlier supervision recipes on the same backbone.

Supervising the final answer at every loop stalls at about two times. Supervising it only at the last loop gives one point four eight. Add norm anchoring to that, and one seed reaches eight times, but the three seeds average only twenty-six point eight percent at two hundred and fifty-six steps.

Flip it around: intermediate targets without norm anchoring stall at about one point eight times. With both conditions, all three PAR seeds reach sixteen times, with accuracies of one hundred, one hundred and ninety-nine at two hundred and fifty-six steps.

Another comparison is telling. Positional stretch alone leaves the fixed-depth and random-loop models at chance by forty-eight steps. But applied to a PAR variant that already has the correspondence and takes two steps per loop, a single run keeps ninety-nine percent accuracy at five hundred and twelve steps, thirty-two times the training length. The authors' takeaway is that stability makes an existing step reusable, but doesn't create one.

My read is that the real contribution is giving every loop a defined job. People tend to treat loop count as a knob you can just turn up. These experiments suggest that if you don't specify what each loop does, extra loops are likely wasted.

My read is that there's also a weakness in the headline comparison. The RLT numbers are taken from its paper, not rerun under the same setup, and the paper groups RLT with looped models trained from scratch, while PAR retrofits a pretrained model. The conditions aren't fully matched.

### Limitations

The authors list several limitations. First, every task is algorithmic or templated state tracking, where intermediate states are well defined. Open-ended natural-language reasoning hasn't been tried.

Second, tasks without a sequential structure fall outside its scope. Graph reachability expands a frontier in parallel, so there's no canonical state for loop k to hold. Every method stays under three point five times there, and PAR gets two point six.

Third, it gets unstable at scale. On Qwen2.5 from one point five to seven billion, seed-to-seed standard deviations at sixteen times are thirty-three to forty-three points, and accuracy doesn't rise steadily with model size. And swap S-five, learned stopping and prefix self-distillation were only tested on the half-billion model.

### Future work

On natural language, there's a first test: a BIG-Bench-Hard-style task with object swaps described in plain language. Trained on up to sixteen swaps, a PAR variant with positional stretch scores ninety-four to ninety-six percent at thirty-two swaps and seventy-nine at sixty-four, against chance at seventeen.

The authors point toward extending the correspondence to open-ended reasoning, and understanding why seeds vary so much on bigger models, so that recurrent depth scales reliably. They also note that since every loop holds an intermediate answer, this kind of unwritten reasoning can actually be decoded loop by loop and inspected.

My read is that the lesson for anyone working on reasoning is direct: before asking how much compute a model gets, ask what each unit of compute is supposed to accomplish. But this is still far from deployment. The core results are on synthetic tasks, the strongest numbers come from a half-billion model, and open-ended problems are untested.
